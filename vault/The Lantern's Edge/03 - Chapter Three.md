# Chapter Three

The matchbox was damp. It took four strikes to get one, and each failed strike was
a small, loud event in a room that had been quiet for eleven years.

The first head came off the stick and went somewhere across the desk, and she spent
a moment finding it with her fingers, because a loose match head in a lamp room is
a thing you find later, in the dark, with the flat of your hand. The second one
struck and guttered out. The third broke at the neck and left a splinter in her
thumb that she could feel and not see.

The fourth caught. She held it up.

On the back of the box, in pencil, in the same untidy hand as the notebook: *Four
is normal. Five means the sea is in the walls.*

She turned the box over twice, as though the other side might give her a second
opinion. It was a joke about damp, she decided, and a good one, and the kind of
thing her grandmother would write on a matchbox and think about afterwards while
somebody else was talking. Carrow had a hundred of them. *Don't eat the fish
before the tide turns. Don't stand on the third bollard. Four is normal.*

She opened the notebook at the very front, where the inside cover had a page pasted
over a page and the top one was not a list.

> *In the lit hour and the last hour,*  
> *on water that keeps no road,*  
> *I hold this light as keepers hold —*  
> *not for the crown, not for the coin,*  
> *not because the dark is cruel,*  
> *but because I am here, and I can see,*  
> *and what I can see, I answer for.*  

Under it, smaller: *Say it before the wick, not after. The lamp can tell the
difference.*

Mira read it twice. Her mouth was dry, and she had been alone in the dark for one
hundred and twelve steps, and she wanted very badly to hear a voice, even her own,
even a bad one.

She said it out loud. She got *answer for* wrong — it came out as *answer to* — and
she would think about that for a long time afterwards.

The match was burning down at the rate matches do when you are using one to think
with, and she was not finished with it. She got the log book open before it reached
her fingers.

It was a good book for the job: a foot high, bound in board, the spine gone soft,
the pages ruled by somebody who had ruled them by hand and not been fussy about
it. There were two hundred and something of them, and she turned them with the
match held well off to the side, because paper and flame are the same argument.

The early pages were a keeper's log and nothing else. *Wind nor'-west, four.
Trimmed and filled. Road visible to the second mile.* Then a date, and the same
three items in a different order, and a note in the margin about a sheep on the
path. Then a page where the hand changed — smaller, more upright, somebody
younger — and the entries went on being the entries.

And then, at a date she did not stop to work out, the log stopped being a log and
became one sentence.

*Trimmed and filled.*

The next week: a date, and *Trimmed and filled.*

The week after that: a date, and *Trimmed and filled.* In her grandmother's hand.
Every week. The match burned down past the middle and she read faster, and it did
not matter, because there was nothing to miss: the sentence was the same sentence
on every page, and the pencil had sat in the same place on each of them, so that
the block of pages showed a line of pale dents down the edge like a book somebody
had been pressing a thumb into for years.

She turned to the back of it to see how far it went, and lost her place.

The last entry had a date on it and her grandmother's hand had gone wrong — not
shaky, exactly, but pressed too hard, the pencil digging, the letters further
apart than the ones above them — and it said *Trimmed and filled.* and underneath
it there was nothing at all. No signature. No note about the road, which had been
the whole point of the road.

The match went out on her fingers. She did not shake it.

*Trimmed and filled*, eleven years ago, on a night when the rest of the west coast
had gone dark, and no light on the page anywhere.

She sat with that for a moment, in the dark, with the box in one hand and the log
open at the last entry. Then she thought, in the flat voice she used on herself
when she had been slow: *she filled it.*

The reservoir was full. The wick was trimmed. The log had promised it and the log
had not lied, and the last person to write in that book had walked down a hundred
and twelve steps afterwards and left the promise standing in the room for eleven
years, and somebody — she was not going to think about who — had kept it.

Then, for spite, she struck the fifth. It caught.

*Four is normal,* she thought, and had the whole of the joke at last, and did not
laugh. *Five means the sea is in the walls.*

She lifted the glass chimney. The wick was trimmed to a point, and the reservoir
was so full that the oil had left a tide-mark on the brass. She had imagined this
part a hundred times on the climb up, and in every version the flame was small and
yellow and needed coaxing.

It took the wick all at once. Not a flame — a *band* of it, gold and level, the
whole length of the wick going bright together, and the air in the lamp room
changed the way air changes when somebody opens a window in another room. It
smelled of hot glass and, faintly, of thunderstorms.

Mira put the chimney back. Her hands were not steady. On the desk, the notebook lay
open at the vow, and the pencil line under *the lamp can tell the difference*
looked, in that light, like advice.

The lens took the light and threw it out over the water in a long flat blade, and
the sea — which had been black, and before that merely dark, and before that
something she had known all her life and never once looked at — became a thing she
could see.

She knew the shape of the lens from the inside of the glass, because she had put
her face against it a hundred times as a child and been shouted at. From out here
it was a mill wheel standing on its edge, a hundred rings of glass with a hole in
the middle, and every ring was a different weight of the same idea: catch the light,
bend it, aim it. The lamp was the size of a head. What came out of the lens was the
size of a street.

The drowned road was there. It ran out from the foot of the cliff in a straight
line for a mile, gold under three feet of water, and where it crossed the old
harbour wall it made a bridge of light out of stones that had been under the sea
since before her mother was born. Mira had listened to Carrow's fishermen argue
about that road for eighteen years. Half of them said it was a story. The other
half said it was a story the wrong way round, and would not explain what they
meant.

She looked at it for a while with her hands on the desk, at the ruts and the wall
and the gateposts, and understood that she was looking at the top of something.

The road was not the reason she stopped breathing.

Page nineteen had said *ships*, and she had assumed, in the way of somebody who
has never seen a thing, that ships meant wrecks: ribs, weed, a mast leaning out of
the water at a sad angle. What the light showed her was a fleet.

They were whole. They were rigged. They were *sailing*, canvas drawing in a wind
that was not blowing, wakes going out behind them in long white stitches. There
were vessels the size of Carrow. There were vessels with three hulls and vessels
with none, sitting on the water like a held breath. None of them carried a light,
and all of them were lit, faintly, from below, as though the sea itself were a
lamp and they were floating on the glass.

Mira counted.

She counted because counting was what she did. Because her grandmother had counted
the stairs and written the number down, and because a number was a thing you could
hold when the rest of it was too large to look at.

She counted in fives, and put a finger down on the desk for each five, and got to
twenty-five that way before she made herself stop and go back to the beginning,
because a count you cannot do twice is not a count. The second time she went across
the water left to right, in bands, the way you count a flock: eight in the near
line, eleven behind them, six hulls beyond that with no masts at all, and one long
thing like a barge that had been a long thing like a barge for four hundred years.

She got to thirty and did not stop. She got to fifty and her mouth was dry. At
fifty-one she lost her place, started again, and made fifty-one a second time,
which was either very good or very bad.

Somewhere in the middle of it she noticed that she was crying, and that she had
been for a while, and that it was not grief exactly. It was the thing that happens
to a person who has been told all her life that a road is a story and then is
shown the road.

One of them was close.

It was a lugger, ninety feet at most, black-hulled, with a bronze bell on the
foredeck, and it was the only ship in the whole bright fleet that looked like
something a person might build. It rode at anchor four hundred yards off the rocks,
and the bell was moving. She could see it move. She could not hear it, and that was
worse than hearing it would have been.

Past it, further out, something that was not a ship at all.

Mira looked at it directly because she had been taught not to look away from
things, and her eyes slid off it like a hand off wet glass. It had no edge. It was
the size of a district. Where it met the sky, the light from her lantern simply
stopped, the way a page stops when the words have been scraped off it, and the sea
around it had gone the flat colour of old pewter.

She looked down at the desk, at her own hands, at anything that would hold still.
The matches. The box was still in her hand. The label had been turned to the door.

She got the log book and counted the entries, now that she could see, because she
needed something to do with her eyes that was not the thing on the water.

Five hundred and seventy. She did the arithmetic twice, with her finger moving down
the page, and got eleven years, less a fortnight, and then she sat back and looked
at the number for a while, because a number was a thing you could hold.

Under the matches, on a leather cord, was a ring.

It was a hoop of glass the colour of a bottle held up to a candle, green-black, and
the rim of it was worked with a pattern like the grain of a leaf. She put it on
because her hands wanted something to do, and it fitted the way a key fits, which
is to say that it went on easily and did not want to come off.

She tried to take it off. It came as far as her knuckle and stopped, and she pulled
once, hard, and then stopped pulling, because the ring had gone warm and she had
the sudden clear knowledge that a person could hurt herself doing this and that the
ring would not be the one who was hurt.

The tower door opened below her. She heard it — a long, slow complaint of wood
that had not been moved in a decade — and then the sound stopped, cut off as
neatly as if a hand had been laid over a mouth.

Then footsteps on the stairs.

They were not careful footsteps. They came up at a run, two at a time, and there
were one hundred and twelve steps and whoever it was did not slow down on any of
them. Mira stood in the middle of the lamp room with a lit lantern behind her and
the whole sea full of impossible ships in front of her, and she could not think of
a single sensible thing to do with her hands.

The man who came through the door was young, and soaked through, and wearing a
coat that had been expensive somewhere else. No hat. He got two steps into the
room, saw the light, and shut his eyes.

"You're a fool," he said, with feeling. "You're a genuine, gold-plated fool."

"Who are you?"

"Put it out."

"No."

He opened his eyes. He looked at her the way a man looks at a bill he cannot
afford. Then he pushed past her to the window, and looked out at the sea, at the
road, at the fleet, and his face did something complicated and tired.

"Teodor Vane," he said. "Teo. I'm the navigator of that." He pointed at the black
lugger with the bell. "And you have just put a light on the edge of the world at
nine miles' range. Everything that can see it," he said, "has seen it."

"I was supposed to," Mira said. "It's written down."

He turned around. "Written down where?"

She showed him the notebook, because she had been alone in a tower with a fleet of
the dead for six minutes and she wanted, very badly, for somebody to tell her she
had not imagined it.

He read the page. Then he read the page again, and something in his face went
careful, which she would come to know as the expression he wore when he was
deciding how much of the truth to spend.

"All right," he said. "Count the ships."

"I did. Fifty-one."

"Fifty-two." He took her by the elbow — not roughly, but as though he had decided
that touching her would keep them both from floating away — and turned her towards
the window, to the part of the sea where the pewter colour started.

"One of them's moving," he said. "And it's coming this way. So I'll ask you again,
and I'd like a better answer than 'it's written down'. Put it out."

Below them, out on the black water, the bell of the *Mercy Bell* rang once, quite
clearly, in air that had no wind in it at all.
