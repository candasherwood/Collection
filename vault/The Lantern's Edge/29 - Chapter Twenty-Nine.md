# Chapter Twenty-Nine

They put the lights in the water at five o'clock, and it took an hour, and it was the quietest hour
of the whole year.

The lip of the Fall is nine miles of shingle that ends without a beach, and the sea goes over it the
way water goes over the edge of a table — not fast, not slow, simply *not there any more* — and the
mist comes up out of the nothing for a mile and comes down again as rain. The horseshoe they had
drawn on the chart had both ends on rock: a headland at the north and a headland at the south, and
between them the whole curve of the lip.

Seven keepers. Seven lights in the water, and two anchored on the rock at the ends.

Nobody made a speech about it. Voss put the boats where they had to be with a pointing finger and a
lieutenant who had been with her nine years, and the keepers went where they were told, and the
fleet's crews stayed aboard and lined the rails and did not talk.

Mira's boat went to the middle. Ede's went north of her, and Meridew's north of that, and the
tavern-keeper's south, and Vey's south of his, and Teo's boat at the south end with Isolde in it and
no ring in either of them, and the seventh place in the line was held by a man who had come with the
four keepers and had tried for two nights and could not hold a thing and was, by his own account,
"the only man in this fleet with a ring and no imagination."

"You'll anchor instead," Mira had told him. "There's a rock at the south end and a housing on it and
you'll sit on the rock and keep the wick trimmed and not let it go out. It's the oldest job in the
order and it's the one that matters."

The north rock had the other housing, and the north rock was empty.

---

Toby Larch came up the shingle at half past five with his coat too big for him and his blade across
his hands, and found Mira at the water's edge with the Ninth's Ember in her fist and the band on her
wrist going round.

"Miss," he said. "Miss Calloway."

"Toby."

"The Ordinator said a teal blade is a superstition and I've had it four years and it's lit twice and
both times it was you." He was out of breath; he had run the last half mile along the shingle.
"There's a housing on the north rock and nothing to put in it."

Mira looked at him.

"Toby, that housing takes an Ember."

"No," said Toby. "It takes a light. That's what the housing is. A cup and a lens and a thing in it
that burns." He held the blade out flat on both palms, and the thread in the fuller was grey in the
last of the light. "It isn't an Ember and it isn't a ring and I know what the order would say, and
the order is six hundred miles north with its wick trimmed and its door shut. This is a light. It's
been given. Seventeen people gave it and none of them got anything back and the Ordinator made it
anyway, and if a light is a thing that's given then this is one, and you're short."

Mira stood there with the sea going over the edge behind her and the noise in her teeth.

"Give it to me," she said.

She carried it up the shingle at a run with Toby behind her and two marines and the case that had
held the three Embers, and they put the boat in, and they went out to the north rock where the
housing stood on its bracket with the cup empty and the lens black.

The blade went in point down. It did not fit — it was a sword and not a lamp — and Mira packed the
cup with oakum and wedged it and put her hand on the hilt and said, "Now. For the coast. For the
seventeen. For whatever you're for."

And the teal came up, all along the fuller, steady and deep and moving like water in a band, and the
lens took it and threw it, and there was a light on the north rock of the Fall.

Toby made a sound like a boy being told he has passed something.

"Right," said Mira. "Get on the rock and don't let it go out."

---

At a quarter to seven the guides went out along the shingle, one behind each keeper's boat, and stood
in the water up to their knees with their bands uncovered.

Nobody had ordered it. Odo Pike had said, on the shingle, "They can't see what they're holding. We
can," and eleven people had walked out into the shallows and spread along nine miles of shore the way
they had spread along a corridor under the Kindling for four hundred years.

"Ede Calloway," said Odo Pike, at her back. "You've got a hairline in your wall at twenty feet, port
side, where the rock shelves. Push it out six inches."

"I've been holding a ring for eleven years."

"Aye," said Odo Pike. "And you've been holding it round an island. This is a shore. It's not the
same trade and I'll not have you learning it at the turn."

At the tavern-keeper's back was a guide of about twenty called Rill, who had come down the whole
coast on the bow of a Crown ship calling the road, and who was, by the end of that week, the most
famous man in the fleet.

"Your wall's all right," Rill said. "It's got a bend in it. It's *supposed* to have a bend in it.
There's a shelf under you. Don't straighten it."

"I'm not straightening anything."

"You are. You're doing it with your shoulders." Rill put his hand flat on the gunwale. "Shove it
out over the shelf and let the shelf do the work. Like leaning on a wall."

"I'm in a boat."

"Then lean on the water," said Rill, and the tavern-keeper laughed, which was the last time anybody
heard him laugh.

---

At six the collection quickened.

Nobody said so. The Receiver stood on the water in the middle of the bay with her book open and her
finger moving down the page, and the Hollowed came down the shingle in rows and stepped off the lip
one after another — a woman with a baby's shawl over her arm, a man in a tax clerk's coat, a girl of
about twelve — and the mist took each of them and closed, and the Fall went on with its noise, and
the book got a mark in it.

Voss stood in the bows of a boat forty feet off the shingle with her hands behind her back.

Konrad Voss came down in the third row.

He came down the shingle in his good coat with his hands at his sides, walking the way all of them
walked, and he came level with the boat, and he turned his head, and he looked at his sister.

"Konrad," she said.

He stopped.

"Konrad. It's Ilse."

He looked at her for a moment with the mild interest of a man acknowledging a stranger.

Then he turned his head back to the water and walked on, and the mist took him, and the Fall went on.

Voss did not move for a long time. Then she said, in a perfectly ordinary voice, "Mr Sallow. Put the
hour in the book. Konrad Voss, keeper of the Second, at the turn of the sixth hour." She turned
round. "Get me to the north end. I will not be sitting in a boat when the ring goes up."

Sallow counted them out loud.

Nobody asked him to. He was standing in the stern of the third boat with his book open against the
mast and his pencil wet, and he said "Four," as a woman with her hair in a knot stepped off the lip,
and then "Five," and then nothing for a while, and then "Six."

"What are you doing?" said Hale.

"Counting," said Sallow. "Somebody has to count. It is the whole of my trade and I have been doing it
for thirty years and I am not going to stop doing it because it is unbearable."

"Seven. Eight."

A man came down the shingle carrying a lamp — an ordinary brass lamp, wicked, trimmed, dry — and
walked off the edge of the world holding it, and did not light it, and could not have.

"Nine."

Mira heard him from the middle of the line, and heard the number go through the fleet the way a bell
goes through a harbour.

"Ten."

The thing standing in the mist with one foot on the land did not move while it was being counted. It
had no need to. It had been waiting eleven years and it could wait an hour, and the counting was not
a thing it objected to. It was, Mira thought later, the only courtesy it ever showed anybody.

Sallow said, "Eleven," at four minutes to seven, and then did not say anything else, because at seven
the first ring went out over the water, and the Hollowed came down the shingle in a body and pressed
against the inside of the wall and could not get through, and the collection stopped.

---

The turn of the tide was at seven, and the Receiver closed her book at three minutes to.

"The interest is met," she said. "The principal is due. Keeper of the Ninth — the Mother's book is
open and the account is nine. The coin is seasons, or a light that has been given. Put your lights
in the water and I will count them as they come."

Mira stood up in the boat with the Ember in her hand and looked along nine miles of shore.

"Ede," she said. "Say it for us. You're the only one who's done it before."

Ede Calloway stood up in her boat, seventy-one years old, with her coat buttoned wrong and her stick
under her arm, and said, "You've all held a light. This is the same and it's longer. Put it out over
the water and push it along until it meets the next one, and when it meets, don't stop. That's the
whole of it. Don't stop." She looked along the line. "And when it starts taking, let it. Don't
fight it. It's not a thief. It's a bill."

"Now," said Mira.

Seven rings went out over the water at once.

---

Mira had held a light before. She had held a hatch for a count of a hundred and eighty, and a bar of
light nine miles long on water with a ship coming, and a lane in the dark for a boat full of people.
This was not that.

The ring went out of her hand like a rope going out of a coil, and it kept going. It went north
along the lip at the height of a man's chest, and she felt it find Ede's and join, and Ede's go on
and find Meridew's, and south it went out of her other side and found the tavern-keeper's and joined
with a click she felt in her teeth, and the whole of it went round the bay in eleven seconds, nine
miles of edge with both ends on rock.

And then the sea inside it went flat.

Not calm. *Flat* — the way a plate is flat — and the noise of the Fall went out of the world, and
the mist stopped moving, and every man in twenty-two ships heard his own blood in his ears because
there was nothing else to hear.

In the flat water the road came up.

Mira had seen it a hundred times through a band and through a lens: the drowned road, gold and
broken, running under three feet of water. This was not that. The road in the ring came up *black* —
black and dry and plain, every stone of it, a road nine miles wide at the lip and running back into
the dark behind them — and it went over the edge of the Fall.

It went over the edge and kept going.

"There's a road," said Pell, in a boat somewhere, in a very small voice. "Miss. There's a road over
the Fall."

"Don't look at it," said Mira. "Look at your hands."

And then the thing on the shingle moved.

---

It had been standing with one foot on the land since four o'clock, a mile of grey shape in the mist,
and now it came down the shingle towards the ring, and it did not hurry, and it was the size of a
town.

It reached the ring at the south end first, where Teo's boat was.

Mira felt it happen the way you feel a door open in another room: the line of the ring went taut and
sang, and Teo's place in it went white-hot and then *grey*, and Isolde's voice came across the water
flat and clear: "Hold it. Hold it. Count with me. One, two—"

The thing went along the ring looking for an end.

That was the worst minute of Mira's life. It was not a blow. It was a *search*: a mile of grey
patience going along the wall of the ring, feeling at it, the way a man runs his hand along a wall in
a dark house looking for the door he knows is there, and every place it touched went cold and old,
and the stones under it went grey, and the men in the nearest ships found they could not remember the
morning.

It came up the western side of the horseshoe, and behind it the Hollowed came down the shingle in a
body and walked into the ring and could not get through: they pressed against the inside of it the way
rain presses against a window, eight hundred and eighty quiet people with their hands at their sides,
and the men in the nearest ships could see their faces through nine inches of nothing.

"They're not fighting it," said Hale, from the *Surety*'s rail. "They're not even trying. They're just
*waiting* at it."

"They're not people," said Sallow. "That's what a Hollowed is. It's the shape a person leaves."

And the fleet rowed.

That had not been in the plan either. Kettle had said he would not be told to hold still in the water
while the sea came up, and he had meant it, and he had spent three days since the vote in the cabin
not saying anything to anybody and looking at the water; and at six that evening he had gone round the
decks with a boatswain's whistle and a mouth full of teeth and told every crew in twenty-two sail that they could not hold a
ring and could not read a road, but they could row, and by God they could get between the ring and the
thing that was leaning on it, one boat at a time, all night, and be something in the way.

So there were boats inside the ring: forty of them, bow to stern, a mile and a half of oars going, and
every man in them with a hooded lamp at his feet and his eyes on the next man's transom. It was the
stupidest and bravest thing Mira ever saw. When a boat came to the wall it turned and went back down
the line and another came up, and the men in them did not shout, because Odo Pike had told them not
to, and because there was something out there that was listening.

At half past seven Marrow began to sing.

He was in the *Mercy Bell*'s boat with the hood off his lamp and his band glowing, and he sang the
tide-bell of the Sill — the four notes, the long one and the three — the way the Silvers used to sing
it coming in over the bar at three in the morning. Rill picked it up from the bow of the next boat.
Then a Crown quartermaster who could not carry a tune in a bucket took it up and got it wrong, and
somebody laughed, and then eleven boats were singing it wrong together, and the noise of it went
across the flat water and up the shingle and into the mist.

And the mist, for the first time in eleven years, sang back.

Not in words. It came back at them low and long and out of tune, an answer from something that had
heard a harbour once and had forgotten what it was for, and every man in the line felt it in the
floorboards, and Kettle stood up in his boat and shouted, "Aye! Aye, that's right, you great grey
article, you've heard it before!" and the crews laughed, and the wall held.

Pell was in the boat behind Mira's with his hand over his mouth.

"Miss," he said. "Miss. Third from the left. The one with the blue coat."

Mira looked. A woman of about fifty in a good blue coat, with her hair pinned up the way women in
Carrow pinned it, standing with her hands at her sides and her face to the water.

"That's Mrs Arden," said Pell. "She had the chandlery. She gave me a biscuit every time I went for
the paraffin and she always said the same thing, she said, *mind the step, it's the loose one.*" He
was crying and not making any noise about it. "She's been standing in a row on a beach for four days
and she hasn't gone over, and I've been — we've been — " He wiped his face with his sleeve. "Can we
get her? Can we just get her and put her on a ship?"

"No," said Mira.

"Why not?"

"Because if we open this ring for one woman in a blue coat, the thing on the other side of it comes
through, and it doesn't stop at one." She said it as gently as she could, which was not gently at
all. "She's not going over. That's the whole of what we can do for her tonight, Pell. She's not going
over."

Pell looked at the woman in the blue coat for a long time.

"Mind the step," he said, to nobody. "It's the loose one."

And the thing behind them came up the western side, and Mira held the middle of the ring with her
arms shaking, and this is the part she could never put down properly afterwards: it was not a
monster. It did not hate her. It came along the wall the way weather comes along a coast, patient and
complete, and when it found a place where the light was thin it *leaned*, and the leaning was worse
than any blow because there was nothing to hit and nobody to hate.

It reached the north rock at eight minutes past seven, and it found the teal light, and it stopped.

Mira felt the teal take the weight of it the way a man takes a shove against a door: the light dimmed,
and held, and then — this is the thing she wrote down four times and never got right — the light
*grew*. It went from the colour of deep water to the colour of the sea in the last of the light, and
the thing on the other side of it made a sound like a bell rung under water by somebody who did not
know the tune, and drew back.

"That's a light," said Toby, on the rock, in a voice of pure terror. "That's a *light*."

"Keep it trimmed," said Mira.

---

The Receiver began to count at a quarter past seven.

"One," she said. "The keeper of the Ninth. A season." She turned a page. "Two. The keeper of the
Ninth before her. A season."

It did not hurt. That was what Mira had not been told and could not have believed: it did not hurt at
all. It was like a hand going into a pocket and taking something out, and the pocket not knowing
until later.

She was holding a wall of light nine miles long with six other people and a boy on a rock. There was a
*grain* in it. And the way you hold something that long is that you give your mouth a job.

She was saying the vow.

Not out loud — under her breath, along the wall, the way she had said it in the cabin for a year. She
was at the third line of it and the wall was holding and she thought, *I have got it right, I have
finally got it right,* and reached for the fourth line.

And there was nothing there.

Not the words. She had the words. They were on page nine of the notebook in her coat and she could
see the page, *answer for*, *keep*, *until the wick is out*. What had gone was the thing that made
them a sentence. She put *not for the crown* down next to *not for the coin* and they sat beside each
other like two stones in a yard.

She tried it the other way, the short form, the one you say when there is no time, and got as far as
*light what you can* and the rest of it went out of her reach like a word she had said too often to
hear.

Ede was thirty feet away in her own boat. Ede was saying something to the tavern-keeper across the
water, and Mira turned towards it the way you turn to a wall in the dark, and there was no wall, and
that was not a memory going. That was a hand going into a pocket.

*Hold the light,* she told herself. *Count. One. Two.*

"Three," said the Receiver. "The keeper of the Third. A season."

Meridew, sixty years old, with a bad hip and eleven years of nothing in her head, said, "Oh," in a
small surprised voice, and then said, "I had a daughter. I had a daughter and I have just—" and then
she stopped, and held her ring, and did not let it go, and the ring did not even flicker.

"Four," said the Receiver. "The keeper of the Fourth. A season."

The tavern-keeper's light went out at twenty past seven.

He did not lose his grip. His body did: he went down in the boat with his hand still out and his ring
still on and the light gone, and the ring had a hole in it nine feet wide, and the thing on the
other side of the wall turned towards the hole the way water turns towards a drain.

"Vey!" Mira shouted. "The hole! South of you!"

And Mr Vey, thirty years old, licensed keeper of the Fifth, who had never in his life done anything
faster than a fair copy, stood up in his boat and put his ring out and *took* the hole — took it the
way a man takes a falling ladder, badly and with his whole body — and the ring closed and the wall
held and the Receiver said, "Five. The keeper of the Fifth. A season."

"Six," she said, a minute later. "The keeper of the Fifth. A second season, entered at his request."

Vey's hands were shaking. He was holding two segments of a nine-mile ring with one ring and one pair
of arms, and he was still standing, and Mira could hear him saying something over and over, and it
was the only prayer Mr Vey knew, which was a list: "Item one. Item two. Item three."

"Seven," said the Receiver. "The keeper of the Ninth's navigator. A season."

Teo made no sound. He had no sound left to make. He stood in the boat at the south end with both
hands out and Isolde's hands on his shoulders, and the light went out of him in a long slow pull,
and when it was done he said, "Isolde. What's a dock?"

"It's where a ship ties up," said Isolde.

"No," said Teo. "What's a *dock*? I know the word. I know it's a place. I've got the word and there's
nothing behind it." He was still holding the light. "There's a lot of it gone. There's a lot of me
gone. Isolde—"

"Hold the light," said Isolde Sable. "Count with me. One. Two."

And the Receiver said, "Eight. The keeper of the Ninth's captain. A season," and Isolde's face
changed, and she said, "Oh, that's not fair, I only held the *rope*," and then she said, "What was the
name of my ship?"

"Hold the light," said Mira, across the water. "Isolde. Hold the light."

"*What was the name of my ship?*"

The wall held.

---

"Nine," said the Receiver, at twenty-six minutes past seven, and stopped.

The book did not close.

"The account is short by one," she said. "I have eight seasons."

"We've given you nine," said Vey. "Mine twice."

"Eight seasons," said the Receiver, without looking up, "and one entry that is not a season. The
ninth line of this account is a **light**: the anchor at the north rock, which is not a keeper and has
no ring and is not on the book. It is holding. It is not paying. The Ember on the south rock I do not
charge for; it was quenched in a season and entered in this book the day it was cut, and the Mother
does not bill a thing twice. A light that has been given is the only other coin she takes, and I can
take it and close the book, or I can take the collection and close the book, and the turn of the tide
is in four minutes."

Mira looked north along the wall of the ring, nine miles of it, to the rock where a boy of sixteen
stood with his hands on a sword that had never lit for him before Thursday.

"Toby!" she shouted. "Toby, can you hear me?"

"Yes, miss!"

"The Receiver wants a season out of your light. It's not a season. It's seventeen. It's seventeen
people from a village called Ostrey and every one of them gave it freely and none of them got
anything back." Her voice cracked. "You don't have to. It's not your coast. You're not on the book.
You're the only person in this bay who isn't on the book."

The wind was coming up. The ring sang.

Toby Larch stood on the north rock of the Fall with his hands on the hilt and looked out at nine
miles of light with a mile of grey patience pressing on the other side of it.

"My name's on the book," he said. "It's on the Ordinator's list. It's been on it since I was twelve
and he wrote it in his own hand." He set his feet. "Take it. Take all of it. It's not doing anything
standing in a rock."

"Toby—"

"*Take it,*" said Toby Larch, "and tell him I learned what a kept coast is."

---

The Receiver took it.

The teal went out along the fuller from the hilt to the point, one long breath at a time, and the
light did not dim and die the way a lamp does. It went *up* — it got bigger and thinner and brighter,
and it filled the lens, and it filled the housing, and it went out over the water in a bar that made
every man in twenty-two ships put his hand over his eyes, and then it went into the book.

And when it had gone, there was still a light on the north rock: small, thin, the colour of deep
water, holding on in a cup packed with oakum.

"Nine," said the Receiver. "The account is settled."

She closed the book.

The Fall went quiet.

---

That is not a figure of speech and it is the only thing in this notebook I have written down without
having to think about how to say it. The Fall went quiet. The noise that had been in everybody's
teeth since the Sill — that grinding, that *world grinding its teeth* — stopped, the way a bell stops
when the ringer puts his hand on it, and there was a sound after it that nobody on that shore had
ever heard, which was the sea, ordinary sea, going over the edge in the ordinary way.

The mist came down. The grey thing on the shingle was not there any more; there was a wet mark on the
stones a mile long and nothing standing in it.

And eight hundred and eighty Hollowed, on the shingle, stopped walking.

They stopped where they were, in rows, with their faces to the water, and they did not go over the
lip, and they did not turn round, and they stood there in the rain.

The Receiver walked up out of the bay with her book under her arm and stopped in front of the fleet.

"The account is closed," she said. "Eleven years of light, eight seasons paid, one light given. The
Mother will not trouble this coast again in your lifetime." She looked at Voss, who was standing in
the bows of a boat with her gloves off. "The Mother will trouble your children's. That is not a
threat. It is a schedule."

"Receiver," said Voss. "My brother."

"He is not in the book," said the Receiver. "He was collected at the turn. He is over the Fall, and
the Fall is not a place I keep an account of, and I am sorry." She said it without any change of
face at all, and it was, Mira thought afterwards, the only kind thing that woman ever said.

"You have eight hundred and eighty of your people left on that shore. Nine went over at four as the
interest and eleven went over at the turn while you were lighting your lamps, and your clerk counted
all eleven, and I let him, because a count that nobody keeps is not a count. They are empty and they
will stay empty. That is what the interest looked like. You may do as you like with them."

She walked out into the middle of the bay and went under, and the water closed, and that was the last
anybody on the Reach ever saw of her.

---

Voss stood at the north rock for a long time with Toby and the small light, and then she came down
the shingle to where Sallow was sitting with his book.

"Mr Sallow."

"Admiral."

"You wrote the hour my brother went over."

"I did."

"I want it in the fleet's book as well, in your hand, with the rest of them. And I want the entry to
say that he was a keeper of the Second and that he kept it for nine years and that he walked to the
end of the world in a good coat and said my name when he was asked." She straightened her gloves.
"And then I want you to write down that the Admiral of the Fleet did nothing, because that is the
truth and the truth is what the book is for."

"Admiral—"

"Write it, Mr Sallow. I have spent eleven years wishing somebody had written it down the first
time."

Sallow wrote it. It is four lines long and it is the only entry in the fleet's book that has a
stain on it.

---

They came off the water at nine o'clock, and the light went out of the ring one segment at a time as
each boat came in, and the sea in the bay was sea again.

Mira could not remember getting into the boat. She remembered Pell's hands on her arm and the shingle
under her boots and somebody putting a coat round her, and she remembered Ede sitting on a rock with
her stick across her knees, and she remembered going to her and kneeling down and saying, "Ede."

"Yes," said Ede.

"Say the vow with me," said Mira.

So Ede said it, in the flat carrying voice of a woman who had said it every night for fifty years, and
Mira said the first line with her, and got the second one wrong, and stopped, and started again, and
got the third one, and then the fourth was a hole with nothing on the other side of it.

"Ah," said Ede.

"I had it in the fleet," said Mira. "I had all of it. I got it right in front of twenty-three sail and
I have been practising for a year, and now there's a hole in the middle of it the size of a line."

"Which line?"

"The fourth." Mira pressed the heels of her hands into her eyes. "I can read it. It's on page nine. It
is just words on a page."

Ede sat with that for a while.

"Well," she said. "You held nine miles of wall while it was going. I'd say the vow did its job and the
light took its wages." She knocked her stick against the rock. "And I'll tell you the thing nobody
tells you, because there has never been anybody to tell it to. It isn't the words that keep a light.
It's the saying. So you'll say it after me every night until your mouth remembers it, and if your
mouth never remembers it, you'll say it after me anyway, and it will keep."

"That isn't the same as having it."

"No," said Ede Calloway. "It isn't."

---

Isolde came and sat down on the stones beside her with her boots in her hand.

"You asked me on the quarterdeck," she said. "What I saw. In the eleven minutes."

"I did."

"I said there was nothing down there that was going to be impressed by a ring, and that was true, and
it was not the whole of it, and I have been carrying the whole of it around for eleven days like a
stolen spoon." She turned her boots over in her hands. "There's a door."

Mira did not say anything.

"At the bottom of the Reach, under the third bell, where the water is not water. It stands open. It's
not the Mother's door — I've been to the Mother's door, everybody on this coast has been to the
Mother's door, it's a queue — this one stands open the way a door stands open in a house where
somebody is expected, and there's a light on the other side of it. Not a lamp. A *day*."

"Isolde—"

"There was a man standing in it," said Isolde. "In a good coat, with his hat in his hands, the way a
man stands in a doorway when he doesn't want to come in. And he looked at me the way you look at
somebody who has come to the wrong house, and he said, *you're early*, and then he said, *tell them
the road goes on.*" She looked at Mira. "I have been trying to work out for eleven days who I was
supposed to tell, and then tonight a boy put a sword in a lamp and the Fall went quiet, and I thought:
oh. It's her."

Mira sat with that for a while. The sea went over the edge in the ordinary way.

"Was it Alder Vane?" she said.

"I don't know what he looked like," said Isolde. "I know what he sounded like. I'd know it in a
harbour." She pulled her boots on, one and then the other, and did not look up. "I can't remember the
name of my ship. I know it had a bell in it. I've got the bell and I've lost the rest and I would
give the whole of the Reach to have the rest."

"Ask me tomorrow," said Mira. "I'll write it down for you. I write everything down."

"You'd better," said Isolde Sable, "because somebody in this fleet has to be the one who remembers,
and it is not going to be the woman who used to be a captain."

---

Meridew sat on the shingle with a blanket round her shoulders and her ring hand in her lap, and
looked at Mira as if she were a stranger who had been kind.

"You're the keeper of the Ninth," she said.

"I am."

"They tell me I had a daughter."

Mira sat down on the stones beside her.

"Yes," she said.

"What was her name?"

And Mira did not know. Nobody knew: Meridew had kept a light in a drowned tower for eleven years and
had never told a soul in the fleet what her daughter was called, because there had been nobody to
tell, and now there was nobody to ask.

"I'm sorry," said Mira. "I don't know it."

"That's all right," said Meridew. "I thought you might. You look like somebody who writes things
down." She looked out at the water. "I have got the whole of her except the name. I have got a girl
in a green dress with her hair wet and a row about a boat, and I have got a hand in mine, and I
haven't got the one word you'd put at the top of the page." She patted Mira's knee. "If you ever
hear it, keep it for me. I'll be the old woman on the wall at the Sill."

---

The tavern-keeper was alive.

They had got his coat open in the boat and Rill's hands on his chest before the ring had even come
down, and it had taken four minutes and a great deal of swearing, most of it Hale's. That was the
first thing she said when they carried him up the shingle: he was alive, and he was sitting on the
stones with a blanket round him and no light in him at all, and he was asking, very politely, whether
anybody could tell him what a boat was.

"I know what it *is*," he said, when Mira crouched down in front of him. "I've got the word. It's a
thing that floats and you get in it. I've got all of it except the part where I know it." He looked
at his hands. "I had a pub."

"You did."

"Was it a good one?"

"I'm told it had a very good window," said Mira, "and a sign that dripped, and a last man who always
said goodnight twice."

"Huh," said the tavern-keeper, and sat in the rain and thought about that, and then said, "I'd like
to go and see it."

Rook carried him up the shingle on his back, which a knight without a surcoat is still allowed to
do, and came back down with his boots full of water and stood in front of Mira and did not say
anything for a moment.

"Well?" she said.

"I have been trying to think what to say to eight hundred and eighty people who cannot go home," said
Rook. "And I find I have one thing, and it is not a knight's thing, it is a guide's, and I am going to
say it whether or not it is mine to say." He turned round to the shingle and raised his voice, which
he had never done in his life. "*We will guide any ship that asks.*"

"Say it again," said Mira.

Rook said it again, to the shingle and the eight hundred and eighty and the rain, in a voice that was
not a knight's voice at all.

"Again."

He said it a third time, and she stood in the rain and listened to it the way you listen to a language
you used to speak.

Rook looked at her, and did not ask.

Nobody else answered either. The Hollowed stood in the rain with their faces to the water and did not
turn round, because they could not.

"Then we will guide them home anyway," said Rook, to Mira, quietly. "Somebody has to walk in front. It
might as well be a man with a dead sword and no vows and nothing to do on Monday."

"It might as well be two of us," said Mira.

---

Vey was on the shingle with the ledger open on his knees, writing.

He had his pen in his right hand and his left hand flat on the page and he was copying, letter by
letter, out of the book in front of him, and it took him a long time to do a word.

"Mr Vey."

"Miss Calloway." He did not look up. "I am entering the day. It is the last entry and it is the
longest and I am going to get it right."

"Your hand—"

"My hand is fine," said Vey. "My hand is perfectly fine." He wrote another word, slowly, the way a
child copies out a sentence from a board. "I cannot read it. That is all. I can write it and I cannot
read it, and I have written nine years of assessments and I am told they are very good, and I will
never know what they said." He dipped the pen. "It is a fair price for a coast and I would like it
noted that I paid it without arguing, which is the first thing in my life I have done without
arguing."

---

Mira wrote it up at midnight in the *Surety*'s cabin with the door open and the sea making an
ordinary noise outside, which she kept stopping to listen to.

*Friday, ninth of the ninth, the turn of the tide at the Fall.*

*Seven holders, nine miles, two anchors, and a boy on a rock.*

*Ede taught them: put it out and push it along and when it meets, don't stop, and when it starts
taking, let it, because it isn't a thief, it's a bill.*

*The ring went up at seven. The sea went flat and the road came up black and dry, and it went over
the edge of the Fall and kept going, and Pell said there's a road over the Fall and I told him to look
at his hands.*

*The thing came down the shingle and went along the ring looking for an end, the way a man runs his
hand along a wall in a dark house. It found the north rock and the teal blade, and the light dimmed
and held and then it grew, and the thing drew back.*

*The Receiver counted. Eight seasons: mine, Ede's, Meridew's, the tavern-keeper's, Vey's twice,
Teo's, Isolde's. The tavern-keeper's heart went at twenty past and Vey took the hole with one ring
and both arms and entered it as a second season at his own request.*

*I have lost the vow.*

*Not the words. The words are on page nine and I have read them out to Ede twice tonight. What is gone
is the thing that joins them up. I got to the fourth line with nine miles of wall in my hands and
there was nothing there, and I said it whole in front of twenty-three sail on Monday, and the light
took it as wages. That is the joke and it is the whole joke: the one thing I was good at.*

*Ede says it is not the words that keep a light, it is the saying, and that I will say it after her
every night until my mouth remembers, and that it will keep. I have written that down. I do not
believe it yet. I have written it down anyway, because that is what the notebook is for.*

*There is a verse under the long form. The first four lines are Ede's, written at nineteen in pencil,
and they have been under the vow for fifty-two years, and I have read them a hundred times and never
once aloud. The second four came to me tonight with a pen in my hand and I have not told her, and she
will have something to say about the rhyme. I am copying out all eight because I can still read,
which is what I have got left.*

*One holds the dawn, one holds the dusk.*  
*Between them, nothing breaks or rusts.*  
*Two sparks that circle, never fight,*  
*and make a single, steady light.*

*You keep your end and I keep mine,*  
*and let the dark come up the line.*  
*A shore has ends. A ring has none.*  
*Two rocks, and nine miles held as one.*

*Eight lines. Two ends. She has been telling me the plan of the Fall since I was six years old and I
thought it was a hymn, and it turns out it was an instruction.*

*Meridew lost a daughter. She had one. She had one for sixty years and she gave it to a wall and did
not let the wall flicker.*

*Teo lost a dock. Not the word — the thing. He asked Isolde what a dock was, and she told him, and he
said he had the word and nothing behind it, and then he held the light anyway. Isolde lost the name
of her ship and told me what she saw in her eleven minutes, which she had been carrying around for
eleven days like a stolen spoon.*

*Vey can write and cannot read. He is copying the last entry out of a book he will never read again
and he says it is a fair price and the first thing he has ever done without arguing.*

*And Toby Larch gave a light that seventeen people gave him, and it went up instead of out, and the
book closed.*

*The Fall went quiet. The noise stopped and there was ordinary sea going over the edge and eight
hundred and eighty people standing in the rain not walking.*

*Twenty went over the lip last night: nine at four as the interest, and eleven between six and seven
while we were putting the lights in the water, and Sallow counted every one of them out loud because
somebody had to. One of the eleven was Konrad Voss, and the Admiral watched him go and put the hour in
the book, and then went and stood at the north end with a boy and a light, and I have never seen
anybody do a harder thing in a boat.*

*Cost, today: eight seasons and a light, an admiral's brother, and the vow.*

*The account is closed. The Mother will trouble our children's. That is not a threat, the Receiver
said. It is a schedule.*

*We have eight hundred and eighty empty people on a shingle and a fleet with one lamp each and a coast
behind us that is dark and has nobody on it, and tomorrow we have to go home and find out what home
looks like.*
