# Chapter Twenty-Seven

The council met in the guides' room on Sunday morning, because the guides' room was the only
place in the Kindling with a table big enough and a door that shut.

Eleven chairs, and every one of them taken. Halloran at the head with his spectacles on. Marshal
Hale with the yellow band on her left wrist that Mira had never seen before and could not stop
looking at. Vey at the far end with the Crown's ledger and a pen he had not uncapped. Voss
standing, because she had come off a boat at two in the morning and had not been to bed. Rook
against the wall in a grey coat with no surcoat and the dead sword in a scabbard that no longer
had anything to do with him. Isolde Sable with her hat on the table and her ship gone. Teo by the
door with a piece of chalk and a board, because a man who cannot find a harbour any more counts
things out loud. Sallow with a pencil. Odo Pike at the other end with the map of the road on the
wall behind him in three colours. Marrow, who had come up the stair at dawn and had not gone to
bed either. And Toby Larch in the corner, on a stool, out of everybody's way, with a grey blade
across his knees.

"You first," said Halloran, to Voss. "You have the worst news and the room is fresh."

"I have a letter," said Voss. "Two letters. The first one is from the Throne and it relieves me of
my command as of the ninth night, which is the politest thing that has ever been done to me,
because it gives me six days to be an admiral in." She put the letter on the table. "The second
one names my successor and is to be opened by him on the ninth night and not before, and I have
not opened it, and I would like that noted, because I am going to do something illegal in front of
all of you before the week is out and I would like the small things on the record."

"What does the first one say?" said Hale.

"It says the coast is to be surrendered to the Assize if the Assize can be shown to be a lawful
power, and that no Crown ship is to be hazarded west of the Cradle after the ninth night." Voss
took her gloves off and put them on the table, one beside the other. "It does not say *abandon the
coast*. It says *do not hazard ships*. There is a difference and it is the difference between a
man who loses a fleet and a man who loses a coast, and Saltmere has decided which one it can
survive."

"Sallow," said Halloran. "What do we know?"

Sallow stood up, which he did now, and read from his notebook in the voice of a man who had once
been paid by the week to make this interesting.

"The Ash's ship — the *Vigil*, still, God help us — was seen from the north tower on Friday evening
going south-west under bare poles with her one window lit. Two of the free crews saw her on
Saturday, four miles off the Cradle, still south-west, still no lights but the window. The un-ships
are gone." He turned a page. "All of them. Every hull that has been sitting in the dark water since
before the Fall. There were nine in the Reach on Wednesday and there are none today, and a fisherman called Tarn on the Sill road says he saw them going south in a line, and that they had
no crews, and that they were making a noise."

"What kind of noise?"

"He says it was like a bell being rung under water by somebody who did not know the tune."

Nobody said anything.

"South-west is the Fall," said Mira.

"South-west is the Fall," said Sallow.

---

"Right," said Halloran. "Marshal. The yellow."

Hale put her hand on the table and turned it over, and the band on her wrist was not green. It was
the colour of a lamp seen through a jar of honey: a slow yellow, thick, going round.

"This is the order's other instrument, and there are three of them in this fortress and I have
never once been glad to put one on," she said. "It is a watch band. A green band shows the road. A
yellow one shows **who is on it** — the living as warm shapes, the Hollowed as holes, the Ash as
the place where the road stops being there at all. That is why the Crown hates them. A band on
credit is a parish with a navy."

"Show them," said Halloran.

Hale shut her eyes.

"It's full," she said. "The road's full."

"Full of what?"

"People." Her voice changed. "Walking. South. Hundreds. Nine hundred. They're not running, they're
not hurrying, they're walking the way people walk when they have been walking for days and there
is nowhere else to be. There's a hole where each of them should be and they're all going the same
way, and the road under them is being eaten behind them, and at the end of it —"

She stopped.

"Marshal."

"At the end of the road there's a place where the road stops being *there*," said Hale. "It isn't
the Ash. The Ash is a hole on the road. This is the road not being there. I have been a watch
sergeant for nineteen years and I have never seen the like of it and I would very much like to take
this thing off now."

She took it off. The room was quiet in the way a room is quiet when forty people have all decided
not to be the one who says it.

"They're not coming for us," said Mira.

"No," said Vey, and surprised everybody. "They are going to the Fall. All of them. Every Hollowed
on this coast, and every un-ship, and the thing that has been following us since Sixthmonth." He
looked at his ledger, and then at the room, and he looked, for the first time in the book, like a
man who had been keeping the wrong book. "I have a list of the Hollowed. It is four hundred pages
and it is not complete, and I have been adding to it every quarter for nine years, and I have never
once asked what they were *for*."

"They're going to a collection," said Isolde.

Everybody turned.

"The Brine Mother collects on the ninth night," she said. "She has a contract on this coast and she
collects in units of light, and every ship and every drowned thing and every empty man on the Reach
is hers to call, and she is calling them. That's not a war. That's a *creditor* getting her books
in order."

"Then we have been asking the wrong question all week," said Halloran.

"What's the right one?" said Mira.

"Not *how do we hold the coast*," said the Warden. "The coast is not the thing being foreclosed on.
The question is what a creditor does when the debtor turns up at the counter and pays."

---

Odo Pike got up and went to the wall.

"Before anybody plans anything," he said, "there is a thing in this room that has not been said out
loud for two hundred years, and if we are going to do what I think we are going to do, it gets said
first."

He took a key off a ring and unlocked the case on the wall and took out the one band in it, and put
it on the table with a cloth under it.

It was orange. Thick, slow, asleep. It did not turn towards Mira's ring and it did not turn towards
Hale's yellow band, and it lay there like a piece of boiled sweet.

"That's the caramel," said Odo Pike, and put the cloth back over it. "There's four colours on the
old roll and one of them is scratched out, and I'll tell you what they are, because if we are going
to be seen this week I would like us to know what we are."

He turned the map round and wrote on the back of it with a stub of chalk, in a guide's hand, which
is to say crookedly and fast.

**GREEN.** The road.

**YELLOW.** The watch.

**ORANGE.** The road taken out of the people walking it. Once in four hundred years. In a case.

And then a fourth line, scratched out so hard the chalk went through the paper:

**WHITE.** The road to the dead. Never made. Every generation of guides has a story about a band
that would show a man the way down and not the way back, and every generation of guides has the
same rule about it, which is *don't*.

"That's the four," said Odo Pike. "Now the vow, because we're going to need it, and because the
last time it was said aloud in this fortress the man who said it was hanged at Saltmere."

He put his band on the table next to the orange one, and the eleven of them stood up.

---

"The guides' vow is older than the keeper's," said Odo Pike. "It was cut into the wall of the first
cellar at the Sill and the wall is under sixty feet of water now. There were five lines. The Crown
struck one out two hundred years ago and put its own line in, and we say the struck one anyway, and
it is a night in a cell if anybody hears you, which is why nobody hears us."

He nodded, and the eleven said it. Not together — a guide does not do anything in unison — but in
the way a road is walked, one after another, each one taking it up as the one before put it down:

> *We go first.*
>
> *We carry no light. We carry the road, and we lend it.*
>
> *What we walk, we pay for. What we pay for, we do not count. What we count, we do not keep.*
>
> *If one of us falls, the next takes the band and the road with it, and says his name once, and then walks.*

And then the fifth, the one that was struck out, said quietly and out of step and in eleven
different voices:

> *We will guide any ship that asks.*

The room was quiet.

"That's the greatest vow on this coast," said Odo Pike, "and I'll tell you why, and then I'll sit
down. It is not great because it is old. It is great because there is nothing in it for the man
saying it. There's no light in it, no glory, no rank, no heaven, no pay. A keeper holds a lamp and
a town says thank you. A guide walks in front of a stranger in the dark and the stranger never
knows he was there. That is the whole of it." He sat down. "The Crown struck that line out because
a road that anybody can ask for is a coast that cannot be governed. They were right about that. It
is the only true thing in the whole catechism."

"The Sill," said Odo Pike, when the room had been quiet long enough, "was four hundred families and
a road and a bell, and it went under in a night, and the water came up the stair while thirty-one
guides walked people out of it. They walked all night. They walked the same road four hundred
times, back and forward, and they lost four people, and three of the four were guides who went
back for somebody who had already gone. When it was done there was no town and there was one
guide left standing on the hill with a band on his wrist and nothing in his head from the year
before, and he cut the vow into the cellar wall with a boat-knife while the water came up his legs."

"And the fifth line?" said Vey.

"The fifth line was the argument. There were people on that hill who had waited four hours for a
guide to come and no guide came, because a guide had been told to walk the mayor's family first.
So the thirty-first guide cut *we will guide any ship that asks* into the wall, and then he went
down the hill and did not come back up, and nobody knows whether that was the water or the shame."
Odo Pike sat down. "Two hundred years later a Crown notary read that line in a cellar and wrote a
better one, and the better one is the one they teach in the hall, and it is the reason you have a
list of our names in your book, Mr Vey."

Rook said, from the wall, "Say the last line again."

Odo Pike looked at him.

"*We will guide any ship that asks,*" he said.

Rook put his hand flat on the table and left it there, which was what his hands did when he was
holding something by not touching it, and Mira watched a knight with a dead sword write a sentence
down in his head.

---

The plan took four hours and it was Mira's, which surprised everybody including Mira.

"Nine lights," she said, at the end of the fourth hour, when the table had got as far as it was
going to get on its own. "That's the whole of it. Nine lights at the Fall on the ninth night, all
lit, all at once, and every one of them seen."

"Seen by what?" said Hale.

"By everything. That's what we've been running from all year. A light is a door. So we make the
biggest door on the Reach, in the one place where the thing behind the Ash has to come through to
get to us, and we make it at the hour the Brine Mother collects, and we let them all come to it."

"That is not a plan," said Vey. "That is a *suicide* with a schedule."

"No," said Mira. "It's a debt with a schedule. Ede held four miles of edge round Carrow for
eleven years with no light at all, and the Ash couldn't cross it, because a ring has no ends.
Nine keepers, nine lights, at nine points on the water at the foot of the Fall, and every one
of them holding the same ring. It's never been done because there has never been more than one
keeper at a time willing to pay for a lamp that wasn't hers." She looked round the table.
"Well. There's nine of us now."

"Nine stones is not nine keepers," said Vey. "You have yourself. You have five licensed keepers in
five Crown towers who have never held anything but a wick. You have a woman at Carrow who is
holding four miles of edge and cannot hold two."

"Then we ask them," said Mira.

"Ask them what?"

"Whether they will stand in the water with me on the ninth night. It's a small question and it has
a small answer, and if the answer is no I would rather know it on Tuesday than on Friday."

"Nine," said Halloran.

"Nine." Mira put the Ninth's Ember on the table.

"Where did you get that?" said Hale. "It's Crown property. It's got a number on it."

"The Admiral sent it up at dawn in a canvas bag with a receipt," said Mira. "*Released to keeper,
unassessed, for the duration of the term.* She wrote the last word herself and she did not put her
name on it." She turned the stone with one finger. "There are nine Embers on this Reach and every one
of them is accounted for, and I have got the only one that got a receipt from a woman who is out of
authority in six days."

"We have this one," said Vey, "and three in the *Surety*'s strongroom, and five in the Crown's relit
towers. That is nine. And to take the five out of the towers you must douse the only five lit
lanterns on this coast, which is *rekindling in reverse*, which is not a crime I have a form for,
which means I would have to write one." He uncapped his pen. "I will need the Admiral's warrant."

"You will need the Admiral's warrant," said Voss, "and the Admiral is relieved of her command on
the ninth night, which is the same night." She looked at her gloves. "So the warrant will be valid
for six days and will be signed by a woman with no authority on the seventh."

"That's a legal problem," said Vey.

"It's a *timing* problem," said Voss. "I have spent my life on the difference."

"And the coast goes dark," said Hale. "Five towers out, and the Ninth's Ember off this rock, and
the Ash gone south with every Hollowed on the Reach — and if we are wrong, we have taken the last
five lights off a coast that has nothing else."

"Yes," said Mira.

"And if we are right?"

"Then on the tenth morning there is a coast with nine lanterns lit and a debt paid, and the thing
that has been eating us for eleven years has come through a door we made, in a place we chose, with
nine keepers standing in a ring behind it."

"There is a price in it that you have not said," said Halloran, "and I would rather you said it
before this room decides you are brave."

Mira looked at him.

"The ring," she said.

"The ring. Ede Calloway holds four miles of edge round one island and pays a season a year for it,
and she has eleven seasons in her and she will not have a twelfth." The Warden took his spectacles
off. "The Fall is not four miles. Nine keepers at nine points is a ring the width of the Reach, and
every one of them pays a season for the piece in front of them, and a season is not a smell or a
name — it is a year. Nine years, out of nine people, in one night, at the end of a coast that has
spent eleven years losing things. And a man cannot hold what he cannot picture. When they fail the
ring has a hole in it, and a ring with a hole in it is a line, and a line has ends, and the thing we
are keeping out walks round them."

Nobody said anything.

"Then we practise," said Mira.

"You have four nights."

"Then we practise for four nights."

Hale looked at her for a long time.

"You are eighteen," she said.

"I know."

"I have been a soldier for thirty years and I have never once been in a room where an eighteen-year-old
was right." Hale stood up. "I'll have the watch bands issued. If we're going to be seen, I want to
know who's coming before they arrive."

"One more thing," said Odo Pike. "If we do this, the guides go on the water with the fleet. Openly.
In daylight. Bands on and nothing over them." He looked round the table. "That is not a small thing
for eleven people with boots by the door."

"Then we say the fifth line," said Rook.

Nobody had heard him speak that loudly all week.

"We will guide any ship that asks," said Rook. "You said it is a night in a cell. Well. I have
been a knight for twelve years and I never did anything that was worth a night in a cell, and I
would like to before the ninth night." He looked at Odo Pike. "Ask us."

Odo Pike looked at him.

"When the fleet goes south," said the guide, "I will ask."

---

The free crews came in on Monday, and there were fourteen of them.

Isolde had done it with no ship and no money and a hat: she had gone out to the Cradle in a
borrowed cutter with Sallow and a list of names, and she had stood in four harbours and said the
same thing four times, which was that the *Mercy Bell* was the Crown's now, that the coast was
going to be lit on the ninth night at the Fall, and that any captain who came would be paid in
nothing and would probably die, and that she was going anyway.

Fourteen sails came up the roads on Monday afternoon. Two of them were wreckers who had been
hunting Alder Vane's road for thirty years. One was a Saltmere packet that had no business being
there. One was a lugger called the *Anselm*, which was not the *Anselm* — the *Anselm* went down
nine days before Teo was taken off her, and this one had been renamed by a man with a sense of
history and a poor grip on it.

The Crown's nine came round from the north at dusk with their yards squared and their topsails
furled, and the *Surety* came first, and the whole of it lay in the roads with the sun going down
behind the Cradle: twenty-three sail, and not one light among them.

---

The captains came aboard the *Surety* at seven and the plan lasted eleven minutes.

There were twenty-three of them in the great cabin, standing, because there were not chairs enough
and because Voss had decided that a room full of standing captains argues faster. Vey read the
warrant — the five Embers, the dousing of the five Crown towers, the hour, the place, the signature
of an admiral with five days left on her — and then Voss said, "Questions," and the room came apart.

"With respect, Admiral," said a post captain from the Fourth rate at the back, whose name was
Ollery and who had been at Barrowfoot, "my orders say no Crown ship is to be hazarded west of the
Cradle after the ninth night. You are asking me to hazard nine of them on the ninth night."

"I am asking you to hazard nine of them *at* the ninth night."

"That is the same sentence with the clock moved."

"It is," said Voss. "Sit down, Captain. You are not wrong and I am going to overrule you anyway,
and I would like you to be sitting when I do it."

A wrecker called Kettle, who had been hunting Alder Vane's road for thirty years and had the hands
for it, said the thing that most of the free crews were thinking and none of them wanted to be the
first to say.

"I'll sail with you to the Fall, Admiral, and I'll fight whatever comes off it, and I'll do it for
nothing, because I've been going to the Fall for thirty years and it's the only thing in my life I
never finished." Kettle looked round the cabin. "But I'm not going to stand in the water and hold a
ring for a girl I met on Tuesday, on the word of a woman who gets relieved on Friday, in a plan
that has nine holes in it and a warrant that expires in the middle. That's not a plan. That's a
wake with a schedule."

"It's my plan," said Mira.

"I know it's your plan, miss. I've been listening. You're the only person in this room who's been
straight about the odds." Kettle put his hat on, which in that cabin was a sentence in itself. "And
I'd rather die behind a bad plan than a good one, because a bad plan at least gets you somewhere.
But I'll not be told to hold still in the water while the sea comes up. That's not dying. That's
waiting."

"Then don't hold still," said Mira. "Row. Every crew that comes rows; that's what crews are for.
The nine who hold the ring are keepers, and there aren't nine keepers in this room, there's one,
and five in towers who don't know yet and a woman at Carrow who's seventy-one."

That stopped the cabin for a moment.

"You haven't asked them," said Ollery.

"No. Five cutters go out at dawn with a letter in each, and by the ninth night I'll either have
five keepers or a plan with holes in it." Mira looked round the cabin at twenty-three captains, which is a thing she had never
done before and would not do again. "But I'm not asking anybody in this cabin to be brave. I'm
asking them to stand in the water next to me and hold one thing and not let go. Any of you who has ever stood a watch —
that's the whole of it. That's the offer."

"And if the chain goes?" said Kettle.

"Then the coast is dark and the thing comes through and it's my name on the warrant."

"It's my name on the warrant," said Voss, without looking up.

"It's both our names on the warrant," said Mira, "and Mr Vey's, because he's the one who's going
to write it."

Vey, at the end of the table, did not look pleased about that, and did not deny it either.

"I'll put it to the room," said Voss. "All in favour."

Nine hands went up: the *Surety*'s officers and the Crown captains who had sailed with her for
three years and would have followed her into the Cradle.

Fourteen did not go up.

"Against?"

"Not against," said Kettle. "Abstaining. There's a difference, Admiral, and it's the difference
between a man who says no and a man who hasn't decided yet, and I'd like the record to show that
I'm the second one."

"The record will show that you are the second one," said Vey, and wrote it.

Voss looked at the table for a moment.

"The motion fails," she said. "Nine for, none against, fourteen abstaining, and one admiral who is
going to do it anyway."

---

The crews were on the quay because Voss had asked them ashore, and they stood in the last of the
light in sixes and sevens: navy in blue with their hair cut, free crews in everything they had, the
drowned in wet boots, the guides with their bands covered, the knights in ash-white with their
swords, and Isolde Sable at the front with her hat in her hand.

Nine empty lamp housings were set along the quay, one for each lantern on the Reach, made by the
forge in a day and never lit.

Voss told them the truth in four sentences, which is what she was good at: nine for, none against,
fourteen abstaining, the motion failed, and she was going anyway.

"So it's a free passage," she said. "Any master who does not like it can sail on the tide and I
will not write his name down. I would only ask that he goes before the ninth night and not during
it."

Nobody moved.

Mira stood in front of them and found that she had nothing to say, which was, she realised
afterwards, the whole reason it worked.

"I'm not going to make a speech," she said. "I've only got one thing and it's not mine. It's my
grandmother's, and it's a year old to me and four hundred years old to the order, and the Crown
has a version of it that I am not going to say."

She took the notebook out of her coat, and opened it at the page pasted over a page, and read it
out in a voice that carried further than she expected.

> *In the lit hour and the last hour,*  
> *on water that keeps no road,*  
> *I hold this light as keepers hold —*  
> *not for the crown, not for the coin,*  
> *not because the dark is cruel,*  
> *but because I am here, and I can see,*  
> *and what I can see, I answer for.*

The quay was silent.

She had said it wrong once, in the lamp room at Carrow, before the fifth match, with her hands
shaking and her voice going up at the end: *and what I can see, I answer to.* She had thought about
it for a year. She had written it out forty times. She had said it over a bucket, and in a boat, and
in the dark at the top of a stair.

*I answer for.*

"*Answer for*," she said again, to the fleet, because it was the only word in the whole thing that
mattered and she wanted them to hear it twice. "Not *answer to*. Nobody up there is going to ask us
why. There's no one to explain it to. It means: what I see, I am responsible for. That's all. That's
the whole vow. It doesn't say win."

She closed the notebook.

"On the ninth night we light nine lanterns at the foot of the Fall and we are seen, and every one of
you is going to be seen with us, and I can't promise you what comes through the door. I can promise
you that whatever it is, it will be counted, and that we will be the ones counting it." She looked
along the line of them. "That's the offer. It's a poor one. It's the only one there is."

Nobody moved for a moment.

Then Captain Kettle took his hat off, which in that man was the whole of a conversion, and said,
"Ah, hell," and put his hand up.

And then the hands went up along the quay — not all at once, and not in a wave, but in threes and
fours, the way a tide comes in over a flat shore: the wreckers first, because they had been going to the
Fall for thirty years and had never had a reason; then the Saltmere packet, whose master had a
sister at the Sill; then the Crown's men, one by one, looking at their feet and then at their
Admiral.

"Mr Vey," said Voss. "Take the count."

Vey counted. It took him a while, because he counted properly, walking the line with his pencil
and his book and asking each master his name, and when he came back he said, "Twenty-two of
twenty-three, Admiral. The *Ivy* is sailing on the tide."

"The *Ivy* is a sensible ship," said Voss.

Then she stepped out of the line and took her gloves off and put them in her coat, and said, "One
lamp each. Masthead. Hooded until the ninth night and then not hooded at all." She turned to the
lieutenant at her shoulder. "If we are going to be seen, let us be seen properly."

"Aye, Admiral."

"And Mr Vey."

"Admiral."

"Write it down. All of it. The date and the hour and the names of every ship, and what we did with
the five Embers and who signed for them. If this goes badly, I would like there to be a record that
somebody on this coast did the arithmetic and then did the thing anyway."

Vey uncapped his pen.

"It will be the longest entry in nine years," he said, "and the only one I will ever be proud of."

And then the guides lit their bands.

It was not a signal. Nobody had told them to do it and there was no order given: eleven people on a
quay in the dark, uncovered their wrists, and the green came up the line like water finding a
channel — no beam, no glow, just the slow oily shimmer, going round and round — and the crews saw
it and made a sound that was not a cheer.

Two hundred years of walking in the dark and saying they did not exist, and eleven of them stood
on a quay in front of twenty-three sail and did not cover their wrists.

"Right," said Odo Pike. "Who's for the Fall?"

---

Afterwards, on the wall, Teo was counting.

"North-north-east, forty feet, the bar on the north side, the slip under the gun platform, the
bell wrong by three," he said, under his breath, the way a man says a prayer he is embarrassed by.
"North-north-east. Forty feet."

"You're getting it," said Mira.

"I'm getting the words." He did not look round. "I can say the words and there's still nothing
behind them. It's like being told a room has a door in it and putting your hand out and finding
wall." He turned round then. "Nettle's teaching me by smell. Pell's teaching me by the sound the
slip makes when somebody drops a crate on it. Between them I've got about nine feet of harbour
and I'm told the rest is very nice."

"It's not," said Mira. "It's a stone basin with a chain in it."

"Then I'm not missing much." He was quiet for a moment. "You said nine. At the Fall. Nine keeps
and a ring."

"Yes."

"And you'll be the one in the middle."

"There isn't a middle. It's a ring. That's the whole trick."

"There's always a middle," said Teo. "I've been in a boat with you for a year. You'll stand
somewhere and everybody will look at you and you'll do the arithmetic out loud and it will be the
right arithmetic and somebody will die anyway, and you'll write it down after." He said it without
any bitterness at all, which was how she knew he had thought about it. "What are you going to do
on the tenth morning?"

"Sleep," said Mira. "For a year. Then I'm going to sit in a boat and not be looked at."

"Can I be in the boat?"

"You can be in the boat."

He put his hand out and she took it, and they stood on the wall in the dark with twenty-three hooded
lamps going up the roads behind them and the guides' green moving along the quay below, and neither
of them said anything for a while, because there was nothing to say that would have improved it.

---

Toby Larch asked Odo Pike to teach him the road at ten o'clock at night, and Odo Pike, who had not
slept properly since Friday, said, "Why?"

"Because I've got a blade that's supposed to be worth something and I don't know what for," said
the boy. "And because the Ordinator left me here to learn what a kept coast is, and I have been
here two days and what I've learned is that everybody works and nobody is paid and a man emptied a
lieutenant in a gate and then everybody went back to work."

Odo Pike looked at him for a long moment.

"Sit down," he said. "Give me your hand. Now shut your eyes."

"I haven't got a band."

"You don't need a band to learn the first thing," said Odo Pike. "The first thing is: what is
under your feet right now?"

"A floor."

"What's under the floor?"

"A cellar."

"What's under the cellar?"

Toby thought about it.

"The rock. And the road."

"And under the road?"

"The deep."

"And under the deep?"

"I don't know," said Toby.

"Good," said Odo Pike. "That's the first thing, and it took me four years to learn it and I had to
walk down a stair in the wet to do it. The road is not a thing you know. It's a thing you are
standing on, and it goes further down than you are tall, and the whole of the trade is remembering
that when you are frightened." He let go of the boy's hand. "Second thing tomorrow. Bring the
blade. If it lights for the second thing, we'll know something."

Mira wrote it up at midnight with the fleet's one lamp in each masthead going up the roads behind
her, one by one, hooded but lit.

*Sunday and Monday. The council, the yellow, the vow, the plan.*

*The Ash is not coming for us. It is going to the Fall, and every un-ship and every Hollowed on
this coast is going with it, and the Marshal put on a watch band and looked at the road and saw
nine hundred people walking south with a hole where each of them should be, and at the end of it a
place where the road is not there. Mr Vey says he has a list of the Hollowed four hundred pages
long and he has never once asked what they were for. Isolde says it is not a war, it is a creditor
getting her books in order, and the Warden says the question is not how to hold the coast but what
a creditor does when the debtor turns up at the counter and pays.*

*There are four colours on the old roll and one of them is scratched out. Green for the road, and
the hours come out of the guide. Yellow for the watch, and the hours come out of the sergeant or
out of a parish that pays him. Orange for the road taken out of the people walking it, once in four
hundred years, in a case with a cloth over it. White for the road to the dead, never made, and
every generation of guides has the same rule about it, which is: don't.*

*The guides' vow is older than mine and there are five lines and the Crown struck one out. We will
guide any ship that asks. Odo Pike says it is the greatest vow on this coast because there is
nothing in it for the man saying it, and I have been thinking about that all night, and he is
right, and I do not know what to do with it.*

*The plan is mine and it is this. Nine lights at the foot of the Fall on the ninth night, all lit,
all seen, and nine keepers holding one ring with no ends in it, which is Ede's trick and has never
been done by more than one person at a time. To do it we must take the five Embers out of the
Crown's five lit towers and douse the only lights on this coast, and Voss will sign the warrant and
be out of authority six days later, and Mr Vey will have to invent a heading.*

*Fourteen free crews came in because Isolde went round four harbours in a borrowed cutter and told
every one of them that they would be paid in nothing and would probably die. Twenty-three sail in
the roads, and not one light among them until the ninth night.*

*I said the vow in front of all of them and I got it right. I have been practising for a year and I
did not know I knew it.*

*Cost, today: five lanterns to be doused, a warrant that expires while it is being used, eleven
people with their wrists bare in front of a Crown that has a list of their names, and five cutters
going out at dawn with a letter in each asking five Crown keepers to put out the only light they
have and come and stand in the water at the end of the world.*

*The vote was nine for and fourteen abstaining and the motion failed, and then I read a poem at
them and twenty-two of twenty-three put their hands up. I do not know what to do with that. I have
been told all my life that a light is a thing you are given, and tonight twenty-two captains voted
for one because a girl with a notebook was honest about the odds. Mr Vey says the *Ivy* is a
sensible ship.*

*Four nights.*
