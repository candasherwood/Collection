# Chapter One

The lighthouse had been dark for eleven years, which was as long as Mira had been
asking why, and she had decided that this was not a coincidence.

Carrow did not have a law about the hill. It had something better: an understanding.
You did not go up. If you went up you did not say so, and if you said so you were not
answered. Eleven years of that had worn a path into the grass on the seaward side and
left the proper path, the one the keepers had used, to the rabbits.

She went up the rabbit one, at dusk, with the lantern hooked over her elbow the way her
grandmother had carried it in the photograph — elbow up, lantern hanging, as though the
light were something you carried for somebody else and did not want to spill.

She had not lit it. That was the whole point of the walk and most of the difficulty of
it. She had a coat, a lantern, a box of matches and a hand that wanted to do the obvious
thing, and she carried all four up a cliff path in the dark like a woman carrying a cup
that was too full.

Below her, Carrow went down to the harbour in tiers: fish sheds, net lofts, the row of
houses where she had been born, the mole with its three bollards and its one crane. Not
one window showing. The parish had put out its own lights in the week the Crown put out
the towers, and it had never once discussed putting them back, which is not the same as
agreeing, and everybody in Carrow knew it was not the same as agreeing.

The wind came off the water in long, flat sheets and pushed at her coat. Out past the
mole the sea worked at the rocks with the patience of something that had never once been
in a hurry.

She was eighteen. She had asked her question in every room in the parish and been given
one answer in a hundred different shapes, and the answer was: *that is how it is.*
The page in her pocket said otherwise, in a hand that pressed too hard, and there was
nobody left in Carrow who would tell her what it was for.

At the top, the tower stood exactly as it did in the picture: white paint gone the colour
of old teeth, the gallery rail rusted to lace, the glass at the top black as a closed
eye. Somebody kept the grass down around the door. That was the sort of thing nobody in
Carrow would admit to and several people did.

The door had no lock. It opened when she leaned on it, and the smell that came out was
rust and cold stone and, underneath, faintly, paraffin.

Mira stood in the doorway with the smell coming out past her and thought, clearly and
against her own instructions: *Somebody has been filling the lamp.*

She told herself she was being ridiculous. Nobody had been up here in eleven years. The
parish would have known. The parish knew everything except the things it had decided not
to.

Her grandmother's notebook was in her coat pocket, and page nineteen said, in a hand that
had never once been tidy: *When the light comes back on, count the ships that are not
there.*

She had read that line four hundred times and could not have told you what it meant. It
was not advice. It was a job, written the way her grandmother wrote everything, as though
the person reading it would already know the rest.

The photograph was folded into the paper pocket at the back of the notebook, and she did
not take it out. She knew the year of it and the story of it and the exact softness the
fold had gone, and she had learned, in eleven years, that there are things you can carry
without opening.

She settled the lantern on her elbow, because her hands wanted something to do. Then she
stepped inside and began to climb.
