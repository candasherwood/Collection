# Chapter Sixteen

A lamp is a lamp. A lantern is a lamp with an opinion, and it took the *Mercy Bell* two
days and one evening to find out which one they had bought.

They tested it at dusk on the second day out of Candlewick, because Isolde said that if
the thing was going to betray them she would rather it did it in water she could name.

The lens came up out of the hold in its frame, with the chains still on it, and took six
men and a block and tackle to get it onto the hatch grating. Seen on a deck in daylight
it was less like a mill wheel and more like a church window that had been left out in the
rain: a hundred rings of glass, grey-green, with a hole in the middle the size of a fist.

Mira put the Ember in the brass cup.

"Right," said Isolde. "Hood off."

She took the hood off.

The light did not go anywhere. That was the first surprise. It came up out of the lens
and stopped, and lay on the deck and in the rigging and on the faces of the crew, and
made the whole ship look like a room, and did not point.

"Ah," said Sallow, from the rail. "You've bought a bell and no clapper."

"It's a lens," said Mira.

"It's a *lens*," Sallow agreed. "Which is a very fine thing to have, if you happen to own
a tower."

Isolde looked at the light, and then at the water, and then at Mira, in the order that
meant somebody was about to be told to fix it. "Well?"

Mira put her hand up with the ring on it and tried to gather the light into a lane.

Nothing happened. Not the flicker-and-fail of a thing she was doing badly: nothing. The
ring was warm on her finger and the light went on lying around the ship like washing, and
she might as well have been holding a hand up at a sunset.

"It's a hand on a lever," she said, and heard how stupid it sounded. "That's what Ede
wrote. A ring is a hand on a lever. But there isn't a lever. There's just — light. It
isn't shaped."

"So shape it," said Isolde.

"I can hold a rope. I can hold a door. I can't hold *light*. There's nothing to get my
hands on."

Marrow had come up from the fore hatch with a coil over his shoulder and he stood and
looked at the lens for a while, which was how he did his thinking.

"Chimney," he said.

They built one in an hour and a half out of the spare water-butt hoops, blackened canvas
and tar, with Pell inside it on his back lacing wire through the eyelets and complaining
the whole time in a voice that carried. It was nine feet long and three feet across and it
looked like the funnel of a kettle that had been in a fire, and when they lashed it to the
front of the lens frame and lit the Ember the light went down it and came out the other
end in a lane.

A lane nine feet wide, going out over the water, steady as a held breath.

Nobody said anything for a moment. Then Pell, black to the elbows, said, "It's like a
road."

It was like a road. It lay on the water from the ship's side to the edge of seeing, hard
and yellow and flat, and where it touched the sea the sea went transparent, and under
three feet of water there was a road.

Not a suggestion of one. A road: with a wall along one side of it, and gateposts, and a
milestone with the top worn round, and ruts, two deep ruts where carts had gone. A road
that had been built by people who expected to use it for a hundred years, and had been
under the sea for four hundred, and was still there, and still going somewhere.

There were tears on Mira's face and she had no idea when they had started.

"Twelve," said Teo.

He was standing at the rail with his hands on it, and his voice had gone flat, which was
the voice he used when he was seeing something the rest of them were only being shown.

"Twelve what?" said Isolde.

"Ships." He did not point. He never pointed. "Along the road. Sitting on the bottom where
the moorings were. There's a big one at the second mile with her masts still up and
there's a light in her window."

"There's no —" Pell started, and stopped, because Teo had turned and looked at him and
Pell had learned what that look meant.

"There's a light in her window," said Teo. "It's been lit a long time."

Rook had his hand on his blade and did not know it.

Then the ship rolled.

It was not a big roll — a swell coming at them a little off the quarter, the kind that
wouldn't spill a mug in the cabin — but the frame was lashed to the hatch grating with
four turns of rope and the chains were still on it and the lens weighed as much as a
horse, and it shifted. Six inches. Enough that the mouth of the chimney no longer sat
over the middle of the glass, and the light came out of the side of it and went up.

It went up. It went out across the water and up into the dark, and it lit the whole ship
like a shop window, and it kept going, out and out and out, and the sea for a mile around
them turned the colour of a page.

"Get it *down*," said Isolde.

Mira put her hand up and took hold of the lens.

It was like taking hold of a cart by one wheel. She had it — she had it, she could feel
the shape of it in her hands, the rings and the weight and the awful tipping lean of it —
and it was so heavy that her knees went and she heard herself make a sound she had not
made since the tower. She held it for a count of eight and she could feel the light
taking something out of her, rifling through her the way a thief goes through a coat, and
she did not have a season to give tonight, she did not have a name to give tonight, and
the lens was still leaning.

Eight. Nine. She was going to lose it.

Then Teo put his hands in the light.

He did it the way a man puts his hands out to stop a door. He was three feet from the
lens and he did not touch it. He simply stood in the spill of it with his hands open, and
the light came into him at the chest and came out of his palms in a lane, and the lane
narrowed, and steadied, and the lens stopped leaning.

The weight went out of Mira's hands so fast she sat down.

Nobody spoke. The beam lay out over the water, nine feet wide, and Teo stood at the end
of it holding it the way you hold a rope, and there was no ring on him anywhere, and he
was not paying.

"That's not possible," said Rook.

"It's extremely possible," said Sallow, in a completely different voice from his usual
one. He had come away from the rail. "It's the whole reason there's a list."

"It is not —" Rook stopped.

"Say it, ser," said Sallow. "Third column. *Lens-capable.* You've carried the Roll since
you were nineteen and you've never once read the third column out loud."

Mira got up off the deck.

"What does he mean, the third column?"

Rook looked at Teo, who was standing in a lane of light with his hands open and his eyes
shut, and Rook's face did the thing it did when the order and the truth arrived at the
same door.

"A marked man can hold a light without a ring," he said. "It is in the Roll. It is why
they are assessed. It is —" He made himself say it properly. "The order calls it a mercy.
The word in the margin is *capable*. There is a note beside it that says a capable subject
may be *employed at a lens* in time of need, and it is signed by a Warden, and it does not
require the subject's consent, and there is no term in it. A keeper has a tower and a vow
and a ring and a name in the catechism. A capable subject has a wall."

The sea went on being lit.

"Until when?" said Mira.

"Until he stops," said Rook.

"Until he stops *what*?"

"Talking," said Rook. "Usually."

Isolde said, very quietly, something that Mira did not catch and that Marrow, who was
standing beside her, repeated later as *"Not on my ship."*

Teo had not moved. His eyes were still shut and the light was still going out of his
hands and his face was calm in a way that Mira did not like at all, because she had seen
that face on other people, in a lamp room, with their families downstairs.

"Teo," she said.

"I'm all right."

"Teo. Open your eyes."

He opened them. They were the same as ever, which was the worst of it.

"I can see the road," he said. "I can see all of it. Mira, it goes all the way to the
Kindling. It goes *under* the Kindling. There's a stair."

"Good," said Mira. "Keep talking."

"Why?"

"Because Rook says the ones who stop talking stop talking." Her voice came out steady and
she was proud of it. "Tell me true things. Anything. Just keep —"

"True things," said Teo.

"True things."

He laughed, once, without much in it. "I don't have a lot of practice."

"Then we'll both be bad at it," said Mira. "Start with your name."

"Teodor Vane."

"Is that your name?"

"It's the name the parish gave me. Vane's the commonest salt-name on the Reach. A hundred
boys got it the year the captain was hanged." The lane did not waver. "That's true. I've
never said it out loud sober."

"Your jaw."

"A door." The lane held. "In Saltmere. I was nine and I was faster than the man who owned
the door and not faster than the door."

"The rings."

"Which rings?"

"You've got seven on. You told Pell they were given to you."

The lane held. Teo's hands were perfectly steady in the light and his face had gone
careful, and Mira, who had spent her whole life watching people decide whether to tell her
the truth, saw him decide.

"They were given to me," he said.

The lane held.

Mira waited for the light to go out. She waited the way you wait for a slammed door, with
the whole of herself braced, and the lane went on lying across the water, nine feet wide
and perfectly steady, and nothing happened at all.

"All right," she said. "Then tell me this one. Are you going to leave?"

"No," said Teo.

The lane held. Nine feet wide, and steady, and going out over the water to a drowned road
with a stair under the Kindling.

Mira believed him. She believed him completely — you could see it in the light, the way
you could see a held rope; there was no doubt in it anywhere — and something in her chest
that had been braced since Carrow let go all at once.

"All right," she said, and her voice was not steady now. "All right."

"It's all right," said Teo. "I've done this before."

The lane went out.

Not dimmed, not weakened. Out, like a candle in a fist, and the light fell out of his
hands and hit the deck and rolled, and the lens came down with a shriek of chain and a
noise like a bell being dropped from a height, and the chimney of blackened canvas took
the blow and folded, and a ring of glass sheared off somewhere in the middle of the great
lens and dropped into the wreck of the canvas and a chip the size of a thumb came out of
the bottom of it and skidded across the deck and lay there, warm, glowing faintly, going
out.

Teo was on his knees.

His hands were cold. That was the thing he kept saying afterwards, in the way a man says
the only fact he has: his hands were cold, and he could not feel the ends of his fingers,
and when Nettle took them and turned them over there was nothing wrong with them at all.

Mira got the hood back on the Ember with her hands shaking and the deck went dark and the
crew stood in the dark and nobody said anything for a while.

Then Isolde said, "Right. Get that thing lashed down properly, and then everybody get
below and eat something, because we are about to have a very stupid evening."

---

They had it in the sail locker, because it was the only place on the ship with a door.

"You lied," said Mira.

"I told the truth about the important one."

"That's what makes it worse, Teo. You told the truth about the big one and you lied about
*'I've done this before'* — what was that? What was the point of that?"

"It was going well," said Teo. "You were crying. I wanted you to stop."

"So you lied."

"To make you feel better, yes."

"That's what a lie *is*," said Mira. "That's the whole thing that a lie is. It's not a
magic trick, it's somebody deciding you can't have the true version."

He was sitting on a coil of cable with his elbows on his knees. His hands were wrapped in
a bit of old blanket and he kept opening and closing them, checking, the way you check a
pocket for a key.

"I've never held anything for anybody," he said. "In my life. Not a door, not a rope, not
a — there was a man in Saltmere who used to pay me to hold his horse, and I was bad at
that. And you asked me if I was going to leave and I said no, and the light stayed up, and
I thought — *it's working, she's all right, tell her something kind* — and the one thing I
had was a sentence I've used on a hundred people in a hundred rooms and it has never once
been true and it has never once cost me anything."

"It cost you your sight," said Mira.

Teo did not answer for a moment.

"I can see the road," he said.

"How many ships are on it?"

"Eleven."

"There are thirteen," said Mira. "You said twelve on deck. Sallow counted, and Pell
counted, and I counted twice, because I'm the sort of person who counts twice."

Teo looked at the bulkhead.

"Then I can see twelve," he said, "and one of them is being difficult."

Mira sat down on the deck with her back against the door, which was a thing she did when
she was too tired to be angry standing up.

"You told me the rings were given to you," she said. "Just now. In the light. *They were
given to me*, and the lane went on going out over the water like nothing had happened. I
sat here for a while trying to work out how that can be, because a Holding is not a
lantern and it does not read what's true. It reads what you *are*. And then I got cold,
Teo, because I worked out what it means: you've told that one so often you don't doubt it
any more."

Teo looked at his hands in the blanket.

"One of them was given," he said.

"One."

"The one on the little finger. Sallow gave me that the night we came up. He said he'd
been paid twice for the same week and it made him feel dirty and he wanted to be rid of
something." He almost smiled. "It's a terrible ring. It's got a flaw in the stone."

"And the other six?"

"Stall in Saltmere. Two for a quarter-mark, if you buy the ones with flaws." He said it
flatly, without any performance in it. "I buy them because a man with rings looks like a
man things have been given to. And then I tell people they were given to me, and they
believe me, because it's a small thing and nobody checks a small thing. I've told it so
long I can feel the shape of it in my mouth. It isn't lying any more. It's a coat."

"That's worse," said Mira.

"Yes."

"A man like that can't be caught."

"No," said Teo. "He can be caught. He just has to care about the sentence, and I've
spent my whole life making sure I don't."

Mira got the notebook out.

Teo watched her do it and something in his shoulders went down, the way it did when she
wrote Pell's name in the watch bill, or Rook's vow.

"Are you writing me down as a liar?"

"I'm writing down what happened," said Mira. "That's different, and it's worse, and it's
true."

She wrote for a while. Then she turned the book round and showed him the page before it,
which was the page she had not shown anybody: the list, in her cramped hand, with the
dates beside it.

*Things that are gone.*

*The tower — the whole of it before the dousing. I know I climbed it. I have the coat.*
*Grandmother's face. Gone west.*
*The winter I was twelve. Gone in the Gull's Teeth.*
*The smell of the kitchen. Gone at Candlewick. I know it was mine because I wrote it down.*

"I lied to Beck," she said. "He asked me what it cost and I said I didn't know yet, and I
knew, I knew before he finished the question. I said it because if I said it out loud in
that room, in front of his boy, then it would be a thing that had happened in a tavern,
and I wanted it to be a thing that happened to me on a ship. So don't sit there and look
like the only liar in the room. There's two of us and we're both bad at it and mine is
worse, because mine goes in a notebook and looks like evidence."

Teo read the page twice. He was careful with it in a way he was careful with nothing else.

"The smell of the kitchen," he said.

"Bread and onions and hot fat. I can tell you exactly what it smelled like. I could write
you a page of it. I just can't —" She stopped. Started again. "There's nothing at the end
of the sentence. It's all words."

"Then I'll smell it for you," said Teo.

"That's not —"

"I know it isn't." He touched the rail, which was a bulkhead, and then took his hand away
and looked at it as though the habit had betrayed him. "I'm not good at this. I'm going to
say a thing and it's going to be the wrong size. But you asked me to keep talking and I
haven't stopped, and the reason I haven't stopped is that you were standing in front of a
lens with your hands up and I have never in my life wanted to be somewhere as much as I
wanted to be between you and that thing." He shrugged. "So. That's the true version. It's
worse than the lie. You were right about that too."

Mira closed the notebook.

"Next time," she said, "lie to me about something that doesn't matter. Whether you've
eaten. Whether the coat's warm enough. Give me one true thing where it counts and you can
tell me the sea's made of wine for all I care."

"That's a bad arrangement."

"It's the only one I've got," said Mira. "I'm building my whole life out of paper, Teo. I
need one page I don't have to check."

---

They came up on deck at the end of the middle watch.

The lens was lashed down with eight turns now and the chimney was propped with a broom
handle, and Isolde had the hood on the Ember and the ship dark and the jury rig drawing
like a good conscience.

Mira lifted the hood for a count of ten, because a thing you have dropped once has to be
looked at again or you will think about it all night.

The road came up gold under the water, the wall and the gateposts and the deep twin ruts.
And beside it, a hand's width to the left, a second road: the same wall, the same
milestone, the same going-somewhere, very slightly out of step.

"There's two," said Pell.

"There's one," said Sallow. "There's one road and one of it is being *said* twice. The
theatre has a word for it. It isn't a good word."

Mira put the hood down.

"Sixty miles of drowned road," said Isolde, when Mira came aft with the notebook in her
hand. "Under three feet of water.
No tower, a canvas chimney, and a glass with a crack in it. And before you say it, I know
the other thing too. We lit a candle on the table and everyone in the room saw it. The
Throne will have it by morning and the Ash does not need to be told twice."

"Eight days to the Kindling," said Mira.

"Eight days," said Isolde, "if the wind stays and the glass stays whole and nobody on this
ship does anything brave."

Behind them, Teo had gone to the bow and was standing with his hands on the rail, looking
out at water that had nothing on it at all.

Mira took out the notebook and wrote, in the dark, by feel, on the back page.

*He says he can see twelve ships. There are thirteen.*

*He is lying. It is not small. It is the only thing he has that we need, and he has given
it to a lens to keep me from crying.*

*We are going to take the Drowned Road anyway, and I am going to have to be his eyes, and
I do not know how to do that, and the alternative is that woman with the white gloves
relights the whole coast in her own hand.*

*And the lens shows the road twice now. One of them is true and one of them is the lens
being clever, and there is nobody aboard who can tell me which, because the man who could
is standing at the bow pretending the water is empty.*

*So: one true page. I have one left in me and I am going to spend it on him, and if that
is a fool's errand then everything I do is a fool's errand, and it has worked so far.*
