# Chapter Eighteen

The stone at Barrowfoot stands three feet out of the water with its top worn round, and
past it the road stops being a road and becomes a bay. The town lies four hundred yards
off along the shore, at the foot of the Seventh's headland: a stone quay, forty chimneys,
a tower up on the head with a bell in it. At dawn there was no boat out of it and no
smoke from any of the forty chimneys and no gull on the water, and the tide went on going
out as if none of it were any of its business.

Six boats came to meet them, from three different points on the shore, which is how you
know a thing has been arranged. Four had oars out and two had a lantern on a pole and men
sitting with their hands on their knees, and the *Tongue* sat in the middle of the bay
with the chimney smoking gently and six people in her who had not slept properly in two
nights.

"Don't row," said Rook.

"I'm not rowing," said Sallow. "I'm resting. There's a difference and it's billable."

The lieutenant came up on the port bow with a boat-hook and made them fast without being
asked. It was the same lieutenant as before, with the same list, and this time there was a
marine behind him with a saltlock and no hat, which is the difference between an
invitation and a receipt.

"Miss Calloway. Ser Rook." He looked at the boat, and counted, and looked at his list.
"Six."

"There are six of us," said Mira.

"Yes," said the lieutenant. "That is what I have."

He read the names off without looking up. Pell — no parish. Marrow — no parish, no next
of kin. Sallow. Vane. Rook. Calloway. He read *Vane* the way a man reads a word he has read
before and does not like, and Marrow, who had not been asked anything, did not answer to
his own name.

"Captain Sable is not here," said the lieutenant.

"The captain is three days round the outside of the Cradle," said Mira.

"Then the Admiral will be disappointed." He folded the list. "The Admiral has a copy of
your captain's log entry. *If not returned, I will come and get them and I will be
extremely unpleasant about it.* She had it copied out and read it at breakfast. She said
she would like to meet her."

"Everybody says that," said Sallow, "and then they do."

---

The *Vigil* had not changed in five days. Beeswax, holystoned decks, not one lantern
anywhere, and the whole enormous black length of her lying quiet at her anchor as though
she were trying not to be heard. The bell forward struck the half hour while they came up
the side and the strike was muffled, deadened, a bell with a cloth in its mouth.

They had a lieutenant at the gangway and a file of marines and a clerk, and Mr Vey was
waiting on the quarterdeck with his box and a form already out and his pen uncapped, which
is the most alarming thing a pen can do.

"A lens," said Vey, "of the Fourth, removed from Candlewick without licence, damaged in
transit, and carried sixty miles through Crown water in an open boat. I have never entered
a sentence like that in my life and I find I am looking forward to it."

"It's damaged because a man lied in it," said Mira.

Vey wrote that down. He did it left-handed, without looking at the page, while he was
saying, "I shall put that in the remarks. In nine years the remarks have never once been
the interesting part." He signed it, sanded it, and held the leaf out to her. "Your
receipt. The lens is evidence. It goes into the hold with a guard on it and it comes out
when a board says so."

"And if I say no?"

"Then it goes into the hold anyway, and I enter that you objected, and it makes a very
interesting page." He looked at her for the first time. "You are not going to fight six
marines over a piece of broken glass in a boat with a boy in it. I know that because I
have assessed you twice, and you are not stupid."

Mira took the receipt. Her hands were steady and her voice had gone very polite, which was
the only thing on her that had.

He looked at the Ember, in its hood, in her hand.

"One coal," he said. "Keeper's. Unassessed."

"Not in the box?"

"The form has no line for it," said Vey.

"That's not the reason."

"No," he said. "The reason is that the last time a coal of will was entered in a book on
this coast, six hundred men stopped rowing, and I have not yet decided what to do about it,
and I am not going to decide it on a quarterdeck before breakfast." He capped the pen.
"The lens has a line. The coal does not. When I find a form that fits it, Miss Calloway,
you will be the first to know."

They took Rook's blade at the top of the gangway. He gave it over hilt first, both hands,
and the marine who took it did not know what to do with a weapon that was warm.

"It will not answer you," said Rook. "That is not a discourtesy. It lights when I mean it
and it will not light for anybody else."

"Ser," said the marine.

"You are not under arrest, ser," said the lieutenant.

"I am aware," said Rook. "I am being *received*."

He did not salute. The lieutenant noticed, and Rook saw him notice, and said, "I salute
people I am about to arrest," and went down the ladder without being told where to go,
which the lieutenant also noticed.

Marrow came last, and the water ran off him on the holystoned deck in a way that made two
of the marines take a step back without deciding to, and Pell went sideways along the rail
counting the gunports out loud because he was sixteen and frightened and had been told not
to be sick.

There were brackets on the *Vigil*. Forty of them, brass, polished, empty, spaced along
the rails and up the masts — a ship built to carry lights, carrying none. Mira counted
them twice.

And aft, high up, in the middle of all that black and varnish, there was one window with a
light in it and a shape in the window that was not moving, exactly as it had been the first
time she saw this ship, from a rowing boat, going home.

---

They were kept waiting in a cabin for the better part of an hour, which Isolde called the
oldest move in the book and the most effective. This time there was no tray. This time
there was a marine outside the door and the door had a bar on it on the outside, and Pell
sat on the floor with his arms round his knees and Sallow lay full length on the bench and
went to sleep in about ninety seconds, which nobody had asked him to do and which was
somehow the most frightening thing in the room.

"They've given me a cabin," he said, at some point, without opening his eyes. "With a
desk. On a ship. What does a man do with a desk on a ship, I ask myself. He sits at it. He
writes. He has a whole profession about it." And then he was asleep again.

At the end of the hour the marine opened the door and Admiral Ilse Voss came in, and she
had not changed either: grey, plain collar, white gloves, and a face that had been doing
arithmetic while the rest of them were at sea.

"Miss Calloway. Ser Rook." She looked at the bench. "Mr Sallow."

"Admiral," said Sallow, without sitting up.

"Captain Sable is four days behind you. I have your log entry. I had it copied." She sat
down at the table and put her hands on it, gloves and all, the way she had put them on the
chart. "I am going to tell you three things and then I am going to ask you one. You may
interrupt, but it will make the arithmetic slower."

"Three things," said Mira.

"One. The fleet came round the Cradle in three days and one hour. I have nine lantern-keeps
to do and eleven days to do them in and I have spent five of those days waiting for you,
which is a thing I do not do, and which I did because I am not certain I can do this
without you.

"Two. Last night I sent sixty men ashore at the Seventh under a captain of marines. They
were to open the tower, hold it, and light it if it could be lit, and I was to bring you in
at dawn to do the lighting in front of the town. Four of them came back. In a boat built
for twelve. They are not wounded. The surgeon says they are not shocked either. The surgeon
says they are *waiting*." She said the word without any expression at all. "I have not
spoken to them. I have been at sea for thirty years and I do not know what to say to a man
who has walked down a hill and cannot tell me why he is doing it."

The cabin was quiet. Pell had stopped breathing audibly.

"Three," said Voss. "There is a licence on this table. It is made out to Calloway, Mira, of
Carrow, keeper of the Ninth — the first licensed keeper of the Ninth in the history of the
Reach. Sixty salt a quarter, a command of your own, the fleet's protection for every keeper
who joins you, and a stipend for the Wren family at the Eighth, who are Hollowed and will
not notice it and will go on trimming the wick. I am not going to pretend that last part is
a kindness. It is a line in a budget, and it is the only line I can put there."

She turned the sheet round. Beside it was another, smaller, with the seal already on it and
the date left blank.

"And there is a warrant on this table," she said, "for a marked man, name of Vane, Teodor,
of no parish. It is legal, it is signed, and the date is the only thing missing from it.
The date is at my discretion. It becomes this morning at my discretion."

Mira looked at the two sheets. She had a hole in the back of her head where sleep should
have been and her hands still smelled of tar and the light of the lens was still printed on
the inside of her eyes, and she looked at two pieces of paper and understood that she had
been outmanoeuvred in a way that had taken five days and a fleet.

"You said you'd spend me like salt," she said.

"I did."

"Then spend me. Don't buy me."

"That is not how arithmetic works," said Voss.

"It's how light works."

Voss was quiet for a moment. "Yes," she said at last. "That is the trouble with you. You
keep saying things that are true and useless, and I keep writing them down." She took the
warrant and put it in her sleeve. "The date stays missing until dawn. At dawn you light the
Seventh or you do not."

"And the form?"

"At dawn," said Voss, "there will be a town watching, and a fleet, and sixty men who are
*waiting*, and after that we will talk about the form."

She stood. At the door she stopped, with her hand on the bar, and did not turn round.

"Before you decide," she said, "I want you to do something. It is not in the terms. It will
not be written down. It will take an hour of your time and it will cost you nothing and I
am asking you as —" She stopped. Started again. "I am asking."

---

The aft cabin was smaller than the one they had been kept in, and it was the only warm
room on the ship. There was a chair, a table bolted down, a bunk with the blanket folded,
a chessboard, and a window with a lamp on the sill, and the lamp was lit and turned up and
had been trimmed so well that the flame had no colour at all.

A man sat in the chair with his hands on his knees. Fifty-one, in a keeper's coat, brushed,
brass buttons, a collar without a mark on it. He looked like the Admiral about the mouth.

He did not look up when they came in.

"Konrad Voss," said the Admiral. "Which one?"

"The little one," said her brother, and she moved the piece for him.

Mira did not know which one he meant. Voss did.

It took a quarter of an hour. Voss played her own side out loud — "the bishop takes the
pawn, and that is a mistake, and I know it is a mistake" — and for her brother's side she
did nothing at all except ask, at every turn, in exactly the same voice, *Konrad Voss,
which one?* And he would say *the little one*, or *the tower*, or *the horse*, or once, at
the end, *the one by the wall*, and she would move it, and he would take her queen, and she
would say, "There. Again. He takes the queen and I have never once seen him hesitate."

"Does he win?" said Mira.

"He has won every game since Brinehollow," said Voss. "Eleven years. I have lost, I
calculate, some four thousand times."

"You move his pieces," said Mira. "You could win."

"Yes," said Voss, and set the last piece in the box.

"Why do you play?"

"Because he is very good at it," said Voss, "and because for one hour a day I can pretend
that what is happening is a game."

She set the pieces back in the box, one by one, taking her time.

"He was the keeper at Brinehollow," she said. "The Second. When the Ash came up the beach
he went down to the water and pulled the harbour master's girl out and put her in my arms
and went back for the boy. There was no boy. There was never a boy, there was nobody left
on that beach but us, and he went back anyway, and the second time he came out of the water
he was walking, and he said my name, and then he never said anything again that he had not
been asked."

"He said your name," said Mira.

"He said it twice." Voss closed the box. "The Ash does not kill people. I would have
preferred it if it killed people."

Mira looked at the man in the chair. The coat was a keeper's coat, and the hands were a
keeper's hands, and the lamp on the sill was lit and trimmed, and the wick was a stub
because it had been trimmed every night for eleven years.

"Konrad Voss," said Mira. "Are you warm?"

"Yes."

She looked at Voss. Voss looked at the window.

"He is not warm," she said. "He is never warm. I have had the surgeon look at him. But you
cannot ask a man if he is warm and get the truth out of him. You can only ask, and he will
answer, and that is the entire remainder of what is left."

"Ask him something he can't answer."

"There is nothing he cannot answer," said Voss. "That is the whole of it. He has never
failed a question in eleven years. He has beaten every physician, every cleric and one
bishop. Ask him the name of the tide, ask him the year, ask him the ninth proposition of
the catechism, ask him my mother's name." She straightened the box on the table. "Ask him
to ask you something."

Mira did not.

"Go on," said Voss.

"Konrad Voss," said Mira. "Ask me something."

The lamp burned. The ship worked, quietly, the way a ship does at anchor. Somewhere aft a
bell was struck and the strike was muffled. The man in the chair sat with his hands on his
knees and his eyes on the window, and he did not move, and he did not move, and he did not
move.

Then his hand came up and found the wick and turned it a quarter turn down, the way a
keeper does when the flame has got above itself, and the light in the room steadied, and he
put his hand back on his knee.

Mira waited a full minute. She counted it.

"Nothing," she said.

"Nothing," Voss agreed. "He will answer anything. He cannot begin."

She came round the table and stood in front of her brother, and she said, "Konrad Voss. Say
my name."

"Ilse Voss."

"Thank you," said the Admiral.

And Mira understood, standing in a warm room on a black ship with a light in the window,
that the woman said that every night, and that she had been saying it every night for
eleven years, and that she kept it for the end of the day the way some people keep a drink.

"You asked me to look at him," said Mira.

"I asked you to tell me what you see."

"I don't see anything." She said it as plainly as she could, because there was no kind way
to say it and she had been raised not to try. "There's nobody in the room. There's a chair
and a coat and a good chess player and a hand that trims a wick. He's not hiding and he's
not ill and he's not waiting to come back. He's a room somebody's left."

Voss did not move for a moment. Then she pulled her glove straight, which was the only
thing she did with her hands when she was not doing arithmetic.

"Yes," she said. "Thank you." She picked up the lamp and set it back on the sill. "You are
the first person in eleven years who has told me the truth about him without being asked to
be kind. I am going to remember that when this is over and you are in front of a board."

"That's not a kindness either."

"No," said Voss. "It is the only kind of gratitude I have."

At the door, with her hand on the bar and the light behind her, she said, without turning:

"Put it out and you tell me what is left of him."

"What?"

"The window." She was a black shape in front of a yellow square. "I know what it is. There
is one light on the flagship of the Salt Throne, nine miles out on the edge of the Ash, in a
bay where sixty men have gone up a hill and four have come down. I have known what it is for
eleven years. My clerks know. My captains know. Nobody has said it out loud to me once,
because I am the woman who does the arithmetic." She opened the door. "If I put it out he
sits in the dark until morning. He does not sleep. He does not call out. He sits in a chair
in the dark like a coat on a hook, and in the morning I light it again and he trims the wick,
and I tell myself that is a man doing his job."

She went out.

---

Vey came to the cabin in the dog watches, with the box and a candle and no clerk, which was
in itself a kind of confidence.

"A correction to the Roll," he said. "I need a witness who has seen the man. You are the
only person aboard who has."

He opened the duplicate on the table. It was a great ugly thing, three hundred pages, in
three hands, with a column for everything — name, parish, keeper, class, and one at the
back called DISPOSITION that was wider than the others.

He found the page. He turned it round.

> *Vane, Teodor. Parish: none. Keeper: none. Class: glass-eyed, unassessed.*
>
> *Disposition: recovered.*
>
> *Recovered at the Saltmere pier, aged six years. Lugger Anselm, lost with all hands off
> the Sill, found afloat nine days later with one child aboard and no boat's crew.*

Mira read it twice. Then she read the small line underneath, which was in a different ink
and much worse handwriting: *no assessment applied for.*

"In the Roll," said Vey, "a person is lost, or assessed, or employed, or disposed, or
recovered. Recovered means the Crown has them back. It is the word we use for cargo and for
bodies." He turned the page back round and did not close the book. "I did not write that
entry."

"Then who did?"

"That is the question I have been asking for nine years." He put his pen down on the page,
parallel to the edge of the table, which was the tidiest thing Mira had ever seen a man do
with his hands. "My box goes to Saltmere on the first boat and comes back on the second,
written. Every week. Names, parishes, keepers, dispositions, in a hand I do not know. I have
never met the man who fills me in. He has never once been wrong. He has entered things that
happened nineteen years ago and things that happened last Thursday, and he has never been
wrong once, and I am the copy of a book that is being written by somebody I have never met."

He said it in exactly the voice he used for reading out a receipt.

"Somebody's been keeping the count," said Mira.

"Yes. I have been saying that sentence to myself since the quay at Carrow, and it is the
most frightening sentence I have ever said out loud, and I have just said it to a girl in a
cabin because there is nobody else on this ship I can say it to." He looked at her. "You
asked me at Carrow why I gave you the receipt. Now you know. I have been deciding things
about other people's light for eleven years on the word of a man I have never met."

"What does recovered mean for him?"

"It means the Crown has him back."

"He was six years old and alone on a pier."

"Yes," said Vey. "And now he is nineteen and alone on a ship, and the Roll says recovered,
and I am obliged to act on it." He laid one finger on the entry. "I can enter a correction.
*In service, licensed household, pending assessment.* It is a lie that stays true for as
long as nobody looks at it. That is the best I do, Miss Calloway, and I have never once
offered it to anybody. I am offering it for a signature."

Mira looked at the page. The lamp on the table was guttering and his shadow was on the wall
and the ship worked quietly around them.

"Sign the licence," said Vey, "and at dawn you light the Seventh as a keeper in order, and
your navigator is a rating, and a rating is not recovered. A rating is *had*. Nobody
reclaims a man who is already on the books."

"And if I don't sign?"

"Then at dawn I serve the warrant," said Vey, "and the boy goes to Saltmere to be assessed,
and they will find him lens-capable, and he will be employed at a lens, and you know what
that means now. You have seen the price of it on a deck."

"Yes," said Mira. "I have."

"Then we understand each other."

"We understand each other," said Mira, and did not sign, and Vey closed the box and went
out with his candle and his manners, and left the duplicate of the Glass Roll open on the
table with her name not in it and Teo's name in a hand nobody could account for.

---

Teo was at the rail on the fo'c'sle in the last of the afternoon, with his coat round him
and his hand on the wood.

"Recovered," he said, before she said anything. "Somebody told the boy who brings the
bread. Then the boy told Pell. Then Pell told me, in the way Pell tells you things, which is
at volume, from a distance, with his hand over his mouth."

"I'm sorry."

"Don't be. It's the first time in nineteen years anybody has had me on a page." He said it
lightly and then he stopped saying it lightly. "That's the thing that's got me. I've been
going round this coast since I was tall enough to hold a rope, telling people I'm nobody
from nowhere, and it turns out somebody's had a page with me on it since before I could
read. Somebody knew where I was the whole time."

"Does that help?"

"No." He rubbed his thumb along the rail. "Yes. A bit. Isn't that stupid."

"It's not stupid."

"It's a list, Mira. The same list. It's the one that's got Rook's third column in it and
the word *employed* in it and a place at the back for what they decided to do with you."
He looked at the *Vigil*'s decks, the marines, the empty brass brackets. "I'd rather be on
your page than theirs."

Mira got the notebook out of the inside of her coat and opened it at the back, where the
paper was still clean, and wrote, with the pencil from behind her ear, while he watched:

*Vane, Teodor. Parish: none. Keeper: Mira Calloway, of the Ninth.*
*Disposition: staying.*

Teo read it upside down the way he always did.

"That's not how a roll works," he said.

"It's how mine works."

He looked at it for a while. Then he said, "You wrote *staying*."

"I did."

"I haven't said I'm staying."

"You don't have to say it," said Mira. "I'm the one who writes things down."

---

At dusk the Admiral had the lens brought up on deck.

She had it done by eight men and a tackle, and Mr Vey stood at the rail with his book and
entered it as a loan, and Mira stood by the frame with the hood in her hand and the Ember
in its brass cup and the whole grey evening going violet out beyond the headland, and
nobody on the *Vigil* said a word, which was worse than being watched.

"Sixty men went up that hill," said Voss. "I am not sending another boat until I have seen
what is on the road."

"It'll show you the road as it was," said Mira.

"I am aware of what it will show me," said Voss. "Show me anyway."

She took the hood off.

The light came out of the chipped lens low and wide and lay across the bay in two lanes
that parted at the bow and were a boat's width apart at a hundred yards, and the water went
transparent, and under three feet of it the old town came up out of the sea.

Not the town that was standing on the shore. The one under it. The old Barrowfoot: a wall,
a gate with the doors open, a quay of dressed stone, a street of houses with their roofs
still on, and the road going up from the shore to the tower in eleven switchbacks, cut
stone, with the ruts of carts in it.

The *Vigil*'s crew made a sound. Nine hundred men, and the sound went along the deck the
way a wind does.

And then Mira saw the holes.

They were small and upright and they were all over the road, and there were fifty-six of
them, and they were the size and shape of people, and the light went round them the way
water goes round a stone.

Voss counted them. Mira watched her do it, twice, the way she counted everything, and then
stop counting.

"What is that?" said Pell. "What's the — why is it full of holes?"

"Because the light shows what it remembers," said Mira, "and it doesn't remember them."

Nobody asked her what she meant. She was glad of that. She was not certain she could have
said it twice.

The holes stood in the street and on the quay and along the eleven switchbacks of the road
up to the tower, and the tower itself stood up gold like a lamp with the door open at the
bottom of it, and there was a long shape lying across the doorway that was the right size to
be a man and did not move.

Then one of the holes turned.

It was slow, and it was not the way a person turns. It came round by degrees, the way a
weathervane comes round when the wind is deciding, and it faced the sea, and it faced the
bay, and it faced the light.

And then the one beside it turned.

"Hood it," said Mira.

Pell got the hood on it in four seconds and the gold went out of the world and left the
evening grey and the deck dark and nine hundred men standing still.

"They saw us," said Rook.

"They saw the light," said Sallow. "There's a difference. It's not a large difference, but
I've been paid for it before."

Mira stood with her hand on the hood rope and her heart going like a bird in a box, and
looked up at the ship. The decks were dark. The masts were dark. The brass brackets were
empty. There was one light on the *Vigil*, high up aft, in a square window, and a figure
standing in it that had not moved all day.

"There is still a light on this ship," said Rook.

Voss did not turn round. She was looking out at the town, at the shore, at the hill.

"Yes," she said.

---

Mira wrote it up in the cabin with the door barred and the marines outside it, by the light
of a candle she had to look at twice because she could not smell it.

*The Admiral has a brother. Konrad Voss, fifty-one, keeper of Brinehollow when it went
down. He sits in a chair aft with a chessboard and a window and he beats her four thousand
times a year, because she asks him which piece and he tells her, and she moves it. Ask him
anything and he answers. Ask him to ask you something and he trims the wick instead.*

*She keeps the window lit. One light on the flagship, nine miles out, on this coast, and she
knows exactly what it is. I have been in the dark for eleven years and I have never seen a
light I wanted to put out until tonight, and I could not do it, and I am not sure that was
mercy.*

*Mr Vey has a copy of the Roll and it is being written by somebody he has never met. Teo has
been on that page since he was six years old, taken off a lugger that went down with all
hands, and the disposition beside his name is RECOVERED, in a hand that is not Vey's, and
Vey cannot strike it and cannot account for it and is frightened, which I did not expect to
see and will not see again.*

*Recovered. It is what they write for cargo and for bodies.*

*So I have written him in mine, at the back, in pencil, in my own hand, and I have put myself
down as his keeper, and it is the first thing I have ever put in that book that the light
did not take from me.*

*They want a signature. Voss wants one at dawn and Vey wants one tonight and they are the
same signature, and if I put it down then Teo is a rating and the light is ours, which means
it is hers, and the war is over and the arithmetic wins.*

*I am not going to sign it. I am going to light the Seventh anyway, at dawn, in front of the
town, with all their papers in my pocket and no licence on the coast with my name on it, and
let them write that down.*

*The town is full of holes. Sixty men went up that hill and four came down and would not
say anything at all, and the holes in the street turned round to look at the light.*

*There is one light on this ship and it is in a window with a man standing in it.*
