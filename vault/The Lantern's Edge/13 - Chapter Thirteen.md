# Chapter Thirteen

The *Vigil* did not smell like a ship. That was the first thing.

Every ship Mira had ever been on smelled of tar and rope and something older underneath.
The *Vigil* smelled of beeswax. Her decks were holystoned to the colour of a tabletop,
her rails were varnished, and there was not one lantern anywhere on her — not in the
rigging, not at the masthead, not a candle in a holder — so that the whole enormous
thing was a black shape with clean floors.

They came up the side in the Admiral's own boat, and there was a lieutenant at the top
with a list, and he said, "Captain Sable. Ser Rook. Miss Calloway," and did not offer
to shake hands, and did not look at Mira's ring.

"Two of your people," said Isolde, pleasantly. "There's three of us."

"The Admiral's instruction was two of your people," said the lieutenant. "She did not
say how many of hers."

---

They were kept waiting in a cabin for the better part of an hour, which Isolde said
was the oldest move in the book and also the most effective.

Mira sat with her hands in her lap and listened to the ship. Nine hundred men and it
was quieter than the *Mercy Bell* at anchor. Somewhere above them a bell was struck
the hours, and the strike was deadened, muffled, as though the ship were trying not to
be heard.

At the end of the hour a servant came through with a tray: two cups, a pot, a covered
dish. He went through the far door and shut it, and Mira heard, muffled, a voice in
the next room — a woman's, patient, asking a question twice — and then nothing.

"There's a chessboard in there," said Isolde. "You can hear the pieces."

Mira listened. She could not hear the pieces.

Isolde took her hat off, put it on the bench beside her and left it there, which Mira
would learn was how the captain marked a room she intended to be difficult in.

"Rules for the next hour," said Isolde. "One. She will offer you something before she
asks you for anything. Let her finish the offer."

"Two," said Rook, from the window, without turning round. "She will be correct about
something. That is her weapon. She will be correct about it early, so that you agree
with her, and then she will use your agreement as a floor to stand on."

"Thank you, Ser Rook. Three, then," said Isolde. "When she asks what the light is, do
not say it's yours."

"It is mine."

"It is a lamp in a tower that your grandmother's family kept for four hundred years,
and the Crown has a receipt for it in a drawer. If you say it's yours, you're a girl
with a stolen lamp. If you say it's the coast's, she has to explain what she's doing
taking it off the coast." Isolde settled back. "Four. Whatever she offers, say you'll
think about it."

"I won't."

"Then say it anyway and don't think about it. That isn't a lie, it's timing."

---

Admiral Ilse Voss received them in a cabin the size of the *Mercy Bell*'s hold, with
windows across the stern and a chart table bolted down and nothing on the walls at all.
She was fifty-four, tall, in grey with a plain collar, and she was wearing white gloves
indoors.

"Miss Calloway." She did not offer a hand. "Captain Sable. Ser Rook."

"Admiral."

"Ilse Voss," said the Admiral, to herself, as though completing a list. "Sit down. Tea
is coming. I don't drink it, but I have found that people say more with a cup in their
hands."

They sat. The tea came. Mira did not touch hers.

"Three days ago," said Voss, "a sound crossed my ship. Six hundred and forty men heard
it. My sailing master — who has been at sea for thirty-one years and has not cried
since he was nine — put his hands over his face and stood there. My surgeon went below
and counted the wounded twice. My own lieutenant asked me, on the deck, in front of the
watch, whether he had been a good man." She turned her glass-sight over on the desk,
face down, and left it there. "What did I hear, Miss Calloway?"

"The truth," said Mira. "In your own voice."

"Whose truth?"

"Yours."

Voss was quiet for a moment. "Then it was wrong," she said.

---

Here is what the Admiral wanted, and she was patient enough to lay it out in order,
like a chart.

"Eleven years ago," she said, "the Ash came ashore at Brinehollow. Do you know what
that means? It means a town. It means four hundred people who had done nothing except
live on the wrong stretch of coast, and by the time the sun came up there were four
hundred people still walking about, still mending nets, still going to the well, and
none of them were there any more." She let that sit. "I was twenty miles away with a
squadron and I could see the lights go out along the coast, one by one, the way you'd
see a house go dark when the family leaves.

"We put out the lanterns because a lit tower is a signpost. The Ash follows light. I
have watched it follow light for eleven years and I have never once seen it follow
dark."

"You put out the safe roads," said Mira. "The drowned road. The shipping."

"Yes."

"People died in the dark. Not Hollowed — dead. Boats on the rocks."

"Yes. Four hundred and eleven the first winter; the number is in my desk and I know it
better than I know my own age." Voss's voice did not change. "I chose the smaller
number, and I would choose it again, and I will not pretend it was noble. It was
arithmetic, and I am the woman who does it, and when the war is over they will hang me
or they will put me on a coin, and I genuinely do not know which."

"So why am I here?"

"Because arithmetic has stopped working," said Voss, "and you are the first thing in
eleven years that has."

---

She showed them the plan, because she was that kind of officer.

It was on the chart table, weighted at the corners: the Reach in outline, the nine
lanterns marked with small brass pins, and a line of red thread running through them
from the Kindling to the Fall.

"One lit lantern is a signpost," said Voss. "Nine lit lanterns, lit in sequence, inside
eleven days, with a fleet in the water and the Emberwatch holding the keeps, is a
wall." She moved a pin. "The road comes back. The coast gets its shipping, its salt,
its post, its dead. And the Ash meets a line of light it cannot cross, instead of
walking in through the gaps.

"I can take six of those keeps with a writ and a company of marines. The outer three I
cannot, because there is nobody left alive up there who will answer a writ. What I need
is a keeper who can light a cold lantern quickly, in front of people, without a wick
and without eleven years of preparation." She looked at Mira. "You did it once, at
Carrow, at eighteen, in the dark, alone."

"I used matches," said Mira.

"You used will. The matches were a formality." Voss put both gloved hands flat on the
chart. "I am offering you a licence, a stipend, a command of your own, and the fleet's
protection for every keeper who joins you. You would be the first licensed keeper of
the Ninth in the Reach's history. You would be in order."

"And the light would be mine until you told me to put it out."

"The light would be *ours*."

"That's the same sentence with better manners."

Rook stirred. "Admiral," he said, "the light cannot be taken. It is given, or it is not
lit. The order's own rule—"

"The order's own rule is being audited by my clerks as we speak, Ser Rook," said Voss,
without heat. "Your Warden has been deferring assessments in his own hand for eleven
years, keeping a woman burning on a rock for the good of the coast. I have read the
file. I am not going to hang him for it. I am going to make it *legal*, which is
kinder, and which he will accept, because the alternative is a visitation."

---

There was one thing she did not say in front of the others, and she said it on the
stern walk with the wind coming over the rail and the sea going black below them.

She had taken off one glove to hold the rail, and Mira saw the hand: the back of it
scarred shiny from knuckles to wrist, old, and the fingertips flat and pale as candle
wax.

"Brinehollow," said Voss. "My brother was the keeper there. I pulled him out of the
water with that hand while the Ash came up the beach behind us, and he came out
walking, and he said my name, and then he said it again, and then he never said
anything else again." She put the glove back on. "He is fifty-one years old and he
lives in a room aft of my own deck with a window and a chair and a chessboard, because
I could not put him in a house where I would have to visit him. He plays chess.
Did you know that? Of course you didn't. He plays chess because I move the pieces and
he moves the pieces I move. Two of us. It is a beautiful game. I lose constantly."

Mira did not say anything. There was nothing to say that was not an insult.

"The Eighth's keeper was Alys Wren," said Voss. "Her family are Hollowed and still at
their posts, and I have had a writ sitting on my desk for four years with her name on
it that I could not make myself sign, because I kept thinking —" she stopped. Turned
her face into the wind. "Because a woman on fire in a dark tower is the best argument
for hope I have ever seen, and I did not want to be the one who put it in a file. So I
left her there. Eleven years." Her mouth did something that was not a smile. "You
spent a night in that tower and did more for her than I did in eleven."

"She died at the turn of the tide," said Mira.

"I know. My clerk had it in the log by morning." Voss turned around. "When you lit
Carrow, the Ash turned west. Do you understand what I am telling you? It turned. It has
never turned before. Every month of the last eleven years I have been losing ground,
and in one night you made it *notice* something, and then three days ago you made six
hundred men stop rowing."

"So you want my sound."

"I want the thing that made it," said Voss. "I don't care what it costs you. That's the
honest version, and you should hear it from me rather than from a form. I would spend
you like salt if it bought the coast a year. I have spent better people than you."

"And if I say no?"

"Then I take the Ninth off you and put a licensed keeper in it," said Voss, "and I light
the other eight with a writ and a company of marines, and it will half work, and people
will die in the half that doesn't, and I will add them to the number in my desk." She
pulled her glove straight. "I am not threatening you, Mira Calloway. I am telling you
what the arithmetic does when it has nobody to argue with."

---

They let them go at dusk, which Voss said was a courtesy, because the *Vigil* could hold
them and both of them knew it.

On the way down they passed the door of the forward cabin, the one with the tray and the
voice behind it, and this time Mira heard the pieces.

Not a game. One piece set down, and then a pause long enough to be a decision, and then
another piece set down somewhere else, and a voice saying something patient and low that
had the shape of a question even through the oak.

Nobody in the party said anything. Rook walked past the door with his eyes on the deck,
and Isolde walked past it with hers on the far end of the passage, and Mira, who had
been brought up to be polite, did not stop.

At the gangway, Vey was waiting with a ledger and a pen, and his ring was in his pocket.
Mira could see the shape of it through the cloth, and the way he kept his hand over it.

"Miss Calloway. A receipt." He tore a leaf out of the book and held it out. "One coal of
will, expended at the Gull's Teeth on the twelfth. Logged as an act of the keeper
without licence, witnessed by six hundred and forty men, and entered under *unassessed*."
He did not smile. "It is the kindest thing I can do for you. In a file, unassessed
means nobody has decided what it was yet, and while nobody has decided, nobody can take
it from you as evidence."

"Why?"

"Because I was at the Fifth," said Vey, "and I have been deciding things about other
people's light for eleven years, and I have begun to think I am not very good at it."
He closed the ledger. "I will be at Saltmere when you change your mind. I will be the
man with the form."

"Will you?"

"I'm always the man with the form," said Ossian Vey.

---

On the boat back, Isolde rowed and Rook sat with his hands on his knees and said
nothing for a hundred yards.

Then: "She will light the eight anyway."

"She'll try," said Isolde.

"She'll be right about some of it." Rook was looking at the *Vigil*, at the clean black
shape of her with no light anywhere. "That's the trouble with her. She's going to be
right about the arithmetic, and we're going to be right about the light, and the coast
is going to be the thing that pays for the difference."

"She was right about Brinehollow," Mira said. "Four hundred people, and she chose the
smaller number, and she has never once pretended it was noble."

"No," said Rook.

"Would you have chosen it?"

Rook was quiet for as long as it took to row ten feet. "No," he said. "Which is why
nobody has ever put me in charge of a number."

Mira looked back at the ship. High up, aft, one window was lit, and a shape stood in it
— a tall figure in grey with one hand raised, moving something on a table, back and
forth, back and forth, the way a person moves a chess piece.

"Eight weeks," she said.

"Seven and a bit," said Isolde. "Nine, if we're late."

"Then we'd better be early," said Mira, and took out the notebook, and on the back of
the page where the four names were, wrote:

*She wants me to be in order. I would rather be a light.*
