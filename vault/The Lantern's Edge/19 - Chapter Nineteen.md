# Chapter Nineteen

The Hollow Bell is the oldest bell on the Reach. It was cast for a tower that has fallen
down twice, and it hangs in the third tower, and it rings with no wind when the Ash is
close. Every child on the coast knows the story and no child finds it comforting. The
keepers of the Seventh kept a log of every ringing for two hundred years, and the log is
in the tower, and at midnight the tower was standing with its door open and nothing in the
world was making a sound.

---

Mira could not sleep, so she went below to count the four men in the cable tier.

They had been given a bench and a blanket each and a jug of water, and they sat in a row
with their hands on their knees and their backs straight, the way men sit in a church when
they are waiting for a funeral to start. Their coats had been taken away to be brushed.
Somebody had washed their faces.

"Private Tibb," said Mira.

"Miss."

"Are you hurt?"

"No."

"Is there anything you want?"

"No."

She had been told this would happen. She had been told it by Voss, in a warm cabin, with
white gloves on the table, and she had believed it and it had not prepared her for the way
the man's voice was *pleasant*. Not flat. Pleasant. A man being helpful.

"Tibb," she said. "Why did you come down the hill?"

"It was time."

"Whose time?"

Tibb looked at her with an expression of mild, patient attention, the way a shopkeeper
looks at a customer who has come in and not said what they want yet.

"What did you hear up there?" said Mira.

"The bell."

"When?"

"Before we got there."

Mira stood in the cable tier with the ship's timbers working above her and the lamp — which
she had to look at, because she could not smell it any more — guttering on its hook, and
she made herself count to ten before she said anything else.

"Tibb," she said. "Did you hear the bell before you went up the hill, or after?"

"The bell was ringing when we came up the shingle," said Tibb. "It had been ringing a long
time. The captain said it was the wind."

"There is no wind."

"No," said Tibb, pleasantly. "There isn't."

Pell was on the ladder with his chin on the top rung, and he had been there for the whole
of it, and he said, in a voice that was trying to be a joke, "They haven't blinked. I've
been watching. Not one of them, not once, not since they came aboard."

"They're tired," said Mira.

"Miss," said Pell. And then, because he was sixteen and could not leave a thing alone:
"Are you going to write it down?"

"Yes."

"Good," said Pell. "Because I want somebody to have written it down."

Then he said, "Listen," and put his head up like a dog.

Mira listened.

It came across the water the way a bell does at night, arriving from somewhere else: one
stroke, unhurried, low enough to feel in the boards.

"There's no bell on this ship," said Pell.

"No," said Mira.

"There's no bell in Barrowfoot. I looked. There's a bell in the tower on the hill."

The bell rang again.

Mira started counting.

---

On deck the watch had stopped moving, which on a Crown ship is the loudest thing that can
happen. Nine hundred men, and the whole deck had gone to the rails, and nobody was saying
anything, and the bell went on striking at a steady interval, the same interval, once every
six seconds, like a clock in a house where there is no clock.

Admiral Voss came up the ladder in her coat and her gloves with her hair pinned and her
face perfectly still, and stood at the rail with her hands behind her.

"How many?" she said.

"Eleven," said Mira.

"Keep counting."

"It's the Hollow Bell," said Teo.

He was at the fore rail with his hand flat on the wood the way he did now, always, before
he said anything that mattered.

"It's not in the tower," he said. "It's in the tower and it's in the water. There's two of
it. There's the one in the bell and there's one coming up through the hull."

Nobody asked him how he knew. That was the thing about the last two weeks: nobody asked
any more.

"Admiral," said Rook. "It has never taken a ship. In four hundred years the Ash has walked
ashore and it has stopped at the water and it has waited there. A ship is not a place. A
ship is a way of leaving. It cannot take a way of leaving."

"It has been at Barrowfoot since midnight," said Voss, "and it has had a town, and a town
is not enough for it. Do you know what I have ashore? Nothing. Sixty men and a captain of
marines and a tower with a bell in it. That is the entire Crown presence on this headland,
and it is already gone." She turned round. "I have nine hundred men, a cannon, and a
light. I would rather they came to me than to the next town down the coast."

"You are going to light the lens," said Mira.

"I am going to light the lens."

"A light will call them."

"Yes," said Voss. "Mr Vey. Have the lens brought up. Mr Rook, I want the marines at the
gangway and along the chains and every man of them with a pike and no orders to use it.
Miss Calloway, when they are aboard and I have seen how many of them there are, I will give
you an order and you will put the light out."

"And if they take the ship?"

"Then they have taken a ship, and I have lost a ship, and the coast has lost nothing. I
have done that sum," said Voss. "Have it brought up."

---

The lens came up in its frame and the chimney was fetched from the *Tongue*, and it took
twenty men and eleven minutes, and the bell counted through all of it.

At forty-one the last of the marines was in place along the chains with a pike and a face
like a boy at his own hanging. At forty-four the boats were got ready. At forty-six Mr Vey
came up with his book and stood at the rail and did not open it.

Mira put the Ember in the brass cup.

"Hood off," she said, and Pell took it off.

The light went out across the bay, chipped, split, throwing the old road twice with a
boat's width between the copies. The water went transparent. The drowned town came up out
of the sea in gold, and the road went up the hill in eleven switchbacks to the tower, and
the tower stood up gold with its door open.

There were no holes in the town.

"Where are they?" said Pell.

"On the road," said Mira.

They were on the road.

They came out of the town in a line, one behind the other, at the walking pace of people
who are not going anywhere in particular, and they came down the quay and onto the road
that ran out under three feet of water toward the ship.

Fifty-two of them. Mira counted them at the water's edge and she counted them again when
the line came out past the third gatepost and she got fifty-two both times, and the bell
was still going, and she could hear her own voice saying the number under her breath.

The gold of the road did not touch them. That was the thing her eyes kept refusing. The
road lay out across the water in a lane four feet wide and the figures walked in the middle
of it, and every one of them was a hole in the light, head and shoulders above water, going
along in single file the way people go along a road they know.

At the front was a woman with a basket on her arm.

At the back was a child.

The *Vigil*'s crew made a sound. Not a shout. Nine hundred men and it came out of them like
air out of a bale.

"Steady," said Voss. "Steady, all of you. They are not running. Look at them. They are not
running."

The bell went on.

"Forty-nine," said Mira.

The line came out past the last gatepost and into the deep water by the ship, and the
woman with the basket walked into it to her chest and kept walking.

"Fifty," said Mira.

The child at the back stepped off the stones and into the water, and the water came up to
its chin, and it went on.

"Fifty-one."

The basket-woman reached the *Vigil*'s side. She did not swim and she did not climb and she
did not call out. She stood in the water with one hand on the channel plate, and then she
went up the chains the way a person goes up a ladder — hand, foot, hand — and the marines
along the chains with their pikes did not move, because there was nothing to put a pike
through.

"Fifty-two," said Mira.

The bell rang again.

---

The bell struck fifty-three.

Marrow was in the water at the foot of the ladder.

He had gone over the side without being told, and he stood chest-deep with his hands on the
ladder and the water running off him, and the Hollowed came down the chains past him and
past the ladder and past the marines, one after another, and he did not stop one of them.

Rook had his blade out and the blade was dark.

It was the first time Mira had ever seen him draw it and get nothing. It was not the slow
kindling it did when he was tired or uncertain; there was no light in it at all, no thread
of Ember in the fuller, nothing. A long piece of grey steel in a knight's hand.

"Ser," she said.

"I cannot mean it," said Rook.

"Rook —"

"There is a man in a coat with a basket on his arm and he is going to the galley and I
cannot make myself mean to kill him." His voice had gone formal, which was his tell, the
way another man's voice goes high. "I have drawn this at men who were running at me. I have
never been unable to mean it before."

"Then don't," said Mira.

"No," said Rook. "I don't think I can." And he put it up, and stood there, and the last of
the line went past him with the child in its arms.

Pell got handed a rope. That was all it was: a man in a seaman's coat came along the deck
with a coil in his hands and put it into Pell's hands and said, "Take a turn on that, son,
it's lifting," and Pell said, "Aye," and took it, and had two turns on the cleat before he
understood what he was doing, and then he dropped it as though it were hot and stepped back
and hit the rail.

The man looked at the rope on the deck, and then at Pell, without any expression at all,
and then he picked it up and coiled it properly and put it on the pin.

"They're not coming for us," said Marrow, from the water. "They're going to work."

He was right. That was the whole of it. They went to the braces and the falls, they took in
slack nobody had asked them to take in, they coiled and they stowed and they put things
where things went, and a woman went down to the galley and began — Mira could hear it, in
the quiet — began to wash a pot.

"I have never in my life," said Vey, at the rail, in a voice of mild professional pain,
"seen a ship's company so thoroughly relieved of its duties."

Sergeant Dace put a hand flat on a Hollowed man's chest at the foot of the mainmast, to
stop him going aft. The man stopped. He did not push, he did not speak, he simply stopped,
and stood, and waited, and Dace stood with his hand on the man's chest and his arm taking
the weight of it, and after a while Dace stopped telling him to go back where he came from
and just stood there, and after a while after that he was standing there the same way the
man was.

"Sergeant," said Voss.

Dace did not answer.

"Sergeant Dace."

"Ma'am," said Dace, in a pleasant voice, and did not move.

Voss looked at him for four seconds. Then she said, "Carry on," and turned away, and Mira
watched the Admiral's face do something that it had not done all week, which was nothing at
all, perfectly, the way a wall does nothing.

"It has learned," said Voss.

Fifty-four. Fifty-five.

Two men came up out of the forward hatch between one stroke and the next, with their hands
at their sides, and went aft.

Fifty-six —

and Private Tibb came up last, and the bell stopped with the stroke still in the air, the
way a bell stops when somebody has put a hand on it.

---

Mira walked the deck and counted them twice and got the same number both times, which was
the number of holes she had seen on the road.

Fifty-six. Fifty-two out of the town and four out of the cable tier, and the bell had not
counted the four until the others were aboard, which meant it had known the four were
already here, and had been keeping their place.

The lens was still lit. Nobody had thought to hood it — the order had never come, and would
not come, and it lay out across the bay all night like a lane of poured metal with nobody
steering anything, and the whole ship was bright around it and nobody minded.

"Miss," said Pell. "Miss, there's eight hurt men down the cockpit and a surgeon and a
hatch on it and no lock."

Mira got the hatch grating.

It was oak and iron and it weighed as much as she did, and she dragged it across the
coaming over the cockpit ladder and sat down on it, and the surgeon's lamp came up through
the slats in a ladder of gold, and she put both hands flat on the wood and found the ring
with her mind the way she found a knot in a rope, and she held it.

The Holding came out of her like a long breath. The grating went rigid and warm under her
palms, and the slats became a floor, and the boat rocking did not move it a quarter inch.

Then they came.

Not fast. That was the horror of it, and it was the horror of the whole night: they came at
the pace of a man who has been asked to wait and is very good at it. Two of them, and then a
third, and they stood on the far side of the grating and looked at it, and one of them put
his hand on the place where the Holding began and left it there.

They did not push.

They waited.

Mira held a door and they stood on the other side of it and waited, and she understood in
the first thirty seconds that they were not going to get tired and she was, and that the
only way to win was to stop needing the thing she was holding.

She said the Circlet rhyme, because it was what she said when she had to hold something for
a long time.

*Ring of iron, ring of light,*  
*count the sisters, count them right:*  
*nine to hold the sea at bay,*  
*eight went out upon a day —*  

The next line was not there.

She went back to the start, which was what you did, and got as far as *count the sisters*
and could not find *count them right*, and stopped, and started again, and the whole thing
had gone out of her head like water out of a hand, and there was only the shape of where it
had been.

She was still holding the door.

She held it for a count of a hundred and eighty, which she counted in fives because fives
were a thing she still had, and the whole time the two of them stood on the other side of
it with their hands on the wood, patient as weather, and behind her the surgeon worked on a
leg in the light of a lamp that came up through the slats, and at a hundred and eighty the
surgeon said, "That's the artery tied. You can put that down now, girl."

She put it down.

The two Hollowed walked over the grating and past her and went aft, and did not look at
her, and one of them had been a woman with her hair in a scarf.

Mira sat on the deck with her back against the coaming and her hands shaking and did the
sum.

She could not remember the rhyme. She could not remember the first teacher who had taught
it to her, or the room, or the three years of school before the year she turned nine; she
knew there had been a school and that it had been warm, and that was all, the way you know
a house by its door.

And she could not remember the name of the family who had lived next door to her
grandmother's house.

She knew there had been a family. She knew there had been a boy, and a dog that was not
allowed in the kitchen, and an argument about a fence that her grandmother had won and been
smug about for a month. She could see the gate. She could not find the name.

*Mira Calloway,* she said to herself, in her head, because she had been taught to.
*Keeper of the Ninth. Eighteen years old. The sea was on the left on the way to school.*

Then she got up, because there was nothing else to do with it, and went to find Mr Vey.

---

She found Sallow instead.

His cabin was the one they had given him, and it was aft on the orlop with a desk bolted to
the deck and a candle in a horn lantern, and the door was open, and he was at the desk with
his coat off and his sleeves rolled and a sheet of paper in front of him and the pencil from
behind his ear in his hand.

He had not stopped writing when the ship went quiet.

He looked up when she came in, and then he did something she had not seen him do in two
weeks: he looked at the door, and then at the sheet of paper, and then at her, and did not
say anything clever.

"Come in," he said. "Shut it, if you like. It won't make any difference now."

Mira shut it.

"Fifty-six," she said.

"Fifty-six," said Sallow. "I counted too. It's a good number. It's exact. The playwright
would have wanted sixty but the playwright isn't in charge of the boats."

"You're writing it down."

"I'm writing my report." He put the pencil down and squared the paper to the edge of the
desk with two fingers, the way a man does a thing he has done ten thousand times. "It's
what I do. Every port, every week. Six salt a week and the Crown pays it out of a fund
nobody has ever asked me about."

Mira looked at the paper. It was a list, in a small neat hand, of the night: the hour, the
number, the bell, the hatch, the sergeant. It was accurate. It was more accurate than her
notebook.

"From the beginning," she said.

Sallow sat back and folded his hands, and the stage came into his voice and then went out of
it again, and what was left was a man of forty-one who had been dead for forty years
explaining a contract.

"When your captain signed me on articles at Carrow," he said, "I had two contracts already
that week and both of them were with the Crown. The first was to bring the Bright to the
Kindling, and I told you that one. The second was to see that the Crown got there first,
and I told you that one too. And there was a third, and nobody asked."

"Tell me."

"It's a report." He turned the paper round. "Who is aboard. Who is well. Who is marked, and
what they can do, and what the light is doing. Where the ship is, and where it is going, and
what it will cost to find it. Six salt a week and a box on the quay at Saltmere, and no
name on the box."

He said it without any shame at all. That was the part Mira had not been ready for. Not
defiance. Not a man brazening it out. Just a man giving the true answer to a plain question
in a clear voice.

"Teo."

"The boy is glass-eyed, and the boy is on this ship, and the boy held a lane in open water
with nothing on his hands, and I wrote that on the second of the month and I was paid for
it in salt at the end of the week." Sallow looked at the candle. "That is the entry that
got him a page. Not mine. Theirs. I sent up a fact and a clerk went and found a nineteen-year-old
fact to go with it, and now there's a word at the back of a book."

"Recovered."

"Recovered," said Sallow.

"And Wren's Fold?"

"Wren's Fold, the fourteenth. A keeper burning without a wick, and a girl with a coal in
her hand and no licence anywhere on her. I wrote it in the boat."

"And Alys?"

"Alys Wren died at the turn of the tide," said Sallow, "and I was in the boat, and I wrote
down the time."

Mira found that she had her hands flat on the desk.

"You sold her," she said.

"I described her."

"You sold the coal. You sold the *toll* —"

"The toll at the Gull's Teeth, six hundred and forty men stopped rowing and me among them,
and I wrote *I have never seen anything like it and I do not know what it was*, and Vey had
it on his desk in nine days." He tapped the paper with one finger. "Six salt, Miss
Calloway. It's the cheapest line on the card. Finding a thing is twelve. Bringing a person
back against their preference is thirty, in advance. Forty years of that and I have never
once changed anything. Six salt a week and I have moved a fleet."

"Did you know what they'd do with it?"

"No."

"Do you care?"

"No." He said it quickly, and then, more slowly, looking at his own hands: "That's not
true, and I'd like it on the record that I knew it wasn't true when I said it. I care the
way a man cares about weather. There is a storm and it is not my storm and I have written
down the wind."

Mira did not say anything.

"Nobody asked," said Sallow. "That's the whole of my defence and it is not a good one and
it happens to be the truth. In two weeks on that lugger not one of you asked me what I do
for six salt. You asked what I charge for a drowning. You asked whether I'd been paid twice.
You never asked what else was in the terms, and I would have told you, because I have never
in my life lied about a contract."

The door opened.

Marrow stood in it with the water still coming off him and pooling on the orlop boards, and
he did not come in and he did not say anything.

Sallow looked at him and kept looking at him.

"Marrow," he said.

Nothing.

"Say something."

Marrow looked at him for a while. He had a rope over his shoulder and his hands were hanging
and his face had nothing in it at all, and Mira understood that she was watching the only
two men alive who had been on the bottom of the Reach together, one of whom had stayed and
one of whom had got off, and that the whole of Sallow's forty years was in the room and
being looked at.

"No," said Marrow, and went away up the ladder.

Sallow sat with his hands on the desk for a moment. Then he picked up the pencil.

"That," he said, "is the first thing in forty years I have not been able to put a price on."
He wrote one more line on the report. "Don't tell him I said that. He'd only say something."

---

Aft, the door of the Admiral's cabin was open too, and the window was lit, and there were
seven of them standing in the room.

Konrad Voss was not in the chair.

He was at the window with his hands flat on the sill, and the other six stood behind him in
a half-circle with their hands at their sides, and the lamp on the sill was turned up and
the wick was trimmed, and one of the six had a smudge of soot on his fingers from doing it.

"He stood up," said Voss.

She was standing by the door with her gloves on.

"At the fifty-third stroke," she said. "I was in the room. I have been in this room every
night for eleven years and I have never once seen him stand up, and he stood up when the
bell rang and he went to the window and put his hands on the sill, and then they came in and
one of them trimmed the wick."

The man with the soot on his fingers was in a keeper's coat with the Seventh's badge on the
shoulder.

"That is Keeper Otway," said Voss. "I have his file in my desk. He has been dead for two
days and he has just trimmed my brother's lamp and handed it back to me, and I took it."

Mira looked at Konrad Voss, at the window, at the six behind him, at the child standing
between two grown men with its hands at its sides.

"Admiral," she said. "Ask him."

Voss was quiet for a moment. Then she came round the table and stood at her brother's
shoulder, and she said, in exactly the voice she used for arithmetic:

"Konrad Voss. What are you waiting for?"

"The bell," said her brother.

"It has stopped," said Voss.

"Yes," said Konrad Voss, and did not turn round. "It will ring again."

Voss stood there for four seconds. Mira counted them.

Then she said, "Thank you," and put her hand flat on the window beside his, and left it
there, and the two of them stood looking out at the dark bay and the drowned road and the
hill, and Mira went out and shut the door on them because there was nothing else she could
do that was not an intrusion.

---

They put her off at four in the morning in the *Tongue*, with the lens in its crutch and a
cask of water and six of them in the boat and a receipt.

"The case against the instrument is suspended," said Vey, at the gangway, with his book
open and his pen uncapped and his voice as warm as it ever was. "Pending a coast. I have
entered that as the reason and I am aware it is not a reason."

"Mr Vey."

"Miss Calloway."

"You'll stay aboard."

"I will," said Vey. "I am the Crown's assessor and the Crown's ship is going to sea with
fifty-six of the Ash's people aboard her, doing the work, and somebody has to write it
down properly." He sanded the page. "I would like to say that I am staying out of duty. In
fact I am staying because I have spent nine years copying a book written by a man I have
never met, and I would like, once, to be the one who saw the thing himself."

Voss came to the rail as they cast off. She had her hat on. Her gloves were off and the
scarred hand was on the rail and she did not put it behind her back.

"Miss Calloway. You did not sign my form."

"No."

"Good," said Voss.

Mira looked up at her.

"I have been wrong about one thing in eleven years," said the Admiral, "and it is the only
thing I ever did out of love, and it has cost me a ship and fifty-six of my coast and my
brother's last two days. I am not going to pretend that arithmetic would have saved him. My
crew are in the boats and aboard the *Sceptre*, all nine hundred of them, and at four I am
going to take this ship out past the Cradle and put her on the north rocks with the Ash's people aboard her and not one man of mine, and then I am going
to the Kindling and light eight lanterns with a writ and a company of marines, and I will be
right about most of it, and people will die in the part I am not right about." She
straightened her hat. "Get off my ship, Miss Calloway. Go and find whatever it is you think
is worth more than a signature."

"Admiral —"

"Mr Sallow," said Voss, without turning her head. "I have no further use for a man who tells
me what I already know. Get in the boat."

Sallow got in the boat.

They pulled away and the *Vigil* stood out behind them, and by the time the sky behind the
headland had gone from grey to the colour of a mussel shell she was under full sail with her
yards square and her wakes straight, and every rope of her was being handled by somebody who
had died at Barrowfoot, and the handling was perfect, and there was one window lit aft.

The bell did not ring again.

---

Mira wrote it up in the boat, with the pencil, at dawn, with the lens throwing the road out
ahead of them and Teo in the bow with his hand on the gunwale calling the holes.

*The Ash took a ship. It has never taken a ship. Rook says a ship is a way of leaving, and
the Ash cannot cross a lit edge or hold a moving thing, and it took a three-decker at anchor
without one blow struck and nobody aboard her died.*

*It did not fight. It worked. They came out of the town in a line and up the chains and they
took in slack and coiled rope and washed a pot, and the ship's company stood and watched
them do it, and by the time the bell stopped there was not a man on that deck with a job.*

*They came for the window. I said so at dusk and I was right and being right has never been
worth less. The Admiral put her own hand on the sill next to her brother's and left it
there.*

*The Admiral asked me a question I could not answer. She wanted to know what my grandmother
was doing on the night the lights went out. I said I did not know. I have never known.*

*Private Tibb said the bell was ringing when they came up the shingle, and that the captain
said it was the wind. It rang fifty-six times and stopped. Fifty-two came out of the town
and four came out of my cable tier, and it did not count the four until the others were
aboard, the way you keep a place for somebody who has not left.*

*Mr Sallow has been filing a report every week for six salt. He did not deny one word of it
and he did not apologise and he wrote the night down while it was happening, accurately,
because it is in the terms. He sold the coal and he sold the toll and he put Teo's name in a
box, and he is rowing in the stern of this boat now, and he has offered to take a watch.*

*Marrow said no to him. One word. I have never seen a man take a wound like that without
bleeding.*

*Cost, tonight: I held the cockpit hatch for a count of a hundred and eighty and they stood
on the other side of it and waited, and when I put it down I could not remember the Circlet
rhyme, or the first three years of school, or the name of the family who lived next door to
my grandmother, the ones with the boy and the dog and the fence. I can see the gate. I
cannot find the name.*

*We are lighting the lens to find the road home, which is exactly what she did, and there is
no other way to get to the Kindling across three feet of water, and I am not going to
pretend it is not the same mistake. The Mercy Bell is four days behind us and I have never
wanted a captain so much in my life.*

*I am eighteen years old and I have a notebook and half of it is a list of things I used to
know, and the other half is a list of people I am not going to lose.*

*It was dawn and nobody had lit anything.*
