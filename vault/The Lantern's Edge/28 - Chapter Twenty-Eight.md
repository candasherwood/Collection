# Chapter Twenty-Eight

The cutters came back on Tuesday, and four of them came back with a keeper aboard.

The fifth came back with a letter. It was written on the back of the warrant, in a hand like a
fence, and Vey read it out in the guides' room with his voice doing something it did not usually
do.

> *To the keeper of the Ninth, from the keeper of the Sixth.*
>
> *I have kept this tower for eleven years. For nine of them it was dead, and the Crown paid me to
> sit in it and trim a wick for a light that had not been lit since before my daughter was born. Two
> years ago they gave me an Ember and a licence and a form to sign, and I have kept the light every
> night since, and it is the only light on the north coast. You want me to put it out so that a girl
> can light nine at the end of the world.*
>
> *If you are right I will look a fool for a week. If you are wrong I will be the man who did it,
> and there will be nobody left to argue with me about it.*
>
> *I am not coming, and I am not putting it out, and I will keep it lit until somebody with
> authority tells me otherwise. I am sorry. I am not sorry enough.*

"Six hundred and forty men in Saltmere," said Sallow, "and the one who says no is the honest one."

The other four came up the slip on Tuesday afternoon with their Embers in their coat pockets like
men carrying eggs. The keeper of the Third was a woman of sixty called Meridew with a bad hip and a
face that had been rained on for thirty years. The keeper of the Fourth had been a tavern-keeper
for nine years before Voss put a ring on him and had never got the smell of the taproom out of his
coat. The keeper of the Fifth was Mr Vey, who had walked out of his own tower at dawn, handed the
ledger to a sergeant, and stood on the quay at Saltmere for a full minute with nothing in his hands
before he got into the boat.

"I have not been without it since I was twenty-one," he said, when Mira asked him. "I did not know
I held it. That is the whole of what I have learned this week and it is not enough to be worth the
journey."

"Where's the fifth?" said Hale.

"The Fifth is me," said Vey. "The one that is missing is the Sixth. And the Kindling's own is the
Warden's, and he is not coming, because somebody has to be at the door when the Assize arrives, and
because he is seventy-eight and I have seen him on a stair."

---

The Warden did not come, and he did not make a speech about it either, which was worse.

He came down to the slip on Wednesday night with his coat on and stood at the head of it while the
boats went out, and when Rook came past him he put something in his hand.

It was the ninth pin. Brass, three inches, worn smooth at the head from eleven years of being
turned on a desk.

"It doesn't hold anything," said Halloran. "The drum's cut. It's a piece of brass with a story on
it."

"Then why are you giving it to me?"

"Because I am seventy-eight and there is a man with a list coming here on Friday to take my forge,
and I have decided that I would rather be at the door than at the Fall." The Warden looked at the
fleet in the roads, at twenty-two hooded lamps, at the guides with their sleeves already up. "I
have signed things for eleven years. I signed eight dousings and a deferral and a stipend and a
letter every quarter that says the guides do not exist. If I am going to be any use at all it is
going to be standing in a doorway saying no to a man who is right about the weather."

"You'll lose."

"Probably." Halloran put his hand out, and Rook took it, which is not a thing knights do either.
"You have no surcoat and a dead sword and no vows you can say, and you are the best knight this
house has produced in forty years, and I would like that on the record before I go and be old in a
doorway." He let go. "Bring the keeper home."

---

They practised on the water.

That was Mira's idea and it was a bad one, and it was the only one, and by Wednesday night it had
cost four people a week each and one of them a month.

The way it worked was this. You took a boat out into the roads at dusk with a lit lamp in the bows
and a rope trailing, and you held the light with your ring and you pushed it out along the rope
until it made a wall — not a beam, nothing you could see from the shore, just a cold flat place in
the air where the water stopped being water — and then you tried to keep it there while somebody
rowed past it and told you where it leaked.

The first night the keeper of the Fourth held his for a count of nine and then sat down in the boat
with his nose bleeding and asked what day it was.

"Nine," said Ede Calloway, from the stern. "That's not bad. Beck held his for four the first time
and then he couldn't find his own front door."

Ede had come south.

She had come north from Carrow in a fishing boat the moment the word reached her, and she had come
up the slip on Tuesday night with a bag and a stick and her coat buttoned wrong, and she had stood
on the quay and said, "The wall's down. I asked the knight for my ring back and took it off, and the
island is dark for the first time in eleven years, and it will keep three nights, because nothing
that has been kept that long goes bad in three nights." Then she had looked at the
fleet in the roads for a while and said, "Well. That's more ships than I have ever seen in one
place and I have been alive seventy-one years."

"You didn't have to come," said Mira.

"I did," said Ede. "You cannot hold a ring at the Fall. You have never held one on a shore."

"What's the difference?"

"A ring is a thing with no ends," said Ede, "and a shore is a thing made of ends. Put your ring on
open water and it closes, and the sea goes round it, and the Ash goes along it looking for a way
past and there isn't one. Put your ring on the Fall and one side of it is *the edge of the world*,
and the edge of the world is not a place you can hold a line against, because there is nothing
there to hold it *on*." She sat down on a bollard, which took her a while. "So you don't hold a
ring. You hold a horseshoe. Both ends on rock, and the rock does the closing."

Mira looked at the map on the wall of the guides' room, and at the Fall on it, and at the shape of
the Fall, which nobody had ever drawn properly because nobody had ever needed to.

"How long is the lip?"

"Nine miles," said Ede. "Near enough. I have been looking at it for seventy-one years and it has
always been nine miles and it has always annoyed me."

Nine miles. Nine lanterns. Nine miles of edge with two ends on rock at either side of the bay.

"Then we need seven holders," said Mira, "and two lights anchored on the rock."

"And a night to teach four Crown keepers to hold a thing they have never held, and one of them is a
clerk," said Ede. "So we had better start."

---

They started that night, and it went badly, and it kept going badly, and then on the second night
it worked, which is the way of all practice.

Meridew could hold. She was sixty and her hip was bad and she had been keeping a light in a
drowned tower for eleven years with nobody to talk to, and when she put her ring out over the water
it went as steady as a wall. She lost a month doing it and she did not care.

"I have eleven years of nothing in my head," she said. "Take a month. Take two. There's nothing in
there I was using."

The tavern-keeper could not hold at all the first night. On the second he got it, and the thing
that did it was Ede asking him what his pub looked like at closing time, and him describing it for
ten minutes — the fog on the windows, the drip off the sign, the way the last man out always stood
in the doorway and said goodnight twice — and then Ede said, "Now hold that," and he put his ring
out and the water went flat for a count of forty.

Mr Vey held on the first night, which surprised everybody including Mr Vey.

"How?" said Mira.

"Because I have written all of it down," said Vey. "Nine years of it. Every quay, every lantern,
every assessment, every man's name and what he was owed and what he was worth. I can picture the
whole coast from the inside of that book, and it appears that a picture is a picture." He looked at
his own hand as if it belonged to somebody else. "I have been keeping a light for nine years and
calling it paperwork."

Teo held on the second night.

He had no ring. He had been lens-capable since Candlewick and had paid for it twice, and when he
put his hands out over the water in the stern of the *Tongue* with a lamp in front of him the water
went flat for a count of a hundred and forty, which was longer than anybody else managed, and then
he sat down and said, "Right. What did it take?"

Nobody answered.

"Somebody say what it took," said Teo. "I can't find it myself. That's the thing about it."

Mira crouched down in front of him.

"You can't hear the sea," she said.

Teo sat very still.

He looked at the water. He looked at the fleet, and at the gulls, and at the *Tongue*'s gunwale
with the paint coming off it, and he said, "Say something."

"Teo."

"Say something else."

"Teo Vane. You're in a boat. It's Wednesday night."

"I can see your mouth move," said Teo. "I can see the gulls. There's no sound in any of it." He put
his hands over his ears and took them away again. "It's not quiet. Quiet is a thing you can hear.
There's just — nothing where the sea was."

Isolde, in the bows with her arms folded, said, "Well, that's the most horrible thing I've ever
seen and I've seen a man eaten by a rope."

"He can still navigate," said Mira.

"I know he can navigate. He navigated us sixty miles down a drowned road with no eyes and he did it
by counting." Isolde came aft and sat down on the thwart beside him, which for that woman was a
public display of affection. "Right. You'll do it by counting and I'll do the listening. When you're
on a deck you'll put your hand on my shoulder and I'll tell you what the sea is doing. That's the
arrangement. It's a stupid arrangement and it'll work."

---

They sailed on Thursday morning with twenty-two sail and one hooded lamp apiece, two hundred miles
of coast to cover before the ninth night, and the land went past them all day like a house somebody
had moved out of.

That was the thing nobody had prepared for. The towns were still there. The doors were open. There
was washing on lines and nets on walls and a dog on the quay at Wren's Fold that barked at them all
the way past and then sat down and watched them go. There were no people anywhere. Not Hollowed,
not dead, not hidden: *gone*, all of them, south, walking the road with a hole where each of them
should be, nine hundred of them and more, all the way down the Reach to the Fall.

"There's a fire still going in the Fourth," said Pell, at Candlewick. "Miss. There's smoke."

"Leave it," said Mira. "It'll go out."

At the Sill they passed the waterfall with the tower in it, and the water going over the rocks, and
nobody in the tower.

That night the guides took the fleet through the dark water, and it was the first time in two
hundred years that a band had guided a Crown ship.

It was Isolde's idea and she sold it to Voss in four sentences: *we have eleven guides and
forty hours and two hundred miles, and no captain on this coast will run dark water at night
because he cannot see the un-ships. Ours can.*

So the fleet went south in a long line at seven knots with the lead ship's guide in the bows and
his band lit, and behind him the next, and behind him the next, and every ship following the ship
in front with a man on the bowsprit holding a rope and a green wrist, and the line bending the way
a line of geese bends, and nobody sleeping, and nobody speaking except the guides, who called the
road across the water in a flat carrying voice, one after another, all night:

"Starboard ten. There's a hull under us, she's old, she's not moving. Starboard ten and hold."

"Port five. Port five. There's a rock at a cable and a wreck on the rock and the wreck has a
boat on it."

"Straight. Nothing. Nothing for a mile."

Mira stood at the *Surety*'s rail at two in the morning and watched the whole Reach go past in the
dark with a green light at the front of it, and thought about a vow with five lines and one of them
struck out, and about a boy of fourteen on a stair saying *hold my hand*, and about a Crown that
had spent two hundred years insisting that none of this existed while its ships ran aground on a
coast it had made dark.

At four in the morning old Marrow, on the bow of the *Mercy Bell*, began to sing the tide-bell of
the Sill, four notes down and four up, and one of the Crown's men on the next ship picked it up
because he had heard it in a port once, and by five there were three ships singing it badly and a
guide laughing in the dark for the first time in anybody's memory.

At dusk the noise started.

You feel the Fall in your teeth before you hear it — the book says thirty miles and the book is
right — and by Thursday night it was in everybody's teeth, a low grinding hum that got into the
planking and the masts and the bottom of a cup, and men who had been at sea thirty years stopped
talking when they came on deck in the morning.

"How far?" said Mira, on Friday at dawn, to Sallow, who had been up all night with the charts.

"Eight miles. There's the bay at the lip — the old chart calls it the Bowl — and there's the edge
past it." He rolled the chart. "There are lights on the water."

---

The Fall is not a waterfall. Mira had expected a waterfall, because that is the word, and the word
is wrong: a waterfall falls onto something. The Fall is two thousand miles of sea going over an
edge into nothing, and it does it silently at the edge and with a sound like the world grinding its
teeth everywhere else, and there is a mist that goes up out of it for a mile and comes down as rain
on ships four miles back.

The bay at the lip is a stone bowl nine miles from headland to headland, with a shingle beach at its
head and the bones of a harbour, and the fleet came into it at eight in the morning with the noise in
their teeth and found it occupied.

Nine un-ships lay in a ring in the middle of the bay, at anchor, with their sails furled and no
crews. Beyond them, on the shingle, stood the Hollowed: nine hundred of them and more, in the
clothes they had been wearing when the Ash took them, standing in rows with their faces to the
water, and not one of them moving.

And at the mouth of the bay, where the water began to run, sat the *Vigil* — black, and quiet, and
her one window lit.

The fleet anchored in a line across the mouth of the bowl with the guides in the bows of every
ship, bands lit, uncovered, in daylight, in front of a Crown that had a list of their names, and
twenty-two hooded lamps going up the masts one after another.

"Nobody fires," said Voss.

"Nobody's going to fire," said Hale.

"I know. I am saying it out loud so that the record has it."

Sallow wrote it down. He wrote everything down that morning; it is the only complete account of the
last day, and the Crown has it now, and it is four pages long and it is mostly names.

*At eight o'clock on the ninth day of the ninth month the fleet of the Reach anchored in the Bowl
at the foot of the Fall: twenty-two sail, nine hundred and forty men, eleven guides, four keepers of
the Crown, one keeper of the Ninth, one keeper of the Ninth before her, and a clerk with a book. The
wind was northerly and light. The noise was continuous. There were Hollowed ashore in numbers we
could not count and nine ships at anchor with no crews and one ship with one window lit.*

*At nine o'clock the woman came out of the water.*

---

At half past eight Voss went ashore with a boat and two marines and no permission from anybody, and
nobody stopped her, because nobody had ever stopped her doing anything.

She walked along the shingle in front of nine hundred people standing in rows, looking at faces,
and the men behind her did not say a word. She went along the third row and stopped.

Konrad Voss was fifty-one years old and he was wearing the good coat he had been wearing the night
Brinehollow went down, and he was standing with his hands at his sides and his face to the water,
and he did not turn his head.

"Konrad," she said.

He did not answer.

"Konrad. Who am I?"

He turned his head then, and looked at her for a moment with the mild interest of a man
acknowledging a stranger on a quay.

"Ilse," he said.

Voss stood very still.

"Yes," she said. "That's right. That's very good." She put her glove back on. "You are not going
over that edge tonight, do you understand me? Not tonight." She turned round and walked back to the
boat without looking at the rest of them, and when she came up the side of the *Surety* her face
was the face she wore in a room with a great many chairs in it.

"Admiral," said Hale.

"Get the fleet's boats in the water," said Voss. "All of them. A line of boats from the shingle to
the lip, a man in every one, and I want them there before the turn."

"To do what?"

"To be in the way," said Voss. "If the book is going to be paid in people, it can be paid past
twenty-two ships' boats and nine hundred men who will not move, and it can start with me."

---

She came out of the water at the mouth of the bay, walking.

She was a woman of about forty in a grey coat that was not wet, and she walked from the middle of
the bay to the shingle without getting her feet under, and the water did not take her weight, or
she did not give it hers. She had a book under her arm, which was the worst part: not a ledger like
Vey's, a book, an old one, with a clasp.

She stopped ten feet from the water's edge, in front of the fleet, and looked along the line of
ships and did not blink.

"Admiral," she said. Her voice carried. It should not have carried over that noise and it did.
"I am the Receiver. I hold the Mother's book for this coast and I have held it for four hundred
years." She opened it. "You are early."

"We are on time," said Voss, from the *Surety*'s rail.

"You are early," said the Receiver. "The turn of the tide is at seven tonight. Until the turn, the
book is open and the debt is not yet due." She turned a page. "But you have brought the light to
me, which is a courtesy I have not been shown in a hundred years, so I will read the account to you
and you may tell me how you intend to pay."

"Read it," said Mira.

The Receiver read it.

*"The coast of the Reach, in the year of the breaking: one covenant, nine lanterns, nine keepers,
and a light kept on the water for the safe passage of the living. In the eleventh year of the dark
the lights were withdrawn by the Crown and the covenant was not renewed. Eleven years of light at
nine lanterns is owed. The debt may be paid in light, or in what light has taken."*

She closed the book.

"That is the whole of it. There is no schedule and no interest and no mercy in it. It is a
contract, and it was signed by people who are dead, and the dead do not get to renegotiate." She
looked at Mira. "You are the keeper of the Ninth."

"I am."

"You are not on the book."

"I know. That's been the trouble all year."

"It is the trouble now," said the Receiver. "A keeper who is not on the book cannot pay a debt that
is. You may light your nine lights and the book will take them, and the book will not care whose
hands they came out of. But I will tell you the thing that your grandmother would have told you if
she had been able to say it out loud, which is this: *the debt may be paid in what light has
taken.*"

"Meaning what?" said Vey.

"Meaning the years," said the Receiver. "Every season a keeper gives a lamp is a season the light
takes and the deep keeps. There are eleven years of them in this coast — in the ring at Carrow and
the rings in the towers and the ring that went out with Aldous Mere — and the deep has been keeping
them the way a bank keeps a deposit. Pay me in those, and the book closes."

"How much?" said Mira.

"Nine seasons," said the Receiver. "Out of nine keepers, tonight, held at the lip until the turn.
That is the price of eleven years of dark, and it is cheaper than you deserve, and it is the only
number I have."

Nobody on the *Surety* said anything.

"And if we do not pay?"

The Receiver turned and looked at the shingle, at nine hundred people standing in rows with their
faces to the water.

"Then I collect in what the coast has," she said. "Which is them. One at a time, over the lip,
until the count is met. It will take until the middle of the night and it will be very quiet, and
then the book will close and the coast will be free and there will be nobody on it."

Hale made a sound.

"You cannot—"

"I do not choose," said the Receiver, without heat. "I am a clerk. The Mother is the creditor. I
have been reading this book out to coasts for four hundred years and every one of them argued with
me and every one of them paid." She turned to go. "At the turn of the tide. Nine lights at the lip
and nine seasons in them, or the collection. You have until seven."

"Receiver," said Isolde.

The woman stopped.

"The *Mercy Bell*," said Isolde. "Raised with her crew aboard her, forty years ago, off the Sill.
I bought her with the crew in her and I have sailed her since, and I have been paying the Mother's
interest every year of my life in one way or another. What's she on the book for?"

The Receiver turned a page.

"The *Mercy Bell*," she said, "is entered as one hull, ninety feet, raised at the request of a
captain, on terms: the hull and her company to be returned on demand." She looked up. "The hull is
entered as Crown property as of the twelfth day of this month. The company is Crown property as of
the same hour. The Mother does not care whose name is on the paper. She cares that the paper
exists."

Vey went white.

"That is my entry," he said.

"It is a very good entry," said the Receiver. "The best in the book. I have read it four times."

---

They did the arithmetic on the *Surety*'s quarterdeck at ten in the morning with the noise in
their teeth and nine hundred people standing on the shingle behind them.

Seven holders: Mira, Ede, Meridew, the tavern-keeper, Vey, Teo, and — after eleven minutes of
argument — Isolde, who had no ring and no light and had never held anything in her life, and who
said, "I have been dead for eleven minutes at the bottom of this Reach and I came back up and I have
never told anyone what I saw, and I am telling you now: there is nothing down there that is going
to be impressed by a ring. Put me in the water and I will hold the rope."

"Nine lights and seven holders," said Vey. "Two anchored on the rock at the ends of the horseshoe,
which is nine lights and eight Embers, because the Sixth is still in his tower. That is the plan as
it stands and it has a hole in it the size of the north anchor."

"Then we light the Sixth's," said Mira.

"He said no."

"He said no to putting it out," said Mira. "He didn't say no to sending it."

She wrote the letter on the quarterdeck with Vey's pen and Sallow's pencil, because Vey's hand was
shaking, and she sent it south with the fastest cutter in the fleet and a man who had volunteered
before she had finished the sentence.

*Keeper of the Sixth. You are the only light on the north coast and I am asking you to put it out
and send it to the Fall in a boat. If we are wrong you will have been right and the north coast
will be dark and it will be my name on the warrant and not yours. If we are right there will be nine
lights at the lip tonight and a book closed. You said you would keep your wick trimmed until
somebody with authority told you otherwise. I have no authority at all. I am asking anyway.*

*— Mira Calloway, keeper of the Ninth, who is not on the book*

---

The collection began at four in the afternoon, three hours before the tide, and nobody had expected
that.

It did not begin with a signal. The Receiver walked out onto the water and stood there with her
book open, and the first row of the Hollowed turned, all together, the way a field turns in wind,
and walked down the shingle to the lip, and the first of them — a man in a fisherman's coat with a
patch on the elbow — stepped off the edge of the world.

He did not fall. That was the thing that broke something in nine hundred men watching from the
ships. He stepped off the lip and went *down*, upright, walking, into the mist, and the mist took
him and closed, and the sound of the Fall went on exactly as it had before.

The second one stepped off. Then the third.

"Nine lights!" Mira shouted, across the water, at the Receiver, at the whole bay. "We're not ready!
It's not seven!"

"Seven is the tide," said the Receiver, without turning. "The collection is not the tide. The
collection is the interest. It began when the book was opened and it will not stop for a plan."

"Then stop it!"

"I cannot," said the Receiver. "I am a clerk."

Ede was beside Mira at the rail with her stick in her hand.

"Girl," she said. "Look at the mist."

Mira looked.

The mist at the lip was not mist any more. It was coming up out of the Fall in a shape — slow, and
huge, and patient, the way a wave stands up before it comes down — and it had shoulders, and it had
a long back, and it went on for a mile, and it was made of the same grey as the water and the same
grey as the road under it, and it was walking ashore.

"That's it," said Ede Calloway. "That's the thing the Ash is a weather of."

It came up over the lip and set one foot on the shingle, and the noise of the Fall changed — it
went *lower*, and the fleet's masts hummed, and every dog in the bay began to howl at once — and
then it stopped, with one foot on the shore and the whole of it still standing in the mist behind,
and it waited.

The Receiver closed her book.

"The interest is paid for the hour," she said. "Nine of them. The principal is due at the turn."
She looked along the fleet. "You have three hours, keeper, and seven keepers, and one light that is
still in a tower six hundred miles north of here. I would begin."

---

Mira wrote it up that afternoon in the *Surety*'s cabin with the door open and the noise coming
through the deck.

*Friday, ninth of the ninth, the Bowl at the foot of the Fall.*

*Twenty-two sail. Nine hundred and forty men. The coast behind us is empty — doors open, nets on
the walls, a dog at Wren's Fold that barked at us all the way past.*

*The Receiver holds the Mother's book and has held it four hundred years and does not blink. The
debt is eleven years of light at nine lanterns. It may be paid in light or in what light has taken.
Nine seasons, out of nine keepers, held at the lip until the turn. That is the price and it is
cheaper than we deserve and it is the only number she has.*

*Seven of us can hold. Ede came south and taught us the horseshoe: you cannot hold a ring on a
shore, because a shore is made of ends. Both ends on rock and the rock does the closing. Nine
miles of lip. Seven holders and two anchored lights, and the whole thing depends on a keeper at the
Gull's Teeth who told us no, and who I have written to anyway.*

*Teo held a hundred and forty and paid with the sound of the sea. He can see it and he cannot hear
it and he has not said a word about it since. Isolde has put herself in the water with him and says
she will hold the rope. Vey held on the first night because he has written the whole coast down and
a picture is a picture, and then he found out that the Mother's book has his entry for the Mercy
Bell in it and that the ship and her company are the collateral. He went white and then he went to
work, which I think is the bravest thing I have seen this week.*

*The Ash came ashore at four. It is standing on the shingle with one foot on the land and the rest
of it still in the mist, and it is a mile long, and it is waiting. The Hollowed are walking over
the lip one at a time and the Receiver calls it the interest. Nine of them have gone over. Nobody
could stop it and nobody tried, and I am writing that down because I want it in the record that we
stood on a deck and watched.*

*Three hours to the turn of the tide. Nine lights and eight Embers: seven held along the lip and two
anchored on the rock, and the anchor at the north end is empty because the Sixth's light is in a
tower six hundred miles north and the keeper of it told us no.*

*I have been keeping this notebook for a year so that I would know what I had lost. I find I have
been keeping it so that somebody else would know what I saw.*

*If this is the last page, the vow does not say win.*
