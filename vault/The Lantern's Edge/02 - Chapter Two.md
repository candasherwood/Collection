# Chapter Two

The stairs went up in a spiral so tight that the outside edge of each step was three
times the width of the inside, and there were one hundred and twelve of them. Mira
counted, because counting was the only thing that kept the dark from closing in around
her, and because page twenty of the notebook said *one hundred and twelve* and she wanted
the notebook to be wrong about something.

It was not wrong.

By the ninth step her right hand had found the wall and stayed there. The plaster was
cold and faintly furred with salt, and it had writing on it — not the usual writing, which
is a name and a date and a word about the weather, but the other kind: initials cut in
with a knife at the height where a right-handed person's shoulder comes level with the
wall, twenty or thirty of them, some of the cuts gone soft with paint and some still
sharp, and under one set of initials, in a smaller hand, *still here.*

She did not stop to read them properly. She had slowed down without deciding to, the way
anyone would in a room where somebody was asleep, and she made herself go up at the pace
she had come up the cliff.

The air changed at about the sixtieth step. It got warmer, which was wrong, and it smelled
more strongly of paraffin, which was worse. Somewhere above her a step creaked — not under
her foot. She stood still and listened to it not happen again, and then went on, and did
not look down, because there was nothing down there but the dark she had come up out of.

At the top there was a door, low and narrow and painted the same white as everything else.
It was not locked either.

The lamp room was eighteen feet across and had windows all the way round it, and the glass
in them was black, and it held one chair, one desk, and a brass lamp the size of a
washtub. There was oil in the reservoir; she could smell it before she could see anything,
and the whole room smelled of it the way a kitchen smells of the last meal. The wick was
trimmed, and the chimney was clean, and none of that had happened by itself.

There was a log book on the desk with a pencil laid across it and a strip of leather keeping
the place. And there was a box of matches, set squarely at the corner of the desk with the
label turned to face the door, as though somebody had been expecting a visitor who would
need to find it in a hurry.

Mira sat down in the chair instead, because her legs had made the decision for her.

It was warm.

Not warm from the day, and not warm from her. Warm the way a chair is warm when somebody
has been sitting in it all evening and has just got up to answer the door.

She counted to ten, then to twenty, and by thirty she had admitted the thing she had been
avoiding since the door swung open: the tower was not abandoned. It was simply waiting, and
it had been waiting for a very long time, and it had expected someone taller.

The log book was a foot from her hand. She looked at the strip of leather and the pencil
and the place kept, and she thought about the numbers on the wall and the names that had
gone soft with paint, and she decided — with the particular care of somebody who has
already decided one enormous thing today — that she would not open it.

Not yet.

Outside, the sea went quiet — all at once, the way a room goes quiet when someone walks
in.
