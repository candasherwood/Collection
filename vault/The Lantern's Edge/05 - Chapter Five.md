# Chapter Five

The knight came up the stairs alone, which Mira thought was either very brave or
very rude, and it turned out to be neither. It was procedure.

He was perhaps twenty-five, in a grey coat with the Emberwatch's small flame
stitched at the collar, and he had the flat, tired politeness of somebody who has
spent a long time being sent to do unpleasant things correctly. He came into the
lamp room, took off his gloves, and inclined his head to Isolde as though she were
a colleague.

"Captain Sable."

"Ser Rook."

"Ser Cadan Rook, of the —"

"I know who you are, boy. Your order doused my grandmother's kitchen fire and sent
me the bill."

The knight absorbed that without expression. He took a folded paper from his
coat, opened it, and held it up so that the light went through it.

"By the order of the Salt Throne and the Warden of the Kindling, this light is to
be extinguished. The keeper is to surrender her ring into my keeping and is to be
conveyed to Saltmere for the assessment of her household." He lowered the paper.
"I am obliged to tell you that resistance constitutes a claim on your property, and
that I am sorry."

"You're sorry," said Mira.

"I am sorry," Ser Rook said, "and I will do it anyway, which is the part that
matters."

Behind him, five soldiers in Crown grey had come up the stairs and were standing
in the curve of the wall, not pointing anything at anybody yet, which was somehow
worse.

"No," Mira said.

"Ser —" Teo began.

"Nobody's talking to you, Vane."

"You know him?" Mira said.

"Everyone knows him," said Isolde. "He's the boy who drowned Brinehollow."

Ser Rook's blade came out of its sheath without a sound, and then the light in it
came up, a thin thread of gold down the fuller, and for one moment the room was
lit from below and everybody in it looked tired and honest.

Then he said, "This is mercy," and the thread guttered out.

Mira saw his jaw tighten. He looked at the blade as though it had said something
in front of company.

"It's a nice trick," Isolde said. "Does it work on you?"

The knight did not answer. He sheathed the sword, which had gone completely dark,
and put his hand out for her ring instead.

And then, from the stairs behind the soldiers, came the sound of water.

Not a drip. A *slosh* — the wet, heavy sound of a coat full of sea — and then
another, and then the soldiers were turning and Mira was looking past them at
five people coming up the last steps of a tower that had been empty four minutes
ago.

They were soaked through. Their coats were the old cut, the before-Fall cut, with
the long cuffs and the horn buttons, and they were so wet that the wool hung on
them like weed. Their faces were calm and pleasant and completely empty, the way a
house is empty, and every one of them was smiling very slightly, as though
remembering a joke from a long time ago.

The first one walked up to the desk and picked up the glass chimney of the lamp.

"Don't," Mira said.

The man did not look at her. He set the chimney down on the floor, carefully, in
the exact place where it would not be stepped on, and then he turned and reached
for the wick.

One of the soldiers hit him with the butt of a crossbow. The sound was terrible.
The man went down against the desk, got up, and reached for the wick again,
patiently, and did not wipe the blood off his mouth because it was not, Mira saw,
his blood to bother about any more.

Ser Rook's blade came out again and this time it was bright, bright as a lit
window, and he put himself between the Hollowed and the lamp and said, in a voice
that had changed, "Get behind me."

Isolde was already moving. "Girl. The lamp."

"It's lit —"

"The *lamp*." The captain had her knife out — not the eating knife; a longer,
flatter, uglier one — and she had the brass housing in her other hand and was
feeling along it for something with her thumb. "This isn't a lamp, it's a box. It
was never a lamp. Somebody has been very clever with brass and a good story."

Mira looked at the wick, at the oil, at the flame she had lit four minutes ago
with the fifth match.

"It's a wick," she said.

"That's a wick," Isolde agreed, "on top of a lid. Hold the light steady, keep your
eyes on the glass, and don't watch what I'm doing with the knife."

Teo took her by the shoulder and turned her towards the window, and she stood
there with the whole impossible fleet laid out gold on the water and listened to
the noise behind her: the soldiers, the Hollowed, the wet slap of a coat against
a desk, and Isolde breathing hard through her nose the way people do when they are
doing something with their hands that they are good at.

Then Teo was gone from her side.

He had gone to the head of the stairs — *why* — and one of the Hollowed had him by
the collar of his good coat, and Teo was on the top step, off balance, with one
heel over the drop and one hand scrabbling at the rail, and the Hollowed was not
pulling him, it was simply holding him out over the stairwell the way you hold a
bucket out of a window.

Mira's ring went cold. That was the first thing: cold, all the way round, like a
handcuff made of ice water.

She did not decide anything. Later she would try to find the place where the
decision had been and there would not be one. There was only the fact of him, and
the fact of the drop, and the enormous, level, ringing certainty in the middle of
her chest that said: *no.*

The light came off the lantern.

It came off the lantern like a rope comes off a coil — one long line of it, gold
and then white at the centre, eight feet of it, going from the open lamp straight
across the room into her hand. It was heavy. It was heavier than a rope; it pulled
at her shoulder and her back and her teeth. She felt it in her knees.

She got it round Teo's wrist and closed her fist.

He looked at the light. Then he looked at her, and there was no joke in his face
at all.

"Pull," Mira said.

Isolde had him by the belt. Marrow had Isolde by the coat. The Hollowed came over
the top step after him, unhurried, and Ser Rook met it with a blade that was
blazing now, and the two of them went down together out of sight and the noise of
it went on and on.

Mira pulled.

It took thirty seconds. Later she would work it out from the clock in the harbour
master's office, which she would never see. Thirty seconds, eight feet of rope,
one boy. She held it until Teo was flat on the floor of the lamp room with his
hands over his eyes, and then the rope went out of her hand like water out of a
sieve, and the room tipped sideways.

She sat down on the floor. It seemed the only thing to do.

"Up," Isolde said. "Up, girl. We are leaving."

"I need a minute."

"You need a boat." The brass housing lay open on the desk, split along a seam
Mira had taken for a shadow, and the captain was holding what had been inside it:
a stone the size of a fist, wrapped in sailcloth, and even through the cloth it was
glowing, a warm steady ember-colour. It made the housing on the desk look like
exactly what it was, which was a box. "Marrow. Sand, from the fire bucket. A lot of
it."

Mira got up, because sitting down was not, in fact, a plan. She got her notebook
off the desk. She got the matches, because they were hers now, and she put them in
her pocket, and she checked that the ring was on her finger, and it was, and it had
gone warm again.

Then she opened the back cover of the notebook, where a photograph was folded into
a paper pocket, because she wanted to see her grandmother's face. Just for a
moment. Just to steady herself.

The photograph was of a woman in a man's coat, standing in front of a tower with a
lantern hooked over her elbow, laughing at whoever was holding the camera. On the
back, in pencil: *Nan, the year the light went out.*

Mira looked at it for a long time.

She knew the handwriting. She knew the coat — she was wearing it. She knew the
tower, and the year, and the story of the photograph, which was that her
grandmother had never once been able to keep still for a portrait.

She did not know the face.

Not *did not recognise*. It was not there to recognise. There was a woman in the
picture with a wide mouth and a crooked nose, and Mira looked at her with the
polite, mild, awful attention she would give a stranger in a queue, and the ring on
her finger was warm, and somewhere below her the sea was full of ships.

"That's the price," Isolde said, behind her. She had the sand bucket under one
arm and the stone in it, glowing up through the grains. "It's always the price.
Nobody warns you because nobody who's paid it can remember being warned."

Mira folded the photograph back into its pocket.

"We're going," she said.

They went down one hundred and twelve steps with the light from a bucket, past
two soldiers sitting against the wall who would not look at them, and Ser Cadan
Rook standing at the top of the stair with his sword blazing gold and both hands
on the rail, holding a doorway against something that did not get tired.

He did not turn round. "Vane," he said, to the stairwell. "The writ says the
keeper is to be assessed."

"The keeper's not here," Teo said.

"Then I will report that the light was already out."

The blade wavered. Mira, halfway down the dark stairs with her hand on the cold
stone, saw it waver, and saw him set his jaw and mean it again, and the gold came
back up the steel like a tide.

She never told him she had seen it.

The *Mercy Bell*'s boat was waiting at the foot of the cliff. Marrow rowed, and
Isolde sat in the bow with a bucket between her knees like a woman bringing home
something she had stolen, which, Mira supposed, she had.

The sea had come back to itself. Wind, and a slap of water, and the smell of
salt — and no bell at all, though the ship was only a hundred yards off and her
bell had rung, by itself, not ten minutes before.

On deck, in the dark, with her hands still shaking, Mira took out the notebook and
the pencil from behind her ear and wrote, at the bottom of page twenty-two:

*Teodor Vane. The Mercy Bell. Both of them are real.*

Teo read it upside down. He did not laugh at her. He was quiet for a moment, which
she would come to understand was the most expensive thing he had.

"Why did you write that?" he said.

"Because I'm going to forget things," Mira said. "I already have."

He sat down next to her on the hatch cover, close enough that their shoulders
touched, and did not make it mean anything, and that was the first kind thing
anyone had done for her all night that she had not had to pay for.

The fleet was gone. Not gone — unwitnessed. Mira stood at the rail with her hands
wrapped in her coat and understood, for the first time, that the difference
between the sea she had grown up with and the sea she had seen tonight was not a
difference in the sea. It was a difference in who was looking.

Fifty-two ships. All of them still sailing, crews at their posts, canvas drawing, a
coast's whole trade going about its business four hundred years after the coast
ended. None of them knew. That was the thing she could not put down. They were not
waiting to be rescued. They would be sailing when she was dead, and there was
nothing under the whole enormous sky that was ever going to tell them.

The wind came off the water and smelled of cold iron. The light in the bucket was
very small. The sea was very old, and did not mind.

Isolde was at the rail with her hat off, which Mira would learn was the nearest
the captain ever came to church.

"Eleven years ago this week," Isolde said, to the water, "they hanged a man at
Saltmere for carrying a light. Four thousand people came to watch. He was asked,
as the custom is, whether he had anything to say."

She turned round. "Do you know what he said?"

"No."

"He said: *I left it out there. All of it, in one place, and I didn't hide it
well.*" The captain put her hat back on. "The way over the Fall. The other half
of the sky. The reason the lights went out the first time. And then he told four
thousand people to go and find it, and grinned at the Admiral while they did."

"They hanged him for that?"

"They hanged him for the light. The grinning was free." Isolde came down the deck
and the lamplight from the bucket went over her face and left it exactly as it
had been. "Then the Crown made it a crime to say the words in a tavern, which is
why every sailor on this coast can say them from memory."

Mira looked ahead, at water that had no road in it, and thought about a man who
had left something in it on purpose. "Why tell me?"

"Because you're going to want a reason bigger than a lamp," said Isolde, "and
that's the only one I've got."

She went forward to bother Marrow about the sails. Mira sat with the bucket
between her feet and the notebook on her knees, and did not write anything for a
while.

Then she put her hand flat on the warm brass, the way the vow said to, and said
the short form under her breath, because her hands were still shaking and it was
better than nothing.

*Light what you can. Count what you lose. Answer for what you see.*

The light did not change. She had not expected it to. It was a wick in a bucket on
a boat, and she was eighteen, and everything she had done tonight had cost her
something she could not get back — but the words sat in her chest like a swallowed
stone, and she found that she preferred carrying them to not.

Behind them, the tower stood on its cliff with the lamp room dark. Ahead, the
dark water. In the bucket, the light did not go out.
