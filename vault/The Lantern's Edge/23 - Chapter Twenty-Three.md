# Chapter Twenty-Three

The Warden kept the paper in a drawer with the pin.

He did not make a ceremony of it, which was worse. He took a key off a ring, unlocked a
drawer, took out a folded sheet that had been folded and unfolded so many times that the
creases had gone soft, and laid it on the desk between them with two fingers, the way a man
lays down a card he is not proud of.

"Read it," he said. "Take your time. I have had eleven years and it has not got any better."

It was not a grand document. That was the first thing. Mira had expected seals and ribbons
and a great deal of Latin, and what she got was one sheet of good paper with four lines of
writing on it and four signatures at the bottom, and the smallest of the four was the
Throne's.

*Be it recorded that the eight lights of the Reach, from the Second to the Eighth, are
withdrawn from the service of the coast, and their towers put into the keeping of the Crown,
for a term of eleven years and a day.*

*In consideration whereof the Crown undertakes to keep the coast by other means: by ships,
by guns, by the fleet at Saltmere, and by the diligence of its officers.*

*And the term shall end upon the ninth night of the ninth month, at which time the lights
shall be restored, or the covenant renewed, or the matter otherwise determined.*

*Signed at Saltmere in the eleventh year of the present peace.*

Mira read it twice.

"That's it," she said.

"That's it."

"That's the whole of it. That's eleven years of the dark. Four lines and a promise to use
ships."

"The Crown has a great many ships," said the Warden. "It has never once used them for this."

"What has it used them for?"

Halloran opened the second drawer and took out a smaller book, and put it down beside the
receipt, and turned it round with one finger.

"That is the office copy of the fleet's sailings for the last eleven years. Every ship the
Crown has sent to this coast since the dark. I had my clerk copy it out because I wanted to
know whether I had been a fool or a coward, and it turns out that a man can be both, and that
there is a figure for it."

Mira read the figure.

"Four," she said.

"Four. In eleven years. One was a survey, one was a transport carrying the auditors who came
to look at my ledgers, and two were wrecks that did not reach the coast at all." He closed the
book. "The coast has been kept, Miss Calloway, by a promise of four lines and four ships, and
the thing that actually kept it was a woman standing on a wall at Carrow with her hands out,
and a girl in a keeper's coat who did not know she was doing it."

She looked at the signatures. The Throne's was small and neat and looked like a stamp. The
Warden's was the largest of the four and the most legible, which was the thing she could not
stop looking at.

"You signed it."

"I witnessed it. There is a difference, and I have been explaining that difference to myself
since the day I signed it, and it takes about four seconds and then it stops working." Halloran put
his hands flat on the desk. "The other three were the Chancellor, the Admiral of the Fleet,
and a notary called Pell — no relation, I imagine. I signed as Warden because a document
about the eight lanterns without the Warden's hand on it is a document that a chapter house
can ignore, and the Throne wanted it to be impossible to ignore."

"And the ninth night of the ninth month."

"Nine days from tonight," said the Warden. "At which point the lights are restored, or the
covenant is renewed, or the matter is otherwise determined." He said the last phrase slowly,
the way a man says a phrase he has read a thousand times. "The Crown's clerks like *otherwise
determined*. It means the Throne may do as it likes and call it a determination."

Mira looked at the sheet of paper.

"Where's the ninth lantern in this?"

"Not in it. Your grandmother's deferral is a separate paper, signed quarterly, in my own
hand, and it is in that drawer, and it is the only thing in this study that I am not ashamed
of."

"Then why has the Ash been moving for eleven years if the dark was supposed to keep it off?"

"Because the dark was never the bargain," said Halloran. "The bargain was that the coast would
be kept. It has not been kept; it has been *quiet*, which is not the same thing, and eleven
years of quiet is a very long time to a man in an office." He folded the receipt along one of
its soft creases. "And now the term is nine days from ending, and the Admiral is relighting
what she can, and the thing outside my harbour has followed a girl up a road for six nights,
and every institution on this coast is going to do exactly what it did the last time, which is
sign something."

"You keep saying the coast was quiet," said Mira. "Not kept. Quiet. What kept it quiet?"

The Warden looked at her for a moment.

"Come down," he said. "I have been telling people the truth in this study since the dark and
it has never once worked. I would like to try showing somebody the machine."

---

The forge is at the bottom of the chimney and you go down to it by a stair cut in the rock,
and the stair is warm through your boots.

It gets hotter the whole way down, and the heat does not come from a fire. That was the thing
Mira could not get her hands to agree with. There was no fire. There was a wheel, forty feet
across, turning slowly in the middle of the floor, and the wheel was a machine and the machine
was a chimney, and the chimney went up through four hundred years of fortress, and at the
heart of the wheel, in a cradle of iron bands as thick as a man's leg, there was a stone the
size of a cart.

It was grey. It was warm. It was the least remarkable thing she had ever been afraid of.

"That is the mother," said Warden Halloran. "What is left of the sun that used to be inside
the Circlet. It has been in that cradle since the Fall and it will be in it when we are all
forgotten, and it gives off heat and nothing else, and no one has ever found a use for it that
did not go through this room."

There were nine sockets in the iron around the cradle. All nine were empty.

"Eight of those are in the towers," said the Warden. "And the ninth is in a canvas bag on a
boat in my harbour, in the brass cup of a lens with a chip out of it."

Mira looked at the sockets, and then at her own hands.

"An Ember is a fist of the mother," said Halloran. "Cut from the cradle and quenched, and
after that it is a lamp's heart and it does not go out. The cutting is a day's work for four
masters. The quenching is the part nobody writes down."

"What's the quenching?"

"A keeper's season," said the Warden. "Not the master's. A keeper stands at the trough and
gives a season into the glass, and the Ember takes it, and after that the Ember gives light
when it is lit and nothing when it is not, and it costs the keeper nothing more. That is the
whole of the trick that the nine towers are built on."

The wheel turned. Somewhere beyond it a forge-master struck something and the sound came
back off the roof.

"There hasn't been a new Ember in four hundred years," said Mira.

"In four hundred years there have been nine, and there have been nine because there have
never been more than nine keepers at once who would stand at a trough and pay a season for a
lamp that would not be theirs." Halloran put his hand on the rail. "And then, eleven years
ago, the Crown took the towers, and the first thing the Crown did was forbid the cutting of
any further Embers, and the second thing it did was put an Admiralty clerk at the top of that
stair with a key to the door."

"Why?"

"Because an Ember-forge is the only thing on this coast that a Crown cannot count," said the
Warden. "You can count a ship. You can count a tower and put a man in it. You cannot count a
girl at a trough who can make a light in a year's time, in a place you have never heard of,
with a ring on her hand and no licence. Nine of them made this coast. Nine hundred would make
it ungovernable."

Mira stood at the rail and looked down at the grey stone.

"There's a tenth thing," she said. "Now that you've shown me the machine. You've been walking
me to it for an hour and you have not said it."

"No," said the Warden.

"Say it."

Halloran looked at the wheel for a while.

"The light is a woman," he said. "It is will and memory and a shape held in the hands, and the
lamp is a chimney and a piece of a dead sun, and there has never been a god in any of it. Every
brother in this fortress above the rank of sergeant knows it. The catechism does not say
otherwise, exactly — that is the beauty of the thing, it says nothing that is untrue — but the
coast believes the light is a miracle, and the order has let it believe that for two hundred
years, because a miracle keeps people paying and a machine asks questions."

"And what does the order do with the paying?"

The Warden did not answer that. He turned and walked along the rail to the end of the gallery,
where a desk stood against the wall with a lamp on it and a man at the desk.

The man was seventy and had the face of somebody who had not slept properly since the autumn
of a year he did not name, and he had a ledger open in front of him and both hands flat on the
page as though he were keeping it from getting up.

"Brother Ostrand," said the Warden. "The line for the dark keeps. Show her the line."

"Father—"

"Show her the line."

The Seneschal turned the ledger round.

It was a page of ordinary accounts, in a fine small hand, and there were columns for the
quarter and the commandery and the sum, and about a third of the way down there was a line
that had been written every quarter for eleven years:

*Attendance at the dark keeps — eight — per quarter — 240 salt.*

Mira read it. Then she read it again.

"Two hundred and forty salt a quarter," she said. "For eleven years. For standing in eight
dark rooms."

"For keeping them dark," said Brother Ostrand. "For trimming wicks that are not lit and
signing the log and being seen to be there. It is not a bribe. The Crown calls it a stipend,
because a commandery cannot be left empty and a knight must be fed."

"And it pays for this place."

"It pays for a third of this place," said the Seneschal, "and it is the collateral behind the
salt-letters, because paper is only worth what stands behind it, and what stands behind ours
is the rent on the dark."

Mira put both hands on the rail.

She thought about a coast of towns that paid the order's letters because the order's word was
good, and a captain who took a letter instead of coin, and a woman at Carrow who had never
seen a salt-letter in her life and had been paying the whole of it a season at a time.

"So the dark was a business," she said.

"The dark was always a business," said Halloran. "Everything on this coast is. The only
question worth asking is who is paying, and the answer for eleven years has been: everybody,
and mostly the ones who could least afford it."

"My grandmother was paying."

"Yes," said the Warden. "She always was."

Rook had come down the stair behind them without a word, and he had been standing at the rail
for the whole of it, and now he said, in the flat formal voice he used when it cost him, "How
long has the order taken that money?"

"The second year," said Brother Ostrand. "The first year the Crown paid nothing and we sold
plate. In the second year a clerk came with a schedule and the Warden signed it and we ate."

Rook looked at the ledger.

"I have eaten at that table for twelve years," he said.

"Yes," said the Warden.

Rook did not say anything else. He put his hand flat on the rail and left it there, which was
what his hands did when he was holding something by not touching it, and Mira watched a knight
of the Emberwatch find out what his bread had been bought with, and it was worse than the
catechism, and worse than the bands, and it did not make a sound.

"On Thursday," said Halloran, "the Admiral will land with a writ that names this room. Not the
commanderies. Not the ledgers. This room, and that stone, and the trough where a keeper stands.
The visitation was never about heresy. It is a valuation, and it has taken eleven years, and I
have helped it every quarter by signing the thing that kept us alive."

"Then why show me?"

"Because in nine days the term ends," said the Warden, "and there are two ways it can end. The
Crown can take this forge and cut Embers by conscription for a hundred years, or a girl who
has already paid more than anyone should can stand at that trough and be the tenth. I have not
decided which I am going to allow. I am telling you that I have not decided, because you are
the only person in this fortress who has a right to know that much."

---

The Mercy Bell came in at noon, past the Ash, and the Ash let her.

Mira watched it from the wall of the Kindling with the band warm on her wrist and her heart
going like a bird in a box. The ship came up out of the south with her sails patched and her
yards badly squared — Isolde had never been a tidy sailor, she had been a *lucky* one — and
she passed within a mile and a half of the *Vigil* and the *Vigil* did not turn, did not fire,
did not put a boat down. The one window stayed lit. The two ships went past each other in the
afternoon like two men on a road who have agreed not to know each other.

"They're not stopping her," said Pell.

"No," said Mira.

"Why not?"

"Because she's not a light."

Pell thought about that for a while.

"That's horrible," he said.

"Yes."

Isolde Sable came up the Kindling's slip at the head of her crew with her hat on and her knife
in her belt and her coat brushed, and she looked around the yard at the forge-hands and the lay
brothers and the eight Crown marines at the door and the thirty knights on the wall, and she
said, "Well. This is a terrible place to be caught in. Who do I bribe?"

"Captain," said Mira.

"Miss Calloway." Isolde took her by the shoulders and looked at her for a moment, and then
let go, which was as much as that woman was ever going to do in public. "You are thinner and
you have a thing on your wrist that I do not like the look of. Where's my ship's boy?"

"Here, Captain," said Pell.

"You're alive. Good. You're worth more alive." She turned. "Teo. You look like a man who has
been staring at the sea for a week."

"I have been."

"Any good?"

"No," said Teo.

"Excellent. Consistency." Isolde's eye went along the wall and found the last of them, and
stopped. "Mr Sallow."

"Captain."

"You have been paid by the Crown to write down where I am."

"I have."

"Are you still doing it?"

Sallow took a moment. Then he took the oily card out of his coat, and the pencil from behind
his ear, and he held them both out.

"I'll take that as a no," said Isolde, and did not take them.

Behind her the crew came up the slip: eleven of them, in twos and threes, quiet, with the
water still coming off their boots. Nettle with her arms folded. A man called Hask with a
patch over one eye that he did not need. Two brothers, or men who had been called brothers for
forty years, which came to the same thing. Marrow came last with a coil over his shoulder and
stood at the back and said nothing, and the guides' woman from the cellar door — her name was
Sennet, Mira had learned, and she had a face like a closed shop — looked at them for a long
time and then went and found Odo Pike.

---

Odo Pike came up into the yard at two in the afternoon and looked at the crew of the Mercy
Bell the way a man looks at weather coming.

"These are the drowned," he said.

"These are the drowned," said Isolde.

"All of them?"

"Eleven of the fourteen. The other three had the sense to be born later." Isolde took her hat
off and put it back on, which was what she did instead of being serious. "They went down with
her off the Sill forty years ago and they came up in the morning and they have been sailing her
ever since. I bought the ship cheap. The crew came with it."

Odo Pike walked along the line of them.

He was not looking at their faces. He was looking at their wrists, and at the way the water
came off them indoors, and at the way none of them stood in a patch of sunlight.

"You know what a band is," he said.

"I know what a band is," said Marrow.

"Then you'll know why I'm asking. A band shows the road, and it takes hours, and the hours come
out of the middle of a life." Odo Pike stopped in front of him. "You've been dead. What's it
going to take out of you?"

Marrow looked at him.

"Nothing," he said.

The yard was quiet.

"Say that again," said Odo Pike.

"There's nothing in me to take," said Marrow. "I've been at the bottom forty years. I've got no
crew in my head and no water in my memory and no dying in me to remember. I came up with a rope
in my hands and a name and nothing else in the jar." He turned his hands over. "You want to
charge me hours. Charge them. There's no hour in me that anybody's using."

Odo Pike stood there for a moment.

"That," he said, "is the worst thing anybody has said to me in this yard."

"It's not a complaint."

"No," said Odo Pike. "That's why it's the worst thing."

He turned round and looked at the eleven of them, and then at Sennet, and then at the case on
the wall that he could not see from the yard and was thinking about anyway.

"Eleven bands," he said. "Eleven drowned. That is either the luckiest thing that has happened
to my trade in four hundred years or the largest bill anybody has ever run up on a coast, and
I have been doing this too long to think it's the first one."

"What's the bill?" said Isolde.

"I don't know," said Odo Pike. "That's what frightens me. Nothing on this coast is free, and a
band that takes nothing has to be owed somewhere, and I would very much like to know where
before I hand my road to a man who cannot be charged for it."

---

They did it in the guides' room, at the long table, with the door shut.

The crew's hands were bad. That was the thing Mira noticed: forty years of rope and salt and
no feeling in the fingertips, and every one of them had to be helped with the clasp. Sennet did
Marrow's. Rill did Nettle's, and Nettle looked at the boy's thin wrist and then at her own and
said, "You're not old enough to be in this trade," and Rill said, "I'm the third generation,"
which was not an answer and was the best one he had.

Odo Pike did not hand his own band over straight away. He stood at the end of the table with
it in his hand, turning it, and the green went round it slow, and he said, "This has four
hundred miles of road in it. The Sill, the Gull's Teeth, the Fold, the crossing at Candlewick
in the year the bridge went, and the way into Brinehollow that nobody's used since." He put it
down in front of Marrow. "If you take it, you walk my roads before you walk your own. That's
the rule. It'll take you a year and you'll not like some of it."

"I'll walk them," said Marrow.

"Aye," said Odo Pike. "I know you will. That's the trouble with your sort."

They put them on. Eleven bands, eleven wrists, and the room went green the way a room goes
when somebody opens a curtain: not light, nothing you could read by, just the slow oily
shimmer in eleven hoops at once and every one of them turning, turning, turning towards the
door.

And then the drowned men looked at the road.

Mira had seen Pell do it and had done it herself, and she knew what it was supposed to look
like: a person standing very still with their eyes open and their mouth a little open and one
hand out, like a man in the dark who has found a wall.

That was not what happened.

They went *down*. All eleven of them, at the same moment, without a word — not falling, not
fainting: eleven men and women going down onto their knees in a cellar with the road under the
floor, and their hands flat on the boards, and every one of them looking at something a long
way below the room.

Nettle made a sound.

"What is it?" said Isolde. "Nettle. What is it?"

"The road," said Nettle, "goes down."

"It's under the floor," said Mira. "It's always under the floor—"

"It goes *down*," said Nettle. "Past the cellar. Past the rock. It goes down under the water
and it keeps going, and there's a — there's a shelf, and past the shelf it's the deep, and the
road's still there, and it's still *there*, and I can see it." She looked up, and her face was
wet, and she was not a woman who wept. "I can see where we went down."

Hask had both hands flat on the boards and his one good eye shut.

"There's a ship," he said. "She's on her side in the mud with her masts in the road. There's a
boat still hanging in the falls. There's a man in the boat."

"Don't," said Sennet.

"He's not moving," said Hask, in a conversational voice. "He's been not moving for forty years.
He's got a coat on. He's got his hand up the way a man puts his hand up when he's been told to
hold something." He opened his eye. "That's my coat."

The two brothers had hold of each other's wrists and were not talking about it.

"We were in the water," said one of them, to nobody. "We were in the water and it was warm,
and we were holding on to the gunwale, and then there was the road."

"There was always the road," said the other.

"There was always the road," his brother agreed, "and nobody ever told us."

Mira looked at Odo Pike.

He was standing at the end of the table with his own hands empty and his mouth open, and he
said, very quietly, "Oh."

"Odo—"

"Four hundred years," said Odo Pike. "Four hundred years of road in these bands and not one of
us has ever seen past the shelf, because the living can't. The band shows the road as it is and
the road as it is goes down under the deep, and a living guide gets to the edge of the shelf
and the road goes dark, and we've always thought that was the limit of the glass." He sat
down, heavily, in the chair at the head of the table. "It isn't the limit of the glass. It's
the limit of us. We can't see it because we haven't been there."

"And they have," said Mira.

"They have," said Odo Pike. "They've been at the bottom of the Reach for forty years and they
came up with a rope and a name, and the band has put the road back under them." He put his
face in his hands for a moment, and then took it out again. "God help us. Eleven of them."

"What does it mean?" said Isolde.

"It means I have eleven guides who can walk the deep road," said Odo Pike, "and no idea in the
world what's at the end of it, and nine days before the term runs out and every light on this
coast is either lit or lost."

---

Sallow did not put his on.

He stood at the end of the table with the band in his hand for a long time while the others
went down onto their knees and came up again, and then he put it on the table, and said, "No."

Odo Pike looked at him.

"I'm not being difficult," said Sallow. "I am being, for the first time in forty years,
accurate. I have been paid six salt a week to watch this coast and write down what I see, and
the one thing I have never been able to do is *see it without narrating it*. If I put that on
my wrist I will have a road, and I will walk it, and I will tell everybody about it afterwards
in a voice like a man on a stage, and somebody will die while I am finding the words." He
straightened the band on the table with one finger, parallel to the edge, which was the
tidiest thing he had ever done with his hands. "Find me a different job. I'll row. I'll carry.
I'll count the boats. I'm good at boats; I've been counting them for the Crown for six
salt a week and I never once got the number wrong."

Marrow, who had not said a word since the road went down, said, "Take it."

Sallow looked at him.

"Take it," said Marrow, "and walk behind me, and say what you see, and stop pretending the
talking is the sin. The sin was the box on the quay." He held up his wrist with Odo Pike's
band on it and the green going round. "I've got the way and no words in my head. You've got
the words. That's the arrangement. Take it."

Sallow sat down at the table.

Then he picked the band up and put it on, badly, and Rill had to help him with the clasp.

---

Isolde stood at the window of the guides' room with her hat in her hands and watched her crew
in the yard below, eleven of them with green on their wrists, being taught to walk a road by a
boy of fourteen.

"They're mine," she said. "I bought the ship. The crew came with it."

"Yes," said Mira.

"Forty years I've had them. They don't get sick, they don't get old, they don't drown, and
they don't remember anything before the morning they came up, and I have never once asked them
what that's like because I am not a woman who opens a door she can't close." Isolde turned the
hat round in her hands. "And now a man in a cellar has given them a road and they are down
there on their knees in the wet looking at where they went down, and I find that I am —" she
stopped. "I don't have a word for it. I have a word for everything. I've got a word for a man
who steals a lifeboat."

"Say the true thing," said Mira.

"I'm jealous," said Isolde Sable.

Mira looked at her.

"Forty years, and I have never once known where I was going," said Isolde. "I've known where
the ship was going. That's a different thing and it's a cheaper one. And they've got a road
and a boy to teach them and a reason to be on it, and I have got a debt, a hat, and a
schedule." She put the hat on. "Right. What's the plan, and don't say a light, I've had a
bellyful of lights."

---

At dusk the guides went down the stair, and the drowned went with them, and Mira went as far
as the bottom because she could not think of a reason not to.

Halfway down, at the two hundredth step, where the stair turns and the wall has a niche in it,
Rill stopped.

His band was green and going round and his hand was out, and Mira could see the road herself
from where she stood, plain as a floor, and she could see the place where it went dark the way
a road goes dark at the edge of a lamp: about forty feet ahead, at the turn, a place where the
stones were there and she could not make herself see them.

"I can't," said Rill. "I've walked this stair since I was nine and I can't. There's the shelf."

Marrow came back up the steps and stood beside him.

"Then hold my hand," he said.

"I'm the guide."

"Aye," said Marrow. "And I've been down there. Hold my hand."

The boy held his hand, and the two of them walked up to the place where the road went dark and
stood in it, and Mira watched a fourteen-year-old guide see something no guide in four hundred
years had seen, which was the way on.

"Oh," said Rill.

"Aye," said Marrow. "That's what everybody says."

Odo Pike was on the stair above them with his hands on the rail, and he was not crying, and
his face was doing something that was worse than crying.

At the bottom, the road lay out under three feet of water with the gold of the lens on it and the band showed
her the same road plain, and the eleven drowned walked out along it in a line with their bands
covered and their hands on each other's shoulders, and the last of them was Marrow, and the
last of them was singing.

Not loud. Under his breath, four notes and then the same four notes, the way a man hums
something he does not know he knows.

"What is that?" said Mira.

Marrow stopped.

"I don't know," he said. "It came up with the road."

"Sing it again."

He sang it again, four notes down and four notes up, and Odo Pike, standing at the foot of the
stair with his hands empty, said in a cracked voice, "That's the Sill. That's the tide-bell at
the Sill. My grandmother sang that."

"Then how does he know it?"

"Because it's in the road," said Odo Pike.

---

They came back at midnight, all eleven, wet to the knees and quiet, and Odo Pike made them sit
down before he let any of them talk.

"Past the shelf," he said. "How far?"

"Two miles," said Nettle. "We turned at the second gatepost. It goes down and it keeps going
down, and the road is the same road — same width, same walls, same gateposts every mile.
There's a turn where a market used to be. There's a well with a roof on it."

"And the end of it?"

"There isn't one," she said. "Not tonight. It goes on past where we could walk. It goes
*under*, and it doesn't stop, and it's the same all the way, and there's a sound."

"What sound?"

Nettle looked at the table for a while.

"Ask Hask," she said.

Hask shook his head.

"Hask."

"It's a big sound," said Hask, "and I'll not be the one that names it in a room full of people
who have to go down that stair tomorrow."

Odo Pike did not push him. That was the thing Mira noticed about the guides: they never pushed
anybody about the deep water. He sat back and did the sum out loud instead, with his fingers
on the table, taking his time over the worst of it.

"Four miles in five hours down a road none of them has ever walked, and that's them, and
they're the best there has ever been at this." He moved his fingers. "The Fall is at the far
end of the Reach. Past Carrow, past the last light, past everything. That's the whole length
of the road and a day on the end of it, and at guide pace with eleven people who have to be
fed and rested, that's four nights there and four back."

"And it's nine nights until the term runs out," said Mira.

"It is," said Odo Pike. "And I have eleven guides who can see the way and a coast that needs
them here, and no time to spend on a road that ends in a place I have spent forty years
not going to." He put his hands flat on the table. "Which is the oldest joke in this trade
and it has never once been funny."

---

Mira wrote it up at the top of the stair, by the band, because the band did not need a pencil
to see by and she needed one to think with.

*The Crown's receipt is four lines long and it is in a drawer in the Warden's study with a pin
and a key. Eight lights withdrawn for eleven years and a day. In consideration whereof the
Crown undertakes to keep the coast by other ships, other guns, and the diligence of its
officers. Signed at Saltmere. Witnessed by the Warden, in the largest hand on the page.*

*The term ends on the ninth night of the ninth month. Nine days. At which time the lights
shall be restored, or the covenant renewed, or the matter otherwise determined.*

*The Warden took me down the chimney this morning and showed me the machine. A wheel forty
feet across and a grey stone the size of a cart in a cradle of iron, and no fire anywhere. That
is the mother. That is what is left of the sun that was inside the Circlet. Nine sockets in the
iron and all nine empty, and one of them is the Ninth, which is in the brass cup of a chipped
lens in a canvas bag on the Bell, and Teo is sleeping beside it because I asked him to.*

*An Ember is a fist cut from the mother and quenched in a keeper's season. That is the whole
trick the nine towers are built on and it has been done nine times in four hundred years,
because there has never been more than nine keepers at once who would pay a season for a lamp
that would not be theirs. The Crown forbade the cutting in the first month it held the towers.
You can count a ship. You cannot count a girl at a trough. I have read that in three books
since and it is the same sentence in all of them.*

*And the other thing, which Brother Ostrand showed me in a ledger with both hands flat on the
page. Attendance at the dark keeps, eight, per quarter, 240 salt, for eleven years and running.
The Crown has paid this fortress to stand in eight dark rooms and be seen to. It is not called a
bribe, it is called a stipend, and it is the collateral behind every salt-letter on this coast,
so every letter ever paid to a captain was a promise backed by the rent on the dark.*

*Rook put his hand on the rail and left it there for a long time and said only that he had
eaten at that table for twelve years. I did not have anything to say to that either.*

*The Mercy Bell came in past the Ash this afternoon and the Ash let her by, because she is not
a light. Isolde says she is jealous of her own crew. I have never heard her say a true thing
about herself before and I would rather she had not chosen this week to start.*

*The guides gave the drowned their bands. Eleven of them. It should not have worked and it
worked better than anything has worked since I lit the Ninth: the band shows the road as it
is, and the road goes down under the deep, and a living guide goes dark at the shelf because
he has never been there. They have. They went down on their knees in the cellar and saw where
they drowned.*

*Marrow has the tide-bell of the Sill in his mouth and cannot say how it got there. Odo Pike
says it is in the road. Sallow took a band after saying no, and I think it is the first time in
his life he has been given something he cannot price.*

*Cost, today: hours, and I do not know how many. The band is warm and the road is there and I
have started to be able to find it without looking, which Odo Pike says is how it starts.*

*Nobody has said the obvious thing out loud, so I will write it. If eleven drowned guides can
walk the deep road, then the deep road goes somewhere, and the only place it can go is under
the Fall. And the Brine Mother's contract comes due on the same night as the Crown's.*

*It was dusk and there were eleven lights on the road and every one of them was green.*
