# Chapter Seventeen

They left Candlewick on the ebb, with the lens in the boat and the ship standing out
for deep water, and it took an hour and a half to get the lens off the deck and into
the boat, which was an hour and twenty minutes longer than Isolde had allowed for it.

The *Tongue* was the *Mercy Bell*'s longboat, twenty-eight feet of her, with a bottom
like a wet leaf and no opinions, and she was called the *Tongue* because a bell has
one. The lens weighed as much as a horse and had lost a ring of glass and
gained a chip the size of a thumb, and it now showed the road twice, which was one
more road than there was. They built a crutch for it out of two oars and the
hatch grating and set it in the bow, and they cut the canvas chimney down from nine
feet to four and lashed it to the stem, so that the light came out low, an arm's
length over the water, like a plank laid down for them.

"Say it back to me," said Isolde.

"Sixty miles," said Mira. "Two nights if the weather holds. The road runs under
three feet of water the whole way and there is nothing on it that will take a keel
except the things that are on it, and those are the things I can't see. Barrowfoot
at the far end, under the Seventh's headland. You go round the outside of the
Cradle and you are three days and you will be there before us or you will not."

"And if it doesn't hold?"

"Then we lie up in the drowned country and wait for the wind to change."

"Wrong answer," said Isolde. "If it doesn't hold you come back. You do not lie up in
the drowned country. Nothing good has ever lain up in the drowned country."

She had the log open on the hatch and she wrote in it with the pencil stub, taking
her time, and then turned it round so Mira could read it. *Tongue away 4.10, ebb,
lens aboard, six hands. Expected Barrowfoot second night, weather allowing. If not
returned, I will come and get them and I will be extremely unpleasant about it.*

"There," said Isolde. "Now you have to be there for the arithmetic."

Six hands. Mira counted them on the ladder down: Pell, Marrow, Sallow, Rook, Teo,
herself. She counted the things she was taking on the second rung: the Ember in its
hood, the lens, the ring, the notebook. Four. She had gone to sea with less.

Rook had his arm strapped across his chest and stood at the foot of the ladder in a
way that was meant to look like waiting and was in fact a man deciding how to ask
for something.

"I will row," he said.

"You will sit," said Isolde. "You have one arm and a shoulder like a bag of gravel
and I have seen you try to be useful. Sit in the stern and steer. A tiller wants one
hand and no opinions and you are qualified in exactly that."

Rook considered this. "I will steer," he said, and got in, and sat in the stern like
a man taking a post.

Sallow came down last with his coat over his arm and his two swords and looked at
the boat, and then at the water, and then at the boat again.

"I want it noted," he said, "that I have been paid for worse evenings. Not many.
But it is noted."

"Noted," said Isolde. "Get in."

She did not make a speech. She stood at the rail with her hat on and her arms folded
and watched them pull away, and when they were twenty feet off she said, in the flat
carrying voice she used for orders, "Teo Vane."

Teo looked up.

"Your eyes are on my ship," said Isolde. "Bring them back."

---

The first hour was wonderful, and Mira was ashamed of how wonderful it was.

The lane came out of the stovepipe low and steady and lay on the water like a road
of poured metal, and the boat went along it at four miles an hour with Sallow and
Marrow at the oars and Rook steering and Pell kneeling in the bow with the hood rope
in his hand, and there was a road under them. A road with a wall. The wall came up
out of the water on the port side for a hundred yards and then went under and came up
again, cut stone, four courses of it, with weed hanging off it like a beard.

Pell put his hand down into the water and touched it.

"It's real," he said, in a voice that had gone strange. "It's a *wall*, it's got
mortar in it."

"It's a copy," said Sallow, from the oars. "Everything on this coast is a copy. That
wall is a copy of a wall."

"That doesn't mean anything," said Pell.

"No," Sallow agreed. "It's the middle of the night and I'm rowing. Very little means
anything."

The ruts were the thing Mira could not get over. Two of them, deep, four inches of
gold in the light, running away from her at the width of a cart, and they were full
of the tracks of carts. Somebody had come down this road with a load on and gone
back empty for four hundred years' worth of Tuesdays.

"How far does it go?" said Pell.

"To the Kindling," said Teo, from the bow. "Under it. There's a stair."

He had said it before, on the deck, in the light, with his hands open. He said it now
the way a man says a thing he has already said, and nobody answered him, and after a
while he said, "It's the same, isn't it. It's the same as it was."

"Same as what?" said Sallow.

"The road," said Teo. "It's the same."

Mira kept her eyes on the lane and did not look at him.

---

She found the copy at the third mile, because she was counting.

Counting was what she did instead of being frightened, and there was nothing else to
do in the bow of a boat at midnight except hold a light and count, so she counted the
gateposts. There was a gatepost at the third mile on the starboard side, square, with
the cap knocked off it, and a hundred and forty feet further on there was the same
gatepost: square, cap knocked off, the same grey chip out of the same corner.

"Stop," she said.

Marrow stopped rowing. The boat went on for its own length and lay still.

"Look at that post," said Mira. "And that one."

"There's two," said Pell.

"There's one post and the light has put it there twice," said Mira. "Look at the
chip. That is not a repaired post. That is a copy of a post."

Nobody said anything for a moment. The light went on lying out across the water,
gold and steady, and two roads went away from the bow, and at twenty feet they were
a hand's width apart and at a hundred they were a boat's width, and at a mile she
could not tell them apart at all.

"So which one's ours?" said Sallow.

Mira looked at the two roads. Then she looked at the water between them, which was
grey and flat and had nothing in it, and then she looked at the place where the true
road should have been, and saw it: a shape the light could not get into. A dark
patch on the gold, ten feet long, with an edge to it.

"That one," she said. "The one with the hole in it."

"That's the *broken* one," said Pell.

"It's the true one." She was aware of everybody in the boat looking at her. "The
light doesn't show the sea as it is now. It shows the road as it *was*, and it does
it from what the glass remembers, and the glass remembers four hundred years ago
because that's the last time anybody aimed it. Everything that's on the road now —
a wreck, a rock, the roof of somebody's house, a crab pot — isn't in the glass's
memory. So it doesn't get lit. It comes through as a hole."

"So the copy's the perfect one," said Sallow slowly.

"The copy's the perfect one," said Mira. "The copy's the road with nothing on it.
The copy is four hundred years ago with nobody in it."

She looked down at the dark shape under ten feet of gold and thought: *you do not
steer by the road. You steer by the damage.*

"Right," she said. "Nobody's off this boat tonight except Marrow. We go by the
holes. And the two roads only stay honest for twenty feet, so we go twenty feet at
a time and Pell lifts the hood off the far end and puts it back on, and it will take
us two nights and it will be the worst two nights of my life, and Sallow, before you
say anything —"

"Nothing," said Sallow. "I'm rowing."

---

They went twenty feet at a time.

It was not the worst two nights of her life, but it was in the conversation. The
lane would go out, they would take three strokes, the lane would come back, and Mira
would read the twenty feet of road in front of them for holes and call it like a
woman calling a dance, and Rook would put the tiller over, and the boat would slide
into the next twenty feet of a road that had been built by people who expected to
use it for a hundred years.

By the sixth hour her voice had gone to gravel and her hands, from holding the hood
rope and passing it to Pell and taking it back, had gone to something she did not
like to look at.

"Wall to port," said Teo.

He had been saying it all night, from the bow, with his hand on the gunwale.

"Wall to port," he said again. "It's close. You're a foot off it."

"You can't see it," said Sallow, not unkindly. "The hood's on."

"I can hear it."

Sallow opened his mouth and shut it again. And then, because he was a man who could
not leave a thing alone, he said, "Do it again."

"There's a gap coming," said Teo. "In about — six strokes. Then the wall picks up
again and it's further off, by two feet. And there's something under the water to
starboard at six strokes, something soft. Weed on stone."

Pell lifted the hood.

There was a gap in the wall in six strokes. And on the starboard side, at the
twenty-foot mark, a black shape lay on the gold with weed on it, and Rook put the
tiller over and the boat went by it with six inches to spare.

Nobody said anything for a while. Then Pell, in a small voice, said, "How?"

"Walls make a noise," said Teo. "There's a swell coming down from the north-east
and it's got a long way to run before it hits anything, and when it hits the wall,
the wall goes *tock*. A wall in three feet of water makes a shape in the water about
a foot and a half off it, and the *tock* comes before the wave does. A gap in a wall
makes no noise at all. So you listen for the *tock* and you listen for where it
stops."

"Since when could you do that?"

"Since I was six," said Teo. "I've just never had a reason to say it out loud where
anybody could hear me."

The lane came back and Mira read it and called it and the boat went on.

And on, and on. And she did the sums in her head, because she was counting: sixty
miles, two nights. Four miles an hour is fifteen hours if nothing goes wrong. Twenty
feet at a time, twenty feet at a time, and the light burning at her feet like a small
furnace, and the price of it paid in a currency she would not know the rate of until
the morning.

---

It happened in the drowned village, which the sign called COLDHARBOUR, with the
water up to the middle of the letters.

They saw the roofs first — a hundred roofs under three feet of water, grey gold in
the light, with chimneys on them, and the road going between them where the street
had been. From the boat, at that hour of the morning, with the sky starting to show
grey behind the headland, it was the most beautiful thing she had ever lit.

"God," said Sallow.

The chapel had a bell-frame, and the bell was still in it, three feet full of water.

They tied the *Tongue* to the frame and sat in the gallery with the boat under the
window and the light hooded, and the water went down the steps and into the aisle and
sat in the pews. Pell pulled the bell rope and the bell went *thunk* instead of
ringing, because it was full of water, and everybody laughed too loudly, and then
stopped laughing, because it was quiet in Coldharbour and the quiet had been there a
long time.

Marrow stood in the street with the water at his chest and looked at the houses.

Mira came and stood beside him.

"Do you remember it?"

"No."

"Does it look like anywhere to you?"

"It feels like the bottom," said Marrow, and went and got the oars in.

---

They ate a little and slept in turns, and Mira did not sleep.

She sat in the gallery with her back against the bell-frame and the Ember in her lap
and the notebook open, and she wrote down the rule about the holes, and the rule about
the twenty feet, and the rule about the wall making a noise, which she wrote down in
Teo's words and then, under it, in her own: *he cannot see it. He has not been able
to see it since the deck at Candlewick and he has been calling the road all night by
sound and by calculation and he has not made a mistake.*

Then she sat and looked at that for a while, and then she went and found him.

He was at the top of the chapel steps with his knees up and his arms on them,
looking out at the grey water over the drowned street, and she sat down next to him
and did not say anything, and the two of them watched a village that nobody had
looked at in four hundred years.

"Tell me what it looks like," said Teo.

"There's a street," said Mira. "It goes down from here to a well, and the well's
still got a roof over it, and somebody built a little wall round the well so the
children wouldn't fall in. There's a boat tied up at the second house along, a
rowboat, on its side. There's a sign over a door that says BAKER, and a shutter
hanging off one hinge, and behind the door there's a room with a table in it and the
water's at the table's knees."

"Are there chairs?"

"Four."

"Somebody was going to want four chairs," said Teo. "Somebody stood in that room and
counted four people and went and bought four chairs, and the fourth one's for a
child, and the child was too small to sit still and the mother had a cloth on it."

Mira looked at him.

"You've been doing that all night," she said. "You've been doing it since the deck."

"Doing what?"

"Making things up and telling me them in a voice like the truth."

Teo did not move. Then he put his hand on the stone, which was a step, and left it
there.

"On the deck at Candlewick," he said. "When the lens went over and you took it. I
could see the un-ships in the lane. I could see them all the way to the horizon, and I could see the ones on the
bottom with their crews still at the oars, and I could see the road, and there was a
stair under the Kindling."

"I know. You said."

"I can't see any of it now." He said it flatly, the way he said prices. "I can see
the water. I can see the light on the water. I can't see what it *shows*. There's
nothing there when I look. There's just sea, and the sea's the same as it always is,
and I've been sitting in the bow all night listening to a wall and doing sums in my
head and calling it like I can see it."

Mira found that she had her hand over her mouth.

"How long," she said, through it.

"Since I said the thing about the ships. Twelve. There were thirteen." He shrugged.
"After that the light went out and my hands were cold and I put my head up and looked
at the road and there was nothing there, and then you asked me how many ships and I
said eleven —"

"You said eleven."

"Because eleven is a number that sounds like a man who can see," said Teo. "And
because you had a lens on the deck and a captain behind you and a knight with a book
of laws and the whole crew looking at me, and if I'd said *I can't see it* then the
last thing anybody had would have gone out of the room."

"The last thing anybody had," said Mira.

"Wasn't the road," said Teo. "It was that we'd got something for it. That we'd paid
for something and got something. I don't know." He rubbed his thumb along the stone.
"I'm not good at this. I've been not saying it for two days and it's like carrying a
box."

"You could have said it to me."

"Alone," said Teo. "In the dark. Not in front of the crew."

Mira sat with that.

"There's a rule," she said at last. "In the light. You can't lie in it."

"That's a rule for keepers."

"It's a rule for *light*." She turned and looked at him properly. "You held a lane
with no ring, Teo. You did a thing last night that the order has a column for and no
word for, and it cost you the only thing you had that nobody else on this ship could
do, and then you got in a boat and came out here and did the work anyway by
listening to a wall. You have not lied to me once since Candlewick. You've just
not said a thing, and I've not asked, and if you think that's the same as lying then
you have not been paying attention to the company you're keeping."

Teo looked at her.

"I'm going to say something badly," he said.

"Everybody does."

"I can't see the road. I'd like to be the one who sees it. I'd like it a lot. But
the thing I actually want is for you to be looking at me when the sun comes up, and
I've been sat here since midnight working out that I don't have a good reason for
that, and I've decided I don't need one."

The sky behind the headland was going from grey to the colour of a mussel shell.

"Teach me the wall," said Mira.

"What?"

"The *tock*. Teach me to hear it. Because I can see twenty feet and you can hear a
hundred, and one of us is going to be tired by the second night."

So he did. He put her hand flat on the gunwale and told her to stop listening with
her ears and listen with her hand, and that the *tock* was not a sound so much as a
thing arriving, and that a wall in shallow water made a shape you could feel the way
you felt a step in the dark. It took him an hour to teach her and she got it in the
last twenty minutes of the hour, and when she felt it — one small flat knock coming
up through the wood, then the wave, then the knock again — she laughed out loud in a
drowned chapel with the water in the pews, and Teo put his forehead against her
shoulder and stayed there.

Then she got the Ember out to check it, by habit, the way she had checked it a
thousand times: hood up, a sniff at the vent for the oil and the hot brass and the
faint sweet stink a wick makes when it is about to go.

Nothing.

She held it there for a moment. She knew exactly what it smelled like — she could
have written him a page of it, oil and hot brass and that sweet edge at the end —
and there was nothing at the end of the sentence. Again.

She put the hood back on.

"All right," she said. "Second night."

---

The second night was faster, because they were better at it, and worse, because the
frame had bent.

It happened at the third hour, in the last of the drowned town. The lane had gone
out for the count of three and Pell had lifted the hood and the road had come back
and Rook had put the tiller over, and the boat had gone on into the dark and hit
something with a noise like a dropped plate.

Not a rock. A chimney.

It came up out of the water at the end of a roof with the pot still on it, square and
ordinary and four hundred years old, and it took the *Tongue* on the starboard bow at
an angle, and the boat stopped and the lens kept going, and for one second Mira
watched a thing the weight of a horse lean out over three feet of water.

She got her hand on it.

It cost her nothing. Eight seconds, a lens in a bent frame, and not one thing gone
out of her — and she understood, with her hands full of a horse's weight of glass,
that the light did not sell by the second. It sent a bill. The bill came whenever it
liked and it was never itemised, and she had been paying it in lumps since the tower
and calling it a fair price because the alternative was to stop holding things.

Pell went over the side.

He went over quietly, with his mouth open and no noise at all, and Marrow was in the
water before the boat had finished rocking, and got him by the collar and stood up
out of three feet of sea with the boy under one arm like a sack.

"I'm all right," said Pell, coughing. "I'm all right, I've got the — I've got the
hood rope —"

He had the hood rope. He had held onto it.

They got him in and got the lens back on the crutch and it had not cracked, and the
frame had not broke, but it had bent, and now the lane came out of the stovepipe at
an angle of about four degrees to the left of where the lens was pointed.

They found that out at the next hood-lift, when the road came up in the wrong place
and Rook steered into the wall.

"Three feet," said Mira. "The light's lying three feet to port. Not the road. The
light."

"Then how do we know anything?" said Pell, who was wrapped in Sallow's coat, shaking.

"We aim wrong on purpose," said Mira.

And they did. She read the road three feet to the left of where it was, and called
it, and Rook put the tiller over the other way, and the boat went down the middle of
a road that nobody in the boat could see properly, for another nine hours, at four
miles an hour, twenty feet at a time.

---

The road ended at a stone.

It was grey light by then, the real grey, the kind that comes up out of the water
instead of down out of the sky, and the lane had gone narrow and then wide and then
there was a stone standing up out of the sea with a mark cut in it, and past the
stone the water went from three feet to eight to thirty, and the gold ran out to
either side and stopped being a road and became a bank of light and then nothing.

Marrow shipped the oars.

Behind them, sixty miles of drowned country. Ahead of them, a bay, and a headland
with a tower on it that had been dark for eleven years, and a town at the foot of the
headland with its chimneys showing.

And out in the bay, with her yards square and her boats down and her lanterns lit and
her pennant hanging in the still air, the *Vigil*.

She was not coming in. She had been there for some time. There was a boat in the
water between them and the town that had a lantern on a pole and men in it who were
not rowing.

"They knew," said Rook. His voice had gone very quiet. "The road is on a chart. There
is one place it comes out."

Teo was in the bow with his hand on the gunwale.

"What does it look like?" he said.

Mira looked at the Crown's flagship and the boats and the lanterns and the town, and
at the whole grey morning, and she thought about a lens that had taken a man's sight
for a small kind sentence, and about a light that reads certainty and not truth, and
about how easy it would be — how *easy*, how little it would cost her, how little it
had cost him — to be the one who says the kind thing.

"Nothing," she said. "Fog on the water. Can't see the town yet."

"Fog," said Teo, and his shoulders came down half an inch.

"Fog," said Mira.

She picked up the notebook.

*Sixty miles of road under three feet of water, and it took two nights, and we came
out at the stone.*

*The lens is chipped and the frame is bent and the light lies three feet to port, so
we aim wrong on purpose, and we will be aiming wrong on purpose all the way to the
Kindling.*

*Cost, so far: I cannot smell a lamp. Oil, hot brass, the sweet edge a wick makes
when it is about to go out — gone, the way the kitchen went. I found out in a chapel
by checking a thing I have checked ten thousand times and getting nothing back. From
now on I look at a flame instead of smelling it, and I will be slower, and one day
that will matter.*

*Teo cannot see what the light shows. He has not been able to since the deck. He told
me in a chapel and it took him two days and it was the bravest stupid thing I have
ever watched a man do, and then he taught me to hear a wall, and I can hear it now,
and I am going to need it.*

*And I have just lied to him. First time. It went out of my mouth as easy as
breathing and he believed it because he cannot check, and I did it to keep him quiet,
and I would do it again, and that is the part I am writing down.*

*There is a Crown ship in the bay and she has a name I am not going to write down,
and she has been there a while, and there are boats out with lanterns on poles and
the men in them are not rowing.*

*So much for the arithmetic.*
