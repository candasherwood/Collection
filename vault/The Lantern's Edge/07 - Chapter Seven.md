# Chapter Seven

The Quiet came at the turn of the middle watch, and Mira heard it arrive, which was
the wrong way round.

She was forward with the bucket between her feet, doing what she did every hour now:
lifting the canvas hood an inch, checking the wick, putting it back. The wick was
fine. The wick was always fine, and she checked it anyway, because it was the only
thing on the ship she could fix.

Then the slapping stopped.

Not one slap, all of them: the little irregular knock of water along the bow that
she had stopped noticing somewhere around noon, gone out of the world between one
breath and the next. The rigging went still. A block that had been ticking against
the mast all day gave one last tick and thought better of it.

Then the bell on the foredeck rang once, in air that had no wind in it at all.

"Quiet," said Isolde, from the quarterdeck, in a voice pitched for a room. "All
hands on deck. Nobody lights a lamp. Nobody rings that bell. Nobody speaks above
this." She held her hand up at the height of her own mouth. "Pell. Keep the hood on
the light and stand by it with your back to it. Nettle. Count the crew, then count
them again, and tell me if the numbers disagree."

Mira looked over the side.

The sea had gone the colour of a floor. Not flat — that would have been a relief; it
was flat the way a puddle is flat after the rain has stopped, with the sky in it and
nothing moving in the sky. Two feet out from the hull there was no reflection at
all, and the *Mercy Bell* sat in the middle of it like a coin in a plate.

Pell had his back to the bucket and his shoulders hunched round it, and he was
talking to the canvas, low and steady, the way a man talks to a horse in a
thunderstorm.

Nettle went round the deck counting the crew on her fingers and came back and said
the number, and Isolde said the number back to her, and neither of them looked
comforted by the agreement.

And one of the hired men — the one who had looked into the light too long on the way
out of Carrow — had stopped coiling rope and stood at the rail with his hands empty,
looking down into water that had no reflection in it, humming. The tune had changed.
It had been a thing with a hornpipe in the middle of it when they left the mole, and
it was slower now, and lower, and it went round the same four notes without getting
anywhere.

"Since the light," Nettle said, quietly, before Mira could ask. "He does that. It
isn't a complaint and it isn't a fit. He's keeping time."

"Time for what?"

"Nobody's asked him," said Isolde, from the wheel, "and I'd be obliged if nobody
starts."

"Teo," Isolde said. "Wheel."

He was already there.

---

Mira went below for the spare wick and found the knight in the hold.

There was no lamp down there. The *Mercy Bell* kept her stores in a loft under the
foredeck with a hatch and a ladder and a smell of tar and old rope, and Rook was
sitting on a coil of cable in the middle of it, in his shirt, with his surcoat
folded across his knees, in the dark.

"Ser Rook."

"Keeper."

"You're sitting in the dark."

"I am permitted an hour of it a day." He said it as though it were a ration. "A
knight of the watch keeps no light of his own. It is in the rule."

Mira thought about several answers and chose the plainest. "That's a strange rule
for a man whose job is lanterns."

"It is the only one I have never broken," said Rook, "so I have kept to it, and I
would rather not discuss it in a cupboard."

She found the wick-box by feel, and then found the lantern-hook, and then sat down
on the other end of the coil because the ship's floor was moving and she had
discovered that she was tired in her bones.

His kit was laid out beside him on the deck, because he had been folding it: one
bag, open. A comb. A spare shirt, grey, folded in three. A whetstone in a leather
sleeve. A small book with a broken spine. A spoon. A strip of flannel. Nothing else.

"You have no money," she said.

"No."

"At all?"

"A knight of the watch carries none. The commandery keeps the ledger." He said it
without pride, the way he said everything. "When I need something, I sign for it and
the order pays, and the order's word is honoured in every port on this coast because
four hundred years of my brothers have died rather than break it."

"Four hundred years of promises is a lot of money."

"It is a great deal of money," Rook agreed. "It is the great scandal of my order and
I have never been able to explain it well, so I usually do not try."

The ship groaned, softly, in a way she had not heard it do before. Up on deck
somebody's boots went past the hatch.

"Why are you telling me?" Mira said.

"Because you are going to ask me something before this is over, and I would like you
to know what I have to answer with." He set the surcoat down. "I have a blade I do
not own, a bag with no coin in it, and a set of words I said once at dawn when I was
fourteen. That is the whole of my estate."

"What words?"

"Three questions. Will you keep what is not yours. Will you go where you are sent.
Will you be seen." He looked at the dark where the hatch was. "The answer to each is
*I will*. That is why I say it instead of yes. I have spent the words once already
and they are the only thing I have that is entirely mine."

Mira sat with that for a while.

"Will you help me keep it lit?" she said.

The knight was quiet for long enough that she heard the bell tick, once, up on deck,
with no hand near it.

"I will," said Rook.

"Even though they sent you to put it out."

"I was sent to put out a lamp," he said. "I have been to the Kindling. I have knelt
in a hall with a rite read over me and I have trimmed nine wicks with these hands,
and I will tell you a thing that will cost me my cloak if it is repeated." He
stopped. "Do you want it?"

"Yes."

"The auditors say we worship the Ember. The Crown says it in the taverns and the
Throne says it in the counting house and it is a useful thing to say, because a
heretic's lands are forfeit." He turned his hands over. "I have knelt in front of a
lit lantern and I do not know what I met there. A man in my order is not permitted
to say that. I have just said it to a girl in a boat."

Mira did not tell him it was all right. It was plainly not all right.

"Then we're both going to be in trouble," she said, "and we can be in it at the
same time, which is cheaper."

Rook laughed — one syllable, surprised out of him, entirely undignified — and put his
surcoat back on.

---

Up on deck, the Quiet was doing what a Quiet does, which is nothing, patiently.

Teo had the wheel with both hands and his hat jammed down and his eyes not on the
sea at all but on the place where the sea should have been reflecting the sky, and
wasn't.

"Two points off the bow," he said, when Mira came up. "There. See the grey that's a
bit more grey?"

"That's water."

"That's a ship," said Teo. "A big one. She's not moving and she's not going to, and
if we go where she is we'll be in her in about nine minutes and I would rather not."

"You can see them."

"I can see where they aren't," he said, and did not look at her. "It's not a gift.
It's like being able to hear a noise in a house. After a while you can't not."

So he steered. The *Mercy Bell* went through the Quiet at a walking pace with her
canvas hanging dead and her helmsman reading the traffic of a sea that had ended
four hundred years ago, and Mira stood at the mast with her shoulder against it and
both hands round the bucket and did the only work she had: she kept the light
steady.

It took an hour to clear the fleet. It took another to clear the second one.

"Two fleets," Isolde said, at the rail, in the flat voice of a woman filing a
complaint. "Nobody tells you there's two."

"There's four," said Teo.

"Of course there are."

At the end of it the wind came back the way a person comes back into a room — all at
once, from the wrong door — and the canvas filled, and the deck came alive under
them, and every soul aboard let out a breath they had been holding for three hours.

Isolde found Mira at the mast.

"Walk with me," she said, "and try to look like cargo."

They went aft, past the wheel, to the little flat deck behind the mizzen where the
captain did her thinking and nobody else was allowed to stand.

"Your favour," Mira said. "You said you'd name it."

"I did say that." Isolde took her hat off, looked at it, and put it back on.
"Here it is. When we reach the Fall, you come with me to the water. You stand in
front of her in your grandmother's coat with the ring on your hand and you tell her
no."

Mira waited for the rest of it. There wasn't any.

"That's it?"

"That's it. She has been holding my ship up for eleven years on a promise I can't
pay and can't refuse, and she is scrupulous, and scrupulous is worse than cruel
because she will hear an argument." Isolde's jaw worked. "She won't take a refusal
from me. I owe her. She will take one from a keeper who means it — that's the whole
of her nature, she collects what's given, and she can't collect a no unless
somebody says it to her face."

"And if she doesn't like the no?"

"Then she takes the ship, and me, and it will be over quickly, and you will be a
hundred miles west with a light in a bucket, which was the deal." The captain
shrugged. "I've had worse deals. I've signed worse deals. Once I signed one in ink."

Mira looked at her. In the lantern light from the bucket the captain's face was the
face of a woman doing sums and getting the same answer she always got.

"Why did you tell me the truth?" Mira said. "You could have asked me for anything. I
was standing in a tower with my hands shaking."

"Because I have been dead," said Isolde, "and the one thing I learned is that you
should spend the truth where it will keep. You'll say no to her. Not for me. For the
road." She put her hand on the rail and looked out. "And because if I'd lied to you
about it, you'd have found out at the Fall, and then you'd have said yes."

---

They came on the Eighth at the end of the first watch, and Pell saw it from the
masthead because Pell was light and because nobody else would go up.

"Light!" he shouted. "Light off the port bow! Low and steady!"

Isolde was on the rail before the echo of it. "Where?"

"Where the chart says the Fold is!"

Mira got up. She got up with the bucket in both hands and went to the rail and
looked, and there it was, low on the black water, four miles off and steady as a
candle in a window.

Wren's Fold. The Eighth lantern. Dark for eleven years, per every chart and every
copy of every chart, dark and doused and its keepers three generations Hollowed at
their posts with the wick still trimmed.

Somebody had lit it.

And out past it, further east, filling a quarter of the horizon with a grey that was
a shade more grey than the sky, something enormous was moving toward it with no
light on it at all.
