# Chapter Twenty-Five

They did the exchange on the quay at nine in the morning, at Mr Vey's folding table, with the
harbour behind them grey as a plate.

The case came off the *Surety* under marine escort and was set on the table and opened, and the
Ninth's Ember sat in its cradle of grey felt next to three others, and it was warm, and it was
Mira's, and it had been in the Circlet.

"The Crown does not sell," said Vey, before anybody could ask. "The Crown is not a shop. The Crown
*exchanges*. The difference is a form and a great deal of self-respect." He turned the ledger
round. "I have valued the Ember at one lugger, ninety feet, the *Mercy Bell*, of this port. I
have valued it at that because it is the only number on this quay, and I will be laughed at in
Saltmere for a year, and I have decided that I do not mind."

"Value it at two," said Isolde.

"I have already written it down," said Vey. "You may have the pencil if you would like to try."

Isolde Sable stood on the quay in her good coat with her hat in her hand and looked at the ledger
the way a woman looks at a tide.

"It's a fair price," she said. "That's the trouble with it."

"The Admiral left a warrant with me before she sailed," said Vey, to nobody in particular. "It
says that if a person of good standing offers the assessed value of the surety, I am to take it
and write *redeemed*. She wrote it at four this morning and she did not explain herself, and I
have been carrying it around all breakfast feeling like a man with a stolen spoon."

"Captain—" said Mira.

"Don't." Isolde did not look at her. "You gave a stone to an admiral to keep the coast for nine
days, and the coast has had one of them, and now the stone's for sale and there's one thing on
this quay that Saltmere wants more than it wants an Ember, and it's my ship. So." She took the
pencil out of Sallow's ear, which was the only pencil on the quay that anybody trusted, and
signed the leaf, and put the pencil back.

"Captain," said Vey, "there is a clause."

"There is always a clause."

"The crew." Vey's pen stopped. "The *Mercy Bell* was raised with her company aboard her. The
company is appurtenant. In law, a ship's people go with the ship."

The harbour went very quiet.

"Say that again," said Marrow.

"The people of a ship go with the ship," said Vey. "I did not write the clause. It is four
hundred years old and it is there because a ship without a crew is a wreck, and the Crown does
not buy wrecks."

Marrow looked at Isolde.

"Captain," he said.

"I heard him."

"You're selling us."

"I'm selling the *ship*," said Isolde, "and the ship is selling you, and I have been telling you
for forty years that a crew is a thing that happens to a hull, and now a clerk has written it
down and made it legal, and I find I have nothing clever to say about it." She turned round.
"Sign the rest of it, Mr Vey. The whole of it. And put the hour in, because I would like the hour
in."

"It is nine and twelve," said Vey, and wrote it.

The Ember came out of the case into Mira's hand. It was warm and it weighed about as much as a
fist, and it had been in the Circlet, and it was hers again, and the price of it was standing on
the quay behind her in fourteen pairs of boots, eleven of them wet.

Isolde put her hat on.

"Right," she said. "Crew of the *Mercy Bell*. You belong to the Crown as of twelve minutes past
nine and I am told that makes you navy, and I want it on the record that I have known better men
than the navy and worse men than you, and none of them could find a channel in the dark." She
looked along the line of them. "You'll be paid. You'll be fed. There'll be a man with a list who
tells you where to stand, and you'll stand there, because that is what you are now: eleven people
who stand where they are told. And when the wind comes up and the fool with the list is
frightened, you will do the thing you have always done, which is the ship, and the coast, and
each other, in that order, and no one will ever write it down." She put her hands behind her
back. "Marrow. Take them aboard."

Marrow did not move.

"Take them aboard," said Isolde, "before I say something true."

---

The wind came at noon, the first wind in five days, out of the south-west, and it came with a
ship.

Mira was on the wall with the Ember in the canvas bag and the band warm on her wrist when the cry
went up from the tower, and she looked where the boy was pointing and saw a black ship standing
in from the seaward quarter under a full press of sail, and three lights at her masthead.

Red lights.

They were the colour of a forge at night seen through a crack in a door. They did not flicker.
They did not waver in the wind. They burned flat and steady on a ship that was making nine knots
with a reef in her topsail, and there was no flag on her anywhere.

And nine miles out, the Ash's window went dark.

Not out. *Away.* Mira watched it happen: the one lit window, that had sat in the grey for four
days and followed them from Carrow, went out like a lamp carried past a window, and came back a
minute later a mile further south, and then another mile, and then it was a pinprick, and then it
was gone into the murk entirely, and the sea where it had been was only sea.

"Where's it going?" said Pell. "Miss. Where's it going?"

"I don't know," said Mira.

"It's *running*," said Pell.

"Yes," said Mira. "It's running."

Marshal Hale came up the wall stair at a run with her sword not drawn and four sergeants behind
her, and looked at the black ship for four seconds, and said, "Get the Warden. Get him now, and
get him up here, and don't let him come up the outside stair."

"He'll come the way he always—"

"*Don't let him come up the outside stair*," said Hale, and then, to a sergeant, "Twelve men at
the gate, shut it, and then open it again when I tell you. Not before."

The Warden came up the inside stair in his study coat with his spectacles in his hand, and he
stood at the parapet and looked at the red lights and did not say anything for a long time.

"Father," said Hale. "What is it?"

"That," said Halloran, "is the Scarlet Assize. I would know those lights in a fog at four miles
and I have prayed every night for thirty years that I would not see them again." He put his
spectacles on. "They are the order's first heresy. Two hundred years before the Crown's clause,
when the bands came off the roll, half the chapter said the light had gone soft and the other
half said the coast would die of it, and the half that said it had gone soft went east into the
machine-country and did not come back for a generation. When they came back, they had those."

"Red," said Mira.

"Red," said Halloran. "An Ember is a fist of the mother quenched in a keeper's season. Ours are
quenched in a season given — the keeper stands at the trough and pays it himself and the stone
takes it, and after that the blade lights when he means it and costs him nothing but a memory he
will not miss. *Theirs* are quenched in a season taken. Somebody stood at that trough who did not
volunteer." He took his spectacles off again. "That is what the colour is. Red is what a season
looks like when nobody offered it."

"Can they—" Mira stopped. "Is it strong?"

"Stronger than yours," said the Warden. "And cheaper, in the way that stolen money is cheaper
than earned money, which is to say it costs the same and somebody else pays it. A red blade does
not take its price from the man who holds it. It takes it from the room. Stand next to one for
long enough and you will find you cannot remember the morning, and then you will find you cannot
remember the year, and then a man will ask you your name and you will look at him."

On the wall nobody said anything.

"The Ash won't follow it," said Rook.

"No," said the Warden. "A red blade is a door nailed shut. It has light coming out of it and
nothing going in, and the Ash cannot use it and cannot eat it, so it does not follow it and it
does not stay near it. That is why they can sail anywhere. That is why we are going to open the
gate."

"You're going to open the gate," said Hale.

"I am going to open the gate," said Halloran, "because if I do not, they will cut it, and the
difference between a gate that is opened and a gate that is cut is the difference between a
conversation and a body count. Twelve men at the gate, Marshal, and twelve more behind them, and
then the yard, and then the guides' stair shut and barred and the cellar empty. And get the
Embers out of the case and into the strongroom of the *Surety* and get the *Surety* out of this
harbour on the tide."

"She won't make the tide," said Hale. "There's no wind in the roads."

"There's a wind now," said Halloran, "and it came with them, and I do not like that either."

---

They came up the slip at half past one: twelve of them, in grey coats with a red cord at the
shoulder, no armour anywhere, no helmets, and six hilts at six belts.

The man at the front was fifty or so and had a face like a schoolmaster's: thin, tired, and
entirely without malice, which was the worst part. He came up the slip with his hands open at his
sides and stopped at the top of it and looked around the yard at the marines and the knights and
the forge-hands and the eleven drowned men standing at the chain with boat-hooks, and he did the
thing that Vey did, which was to take a list out of his coat.

"Warden Halloran," he said. "Marshal Hale. Ser Cadan Rook. Miss Calloway." He looked up. "I am
Anselm Cray. I am the Ordinator of the Assize, which is a title I did not choose and have not
enjoyed. I have a list, and it is short, and I would like to read it and then go, because I have
no wish to be in this yard longer than I have to be."

"Read it," said Halloran.

Cray read it.

"Item. In the eleventh year of the dark the coast of the Reach maintained nine lanterns, of which
one was lit. It has been kept, in that time, by a receipt, a stipend, and a promise of four
ships. It has not been kept. It has been *quiet*." He turned the leaf. "Item. In Sixthmonth a
keeper without a licence lit the Ninth at Carrow for one night, and the coast called it a miracle,
and the Crown called it a crime, and neither of them did anything with it. Item. In the eighth
month the same keeper walked a drowned road with a chipped lens and a man who could not see, and
lost a tower, a town and a woman, and gained a knight, a crew and a sound. Item. Yesterday a boy
held a lane in the roads of a Crown anchorage for four minutes and a fleet of nine sail did
nothing, and a woman with eleven years of arithmetic in her filed a warrant of sufferance." He
folded the list. "That is the coast. That is the whole of it. You are brave, and you are kind, and
you are going to be eaten."

The yard was silent.

"By what?" said Halloran.

"By what is coming over the Fall," said Cray. "You have a Brine Mother with a contract and a
deadline and a thing in the water with a lit window, and you think that is the end of the world.
It is the small end. The thing in the water is a weather. It eats coasts that have forgotten how
to be hard. There is something behind it that eats coasts that have never been hard at all, and it
is coming, and it has eaten better coasts than this one, and when it comes it will not care about
your vows or your receipts or your beautiful, useless, freely given light."

"You don't know that," said Rook.

"I have seen it," said Cray. "I have stood on a sea-wall at a place called Harrow with four
hundred people behind me and I have watched it come up the road in the shape of the people it had
already eaten, and I was the only one on that wall who had a blade that would hold, because I had
taken the seasons of eleven volunteers to make it — men who agreed, and did not know what they
were agreeing to, and I did not tell them — and I have never once been able to decide whether
that makes me a murderer or a man who did the arithmetic." He looked at his hands. "That was
nineteen years ago. There were nine of us who came off that wall and six of us are here, and the
three who are not here are the three who would not take a season, and they died being kind."

Nobody in the yard moved.

"So we temper," said Cray. "We go to a coast that has been kept soft, and we break something in
it, and we leave it harder than we found it. We are not the thing over the Fall. We are the
tempering before it. Some of us will be called monsters for that, and some of us will *be*
monsters, and I have made my peace with both, and I would like you to understand that I am not
here to enjoy this."

"You're here for the Embers," said Halloran.

"I am here for the mother and the nine," said Cray. "The forge can cut, and I need blades, and
there is no other forge on the Reach. I am not asking today. I am serving notice, the way your Crown does. Until the ninth night — seven nights, by my count,
and I have counted it twice. On the ninth night I will come back with the whole Assize and I will
take the forge, and any man who stands in the door will be a man who chose it." He put the list away.
"Now. Before I go. I would like to see the door."

---

It took one wrong second to become a fight and Mira never afterward could say which one it was.

Halloran said no. Cray said he had not asked. Halloran said the door was the order's and the
stair was the order's and the road under it was nobody's, and Cray said that was a very fine
sentence and he would like to see it, and walked towards the cellar stair with four of his people
and the rest of them standing in the yard like a hedge.

Marshal Hale put herself in front of the stair with her sword drawn and forty marines behind her.

"Marshal," said Cray, without stopping. "You have a beautiful line. It will not hold. Stand
aside."

"Not today," said Hale.

"Then I will make an example in a doorway instead of a yard, and it will be worse for everybody."
Cray turned his head. "Sister. The chain."

The woman at his shoulder was forty and had a face like a shut ledger, and she walked to the
harbour chain — the great iron thing that is cranked across the mouth of the Kindling's harbour
and has been cranked twice in a hundred years — and she put her hand on the windlass and looked at
the marine sergeant beside it.

The sergeant was nineteen and had been told to hold the chain and he held it, with his hands on
the spokes, and said, "Ma'am, I can't let you—"

She did not draw. She put her hand on the windlass next to his and the red came up out of her
sleeve and along the iron and the sergeant let go of the spokes and sat down on the stone with his
mouth open, and the chain went slack with a noise like a bell falling downstairs.

That was the second it started in.

The marine behind him fired his saltlock and the bolt went wide and hit the woman in the shoulder
and broke on her coat, and Cray said, "Ah," in a tired voice, and six red blades lit at once.

Mira had seen an ember-blade before. Rook's was gold, and it came up like a candle being coaxed,
and it had a warmth to it and a flicker at the edges and it made the air around it smell of hot
iron. The red ones did not flicker. They came up all at once, six flat bars of red light, and the
air around them went *cold* — she felt it on her face from thirty feet away — and the sound of the
yard went out of the world like water going out of a bath.

Then they moved.

What Mira remembered afterwards, and what she wrote down, was not the speed. It was that nobody
screamed. The marine sergeant was on the stone with his mouth open and no wound on him, and a
corporal who ran at the woman with a boat-hook put the hook through her coat and she took the hook
away from him and put her hand on his chest, and he stopped, and stood there, and said, "I'm
sorry, I don't—" and then said nothing else for the rest of his life.

The marines held the line. That was the thing nobody who was not there ever believed: forty men
with saltlocks and cutlasses against six blades of red light, and they held the line for four
minutes because the line was where they had been told to stand. Hale was in the middle of it with
her sword and her voice, and the voice was the part that held: *dress, dress, hold the left, hold
the left, you are not to go past the gate, you are not to go past the gate.*

And the pirates came.

Nobody ordered them. The eleven drowned men of the *Mercy Bell* came off the chain with their
boat-hooks and their clubs and one of them with a capstan bar, and Pell came with them, and
Nettle, and Hask with a patch over an eye he did not need, and Marrow at the front with a length
of chain in his fist and his band going round his wrist.

"You'll want the gate," Marrow said, to Hale, as he came past her.

"Hold the gate," said Hale.

"Aye," said Marrow. "We're Crown now. It's in the book."

They hit the flank of the Assize like weather. It did not help — nothing helped; the red blades
went through a capstan bar like a knife through a candle and the cold came off them in a wave —
but it made the six of them turn, and it made four minutes into six, and in the six minutes the
guides got the cellar stair barred and the strongroom of the *Surety* shut and the case with
three Embers in it out of the harbour on the tide.

And Lieutenant Airey died in the gate.

Mira saw it. He had his marines in a wedge in the arch and his sword in his hand and he was
saying something to the man in front of him — she never heard what — and Cray walked up the arch
through his own people and put the red blade through the lieutenant's guard without hurrying, and
it went past his shoulder rather than through it, and Airey sat down against the gatepost with his
sword across his knees and looked at the yard with an expression of mild surprise, and stopped.

There was no mark on him. There was no blood anywhere in that gate.

"Enough," said Cray, and the six blades went out, all at once, and the cold went out of the air
with them, and the sound of the world came back — gulls, and somebody crying, and someone
shouting for a surgeon.

"You will find that your lieutenant is not dead," said Cray, to the marines. "He is emptied. He
will sit there for a day or two and then he will be up and about with no memory of this morning
and no idea what year it is, and he will be gentle, and someone will have to feed him." He put his
blade away. "That is what a season looks like when it is taken, and I have just taken his, and I
did it in a doorway in front of forty men so that you would all know exactly what we are. I could
have killed him. It would have been faster and kinder, and it would have taught you nothing."

Hale was on her knees by the gatepost with her hand on her lieutenant's shoulder.

"You could have asked," she said.

"I did ask," said Cray. "Eleven years ago, at Saltmere, at a table with a great many chairs. I
asked for eight blades and a forge and a coast that would be hard, and a man with a good voice
explained the cost to me, and then he explained the stipend, and I have been out in the
machine-country ever since, getting harder." He turned round. "Warden. The door."

---

The door at the top of the three hundred steps had held for four hundred years. It had nine
slots in a brass drum and eight pins in the slots and one pin in a drawer, and it had been shut
against the road since before there was a Crown to shut it for.

Cray went down the stair with four of his people and a lantern and the rest of the Assize in the
cellar with their blades out, and he was gone eleven minutes, and when he came back up he was
carrying the drum of the lock.

He put it on the flagstones. It had been cut through, all nine slots at once, as clean as if it
had been cast that way, and the edges were still warm.

"Now," he said, "the door is a door. It will open for anybody." He turned to the Warden, who had
come down the stair behind him because nobody had been able to stop him. "You have a guide with a
band and a girl with a light and seven nights. Use them. That is the whole of what I am giving you,
and it is more than the Crown has given this coast in eleven years."

"You've opened a road into my fortress," said Halloran.

"I have opened a road out of it," said Cray. "There is a difference and you will learn it in
seven nights." He looked up the stair. "And I am leaving you something."

The blade he left in the door frame was red and it was lit, and it stood there in the iron
without a hand on it, burning flat and steady like the lights on his ship, and the cold came off
it in a ring you could feel from the bottom step.

"That will burn until the ninth night," said Cray. "It is taking its price from the stair. By the
last of it the air in here will be so thin of years that a man will come down those steps and
forget his own name on the way. You may pull it out. If you pull it out with your hands you will lose
the hand and the year it was born in." He started up the steps. "You have a knight. The knight
has a blade. A blade of given light can put out a blade of taken light, and I have only ever seen
it happen once, and the man who did it was never the same."

"Why?" said Mira.

Cray stopped.

"Because I am tired," he said, "and because the thing over the Fall does not care whether I win,
and because a coast that is only hard is a coast that is already eaten. You want to know the
truth, keeper? I have been tempering coasts for nineteen years and I have never once seen one
come out of it able to be kind. If you can do the other thing — if you can be hard and keep the
kind light — you will have done the one thing I could not." He went up the steps. "Seven nights. Tell them I
came."

---

They counted the cost at five in the afternoon in the yard, and Vey read the names off a list,
because that was the only liturgy anybody on that quay had.

Lieutenant Airey, who was not dead and would never be himself. Two marines. A forge-hand called
Dodd who had been carrying a barrow across the yard when the cold came and who had put it down
and walked into the wall. A guide called Sennet, with her arm broken in two places and her band
cracked clean across. Four drowned men of the *Mercy Bell* — Hask, one of the brothers, and two
whose names Vey had to get from Marrow — none of them dead, all of them emptied, all of them
sitting in the guides' room with their bands still going round their wrists and nothing at all
behind their eyes.

Marrow was not among them. Marrow had a red burn along his forearm where he had put his chain
between a red blade and Pell, and he sat on the bollard with his arm in a bucket of salt water and
said, when Mira came to him, "Four of mine. Four of my crew, in a yard, for a ship that isn't
ours any more."

"You saved Pell."

"Aye," said Marrow. "And I'll tell you what I can't work out. The cold came off that blade and it
took Hask's forty years off the bottom of the sea and there wasn't anything in him to take, so it
took the next thing, which was the road. He's lost the road. He's lost the deep road, and he's
sitting in there with a band on his wrist and no way under the world." He looked at the water.
"Four of them. That's the bill for a ship that was sold this morning for a stone."

"It wasn't your sale."

"No," said Marrow. "It was the captain's. And she's standing on the quay not saying anything, and
she's been standing there an hour, and one of us ought to go and stand next to her."

---

Isolde was on the quay with her hat in her hand, looking at the *Mercy Bell*.

The Crown's pennant was going up her mainmast, and the paint was going on the quarter, and a
lieutenant of the *Surety* was on her deck with a list, telling fourteen people where to stand.

"How bad?" she said, when Mira came up.

"Four of your crew emptied. Hask's lost the road. Lieutenant Airey will be a boy in a chair for
the rest of his life. Dodd's dead. The door's cut open and there's a red blade in it that will burn
until the ninth night."

"Right," said Isolde. "And the Ember?"

Mira took the canvas bag out of her coat and opened it so that the warm grey stone showed in the
harbour light.

Isolde Sable looked at it for a long moment.

"So that's what a ship is worth," she said. "I've been wondering for forty years. I had a number
in my head and it was wrong, and now I know, and I find I would rather not have known." She put
her hat on. "Take it up to the tower and put it somewhere it can't be taken again. And get the
Warden to open that door and get the guides down there before the cold eats the stair. And find
out what the boy wants."

"What boy?"

"The one they left behind," said Isolde. "He's been on the bollard since they went down the
cellar and nobody's had time to shoot him."

---

He was sixteen, and he was sitting on the bollard at the end of the quay with a sword across his
knees and his hands on it and his coat too big for him, and he had not moved for two hours.

"Don't come near," he said, when Mira was ten feet away.

"Why not?"

"Because it's not lit and it's not going to be, and when it does it goes teal, and the Ordinator
says a teal blade is a thing that people get ideas about." He looked up. "I'm Toby Larch. I'm his
novice. He left me here."

"He left you here."

"He said, *stay and learn what a kept coast is, and when you're done, come and tell me.*" Toby
looked at the sword on his knees. "That's not a punishment. That's the worst thing he's ever said
to me."

Mira sat down on the next bollard, which was not ten feet, and Toby did not move away, and the
band on her wrist turned towards the blade in his lap.

It was a plain hilt with a thread in the fuller, and the thread was grey.

"May I see it?"

"It won't do anything."

"May I see it anyway."

He turned it over and held it out flat on both palms, and Mira put her hand near it, and the
thread in the fuller went **teal** — a deep green-blue like the sea in the last of the light, not
bright, not flat, but moving, the way the shimmer moves in a band.

Toby made a sound.

"That's the first time," he said. "I've had it four years. The Ordinator made it for me out of
seventeen people's seasons — seventeen, all given, all volunteers, out of a village called
Ostrey that he saved from something worse — and it's never once lit for me, and he said that's
because a teal blade has to be *earned* and I hadn't earned it, and he said it would come up when
I understood something." He was looking at the light. "What did you do?"

"Nothing," said Mira. "I've got a ring on my hand and a band on my wrist and a stone in my pocket
and a great deal of practice at being told what I am." She looked at the teal thread. "And I think
it lit because somebody in the last four days told the truth in front of you, and it's the first
thing all day that has got brighter instead of going out."

Toby Larch sat on the bollard with the teal light in his hands and the harbour going dark around
him, and then he said, in the voice of a boy who has decided something and is frightened of it,
"What's a kept coast?"

---

Mira wrote it up at midnight in the guides' room with the door open, because the stair had a red
blade in it and nobody wanted a door shut on that.

*Friday. The Crown does not sell, it exchanges. Mr Vey valued my Ember at one lugger, ninety feet,
and wrote the hour in because she asked him to, and Isolde Sable signed her ship away at twelve
minutes past nine and told her crew they were navy and that they were not to cry.*

*A ship's people go with the ship. It is four hundred years old and it is there because a ship
without a crew is a wreck and the Crown does not buy wrecks. So the drowned are the Crown's now,
and they fought for her this afternoon like they had never heard of the sale, and four of them
will not walk the deep road again.*

*The Scarlet Assize came in at noon on the first wind in five days and the Ash went *away* from
it. Nine miles out, the one lit window that has followed us from Carrow since Sixthmonth went
dark, and came back a mile south, and then was gone, and the sea where it had been was only sea.
A red blade is a door nailed shut. It has light going out of it and nothing going in, and the Ash
cannot use it, so it does not follow it. I have written that sentence four times and it is the
most frightening sentence in the notebook.*

*Anselm Cray is fifty and has a schoolmaster's face and read us a list of our own failures in a
tired voice, and then told us what the Ash is a weather of. There is something behind it. Nineteen
years ago he stood on a wall at a place called Harrow and watched it come up the road in the shape
of the people it had eaten, and he took eleven volunteers' seasons to make a blade that would
hold, and he has never forgiven himself and he has never stopped.*

*He is not the thing over the Fall. He is the tempering before it. He breaks soft coasts so they
will be hard enough, and he counts the breaking as a kindness, and the worst of it is that he
spared the Warden to carry the message and left his own novice behind to learn what a kept coast
is. He is trying to be careful. That is what I cannot get past. He is trying to be careful and he
emptied a lieutenant in a gate to teach us a lesson.*

*The door is cut. Nine slots through at once and the drum on the flagstones and a red blade
standing in the iron that will burn until the ninth night and take the years out of the stair. Cray says
a blade of given light can put out a blade of taken light and that he has seen it once.*
