# Chapter Eleven

They were four days out of the Fold when Isolde put the question to the deck.

"Six days there and back," she said, "and eight weeks to the Fall. I can lose six
days. I cannot lose the woman, the light, or the ship, and I will not get all three."

"Then we go back," said Mira.

"Say why."

"Because I told her I'd come back and say it again."

Isolde looked at her for a while, hat in her hands, the way she looked at a chart
that had been drawn by somebody optimistic.

"That's not a reason," she said. "That's a *promise*. But it's the reason I'd use, so
we'll go back."

They went back with a following wind and Sallow talking.

He talked about the Fold the way a man talks about a house he has never been inside:
in detail, and with the door in his hand. "Two arms of rock, one road in, twelve houses
and a keep with no glass left in it. You could hold that harbour with nine men and a
bad dog." He was sitting on the hatch cover with his boots off, which nobody had asked
him to do. "I've read the survey. There's a copy in the Warden's study and another in
a drawer at Saltmere, and both of them are wrong about the depth in the channel, which
means the second man copied the first and neither of them went."

"How do you know the depth?" said Pell.

"Because I have asked a man who has," said Sallow, comfortably. "The Fold is the
safest harbour on this coast, which is exactly why nobody uses it. Nothing gets in
without being seen from the tower, and there is nobody in the tower, so nothing gets in
at all. A place like that makes a man religious."

"You knew there was a keeper up there," said Mira.

"I knew there was a light." For once he did not make a joke of it. "I didn't know it
was a woman paying for it out of her own face. That's not a job. That's a
subscription."

---

The Fold was not empty when they came in.

There was a cutter at anchor in the middle of the harbour, white-hulled, two masts,
with the Crown's salt-mark on her bow and her boats already down. A clerk sat on the
quay with a box on his knees. A soldier stood at the harbour log with his hands
behind his back, doing nothing in the manner of a man who has been told to do nothing
and is very good at it.

And in the middle of the swept stone, talking to Perrin Wren with his hat under his
arm, was a young man in a keeper's grey that had been cut by somebody better than a
coast tailor.

"Ah," said Isolde. "A licence."

The man turned. He had a pleasant, tired, competent face, and he came down the quay to
meet them with his hand out, which on the Reach is not a thing anyone does.

"Captain Sable. Ossian Vey, licensed keeper of the Fifth at Saltmere, and — before
anyone says it — no, I'm not here to douse anything." He smiled. It was a good smile.
It had been practised in front of a hall. "I'm here to see that it's done properly."

---

Vey did the Fold in an afternoon, and he did it beautifully.

He did not go up the tower first. He went round the village with the clerk and the box,
and he looked at the washing, and he spoke to Perrin Wren — who answered — and to the
woman in the boathouse, who answered — and he wrote, and the clerk wrote, and the box
filled.

"He's not touching anything," Teo said, from the wall. "He's not moving anything. He's
just writing."

"Which is worse," said Isolde.

At four he came up the sixty-two steps with the box and the clerk and no soldiers, and
he stood in the lamp room with the light coming out of Alys Wren and did not look at
it once.

"Mrs Wren," he said.

"Miss."

"Miss Wren. I'm going to be brief, because you're tired and because I don't enjoy
this." He put the box down. "You are the only lit lantern north of Saltmere and you
have been for eleven years, without a licence, without a stipend, without relief, and
without a single complaint that I can find in any file on this coast. The Crown's
position — officially, and my own, privately — is that this is the most embarrassing
thing that has happened to the Throne in living memory."

Alys laughed. It turned into a cough at the end of it.

"What I can offer," Vey said, "is regularisation. You come to Saltmere. There is a
house on the harbour wall with a window and a bed and a fire. Relays of licensed
keepers tend the lamp on a schedule; you are consulted, you are paid, and you are not
required to be on fire at any point in the arrangement." He set the papers on the
ledge. "It's not a cage. It's a *post*. I have brought a form."

"And if I say no?"

"Then I record the refusal, and I go away, and I will be back in three months with
another form." He said it without unkindness. "I'm not going to break your ring, Miss
Wren. I'm thirty years old and I have never broken anybody's anything. I'm going to
ask you, politely, once a season, until one of us dies."

Mira stood in the corner and watched Alys look at the form.

It was the first time in eleven years that anybody had offered her a warm room.

"Girl," Alys said, without turning. "What do you think?"

Mira's mouth was dry. "I think it's a real offer."

"It is a real offer," Vey agreed, pleasantly. "Everything I do is real."

"If she signs it," Mira said, "then the light isn't hers. It's his, and the Throne's,
and it goes out for a speech whenever the Admiral wants the coast quiet. That's what
happened to the Fifth. You *know* what happened to the Fifth."

"I was at the Fifth when it happened," said Vey, and for a moment his pleasant face
was not pleasant at all. "I was nineteen and I was holding the wick and a captain of
the fleet put his glove over the glass and told me it was for the good of the coast. I
have spent eleven years making sure that can never happen again by accident, and the
way I have done it is paperwork." He tapped the form. "You are young and you think a
signature is a betrayal. I think a signature is the only thing that ever stopped
anyone."

Alys looked at him for a long time.

Then she said, "No."

"May I ask why?"

"Because I've read your form." She picked it up, turned it over, put it back down. "It
says the keeper shall light the lamp on the hours appointed by the Wardens of the
Harbour. On the *hours appointed*." Alys shook her head slowly. "I didn't spend eleven
years of my life on fire so that somebody could appoint the hours. The light doesn't
come on at a convenient time, Mr Vey. It comes on when somebody means it."

Vey wrote that down, which Mira would remember later.

---

Alys did the flare at dusk, because the fleet had been out there for days and she
wanted them to see it properly once.

She put her hand on the dead lens, and the room gathered, and the light went out over
the water in a long gold road that did not stop at the harbour mouth or the bay or the
two arms of the Fold. It went east, and it kept going, and it found them.

Fifty ships. A hundred. The un-ships in their long ranks, canvas drawing in a wind
that was not blowing.

And behind them the grey that was more grey than the sky, moving, filling a quarter of
the horizon with something that had not been a colour before and now was.

"The Ash," said Alys, in her ordinary voice. "That's what it looks like. I've been
looking at it for eleven years and I still can't hold the shape in my head long enough
to describe it."

"How long?" said Isolde.

"At this speed? Twenty days to this rock. Thirty to the Ninth." Alys's hand was
trembling against the brass. "And the Crown's fleet is in front of it, coming west, so
it'll be here in eight, and it will say it has come to save the coast, and the coast
will let it, because the coast is frightened."

Mira looked at the light instead of at the thing in it, and that was when she saw
Vey's hand.

He had come up the steps behind her to see, and his hand was on the rail, and the
lantern light went through the room the way it goes through everything, and the glass
of his ring lit up — and did not. There was a seam in it. There was brass under the
glass, and a bead at the centre that had been coloured with something, and the seam
went round the whole hoop like the join in a cheap cup.

A real ring, Alys's ring, was white all the way through.

Mira looked away before he noticed. She was eighteen and he had been kind to her and
she had no idea yet what she was looking at, so she said nothing, and that is how a
spy stays a spy.

The light came back. The Fold was a harbour again.

"Twenty days," said Isolde, into the silence.

"Twenty days," said Vey, writing.

---

Alys died at the turn of the tide, which was the hour she had been born, and which
Isolde said afterwards was either a kindness or a joke.

She did not make a speech. She sat down on the step under the lens with her hands in
her lap and said, "Girl. Say the names."

"Perrin," said Mira. "Sela. Tobe. Liss."

"Again."

"Perrin. Sela. Tobe. Liss."

"Good." Alys closed her eyes. "That's the trick of it. You keep saying them and
somebody keeps hearing them and it stays true, even after it stops being true for
me."

Her ring went from white to clear, and the light in it went out like a lamp does when
the oil is done: not a flicker, just an ending, and the room was dark and the dead
lens was a shape she could not see.

Then something warm moved in Mira's hand.

She looked down. Her own ring — green-black, the ring that had fitted a key — had a
coal in it. Not a flame. A small, steady, patient weight of light the size of a
fingernail, with no wick, no oil and no lamp anywhere near it, and it had come out of
a woman who had spent eleven years learning how to give it away instead of holding it.

Mira understood, standing in the dark with a dead woman's last light in her hand,
that she had just been handed the rest of her life, and that it was going to hurt, and
that the bill was already in the post.

"Say something for her," Isolde said, from the door.

Mira said the only thing she had.

"I am Mira Calloway, keeper of the Ninth, and I am here."

Nobody laughed. It was not that kind of a room.

---

Vey was on the quay when they came down, with the box closed and the clerk behind him.

"Your light is out, your keeper is dead, and the Crown will be here inside a week,"
he said, in a tone of mild professional regret. "I'll put in my report that the
lantern was doused by the keeper's death and not by any action of the Throne. It will
help you, later, in front of a board. It is not much, but it is the truth, and I deal
in it."

"You're very good at this," said Isolde.

"I'm the only man on this coast who's good at it, which is the problem, and the reason
I have a licence." Vey put his hat on. "One more thing and I'm gone. The Roll."

"What about it?"

"I hold a copy. It's not a secret; the order keeps the original and the Throne keeps a
copy, and I am the copy." He looked at Mira, and this time he did not smile. "The
glass-eyed boy. Vane. *Recovered* — someone has already written it in, in a hand I
don't know, which I'll be raising with the Warden. I'd like to meet him. A seer is
worth more to the coast alive and registered than blind and asleep in a hammock."

Nobody had said Teo's surname. Not once. Not to Vey, not in front of Vey, not on the
quay or in the lamp room or in the village.

Mira's hands went cold, and her ring went warm, and the little coal of light in it —
the light that had been Alys Wren, who had been on fire for eleven years so that a
coast could see — turned, in Mira's hand, a quarter of an inch, the way a head turns
when somebody comes into a room.

"Corbin Sallow, as well," Vey said, to the air, to nobody in particular. "On your
articles now, I hear. I'd have thought the captain had better sense." He touched his
hat. "Good evening, Captain. My condolences, Miss Calloway. The form stands, whenever
you want it."

Then he got into his boat and rowed out to the cutter, and did not look back at the
tower, or at the dark harbour, or at the four people standing on the quay watching
him go.

"How did he know the boy's name?" said Isolde, when the oars were small.

"Because Rook wrote it in the Roll," said Mira. "That part's easy. He read the margin."

"And the seer? And Sallow, four days out of a port we never called at?"

Mira looked at the *Mercy Bell*, black against the last of the light, and at the
lamp in the hold that had been trimmed and filled and set exactly right by somebody
who was not her.

"Because somebody on that ship has been keeping the count for him," she said.
