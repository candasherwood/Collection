# Chapter Fourteen

Three days out from the *Vigil* the glass began to fall, and Isolde stood at the
weather rail looking at the sky the way a woman looks at a bill.

"Nothing in that," said Teo.

"There's plenty in that," said Isolde. "It's just not showing yet."

It showed at the turn of the afternoon watch. It came up out of the south-west as a
grey line with no cloud over it, which is the worst kind, and it crossed the last two
miles in under a quarter of an hour, and by the time Isolde had shouted the second
order the *Mercy Bell* was lying over with her lee rail in the water and the rain
coming flat and hard as thrown gravel.

"Hands! Topmen! Get that off her before she tears the stick out!"

The mainsail had been old in the tower's shadow. It had been old when Mira was born. It
went with a sound she had never heard before and would hear again: not a bang, a long
tearing *rip* like cloth being pulled off a table, and then the whole sheet of it
cracking and snapping above the deck in the wind, and every man on the ship looking up.

Pell was already going up.

"Pell!"

"Somebody's got to!"

The boy went up the shrouds with the wind trying to take his coat off him, and Isolde
was shouting things that Mira would have called unforgivable on land and that everybody
aboard understood as *be careful*, and Marrow had a rope round his own waist and was
walking to the mast, and Teo had the wheel with both hands and his jaw set.

Mira got the hood off the bucket and held it up.

The light went out into the rain and made a long white lane of it, and in the lane Mira
could see Pell's boots on the ratlines, and the torn sail cracking like a flag in a
gale, and Rook.

Rook was halfway up the mast already. He had not been told to go and he had not asked;
he had put his good hand on the shrouds and gone up with the noise and the water coming
down and his shoulder still bandaged from the Gull's Teeth, and he went up the way
people go up who have spent their lives being told to be somewhere.

He got to Pell. He got an arm round him. He shouted something that came down to the
deck as pure noise.

Then the sail tore free at the head and came down.

It came down over both of them like a net.

---

The canvas had them both against the mast, and the whole weight of it was full of wind,
and it was not still. It breathed. Every time it breathed it took an arm's length of the
mast, and gave it back, and took it again.

Mira held the lane.

There was nothing else to do with her hands that would not have made it worse. She put
her back against the deckhouse and kept the light on the mast, and the lane lay up
through the rain, and inside it she could see Pell's boot, and then Pell's hand, and then
the sail coming over the hand again.

Isolde counted. "One. Two. Three." She was not counting time; she was counting grips, and
men on a rope, and she got to eleven before the canvas came free at one corner and
somebody at the rail took a turn with it and lost it again.

"He's got him!" Marrow, halfway up, with the knife in his teeth and no way to say it
properly. "He's got the boy and the boy's got the rope!"

The sail took the mast again. Behind Mira, in the dark, a man said *oh God* in a
conversational voice, and another man told him to shut it.

Her arms went past hurting into the place beyond it where they simply reported that the
bucket was no longer a thing she was holding but a thing that had her. She could feel the
lane starting to wander. She put her chin on the rim of the bucket to steady it, which
burnt, and did not move, and the light stayed on the mast, and that was the whole of her
part in it.

They got them out. It took nine minutes, which Isolde counted out loud afterwards and
regretted, and it took Marrow going up the mast himself with a knife in his teeth, and
it took every man on the deck holding a line.

Pell came down first, hauled in a bight of canvas, swearing and crying and alive.

Rook came down on his back on the deck with the wind knocked out of him and his shoulder
open again, and lay there in the rain looking at the sky with an expression Mira could
not read.

"Ser Rook," said Nettle, over him. "Can you feel your fingers?"

"I can feel all of them," said Rook, "and I would like them to stop."

The mainsail was gone. Not damaged — gone, the whole sheet of it over the side and away
in the grey, and the ship was under a jury rig by nightfall, wallowing, making three
knots where she had made nine.

---

They ate in the hold that night because the deck was a washing tub, and Isolde sat on a
barrel with a mug and said the thing everybody had been working out for themselves.

"The *Vigil* will be off Candlewick by tomorrow noon. She'll have the wind and the
mainsail and nine hundred men, and she will be at the Kindling eleven days before we
are, and she will light the eight with a writ and a company of marines and half of them
will go out inside a week because a lantern lit by a man who doesn't mean it is a fire
in a box."

"Then we take the short way," said Teo.

"Say the short way."

"The Drowned Road."

"There it is," said Isolde, and drank.

The road was the old coast road, under three feet of water since before the Fall, running
the whole length of the Reach from Carrow to the Kindling, and it could be walked at low
light because the stones of it were still there and a keeper could see them.

"Four days," said Teo. "Maybe five. It's faster than anything on the water and it's
never patrolled, because a ship can't follow a road and a horse can't walk in the sea."

"And the light?" said Isolde.

Mira took the hood off and carried the bucket to the rail.

"What are you doing?"

"Seeing how far it goes."

She held it out over the water at the height of her own waist, which is not how a tower
holds a light. It showed her the sea for about the length of a boat and then stopped —
not faded, stopped, the way a lamp stops at the edge of its own circle with the dark
standing outside it like a wall.

"Further," said Teo.

She raised it to her shoulder. The circle went out to twenty feet, then thirty. At
forty the light was so thin on the water that she could not have told a stone from a
shadow, and if the road was down there it was a black nothing under a grey nothing.

"That's not a light," said Isolde. "That's a candle with a good opinion of itself."

"It's the best Ember on this coast."

"It's the best Ember on this coast in a bucket," said Isolde. "A lens is the difference
between a coin and a road."

Mira put the hood back on. It took her two tries, which nobody mentioned.

"We need a lantern," said Mira.

"We need a lens," said Isolde. "There's one left on this coast that isn't in a crown
warehouse or on the bottom of the sea, and it's hanging over a bar in Candlewick where
the Fourth used to be, and the man who owns it won't sell it, and I've asked."

"Then we'll ask better," said Mira.

"That's the spirit," said Isolde, "and also not a plan."

"Then here is how we walk it," said Isolde, and put her mug down, which on that ship
meant the talking was over. "Low water, first light. We go in a line and we are roped,
and the rope is not for the water. The rope is for the dark: if the line breaks, the
rope is the only thing that stops a man walking off the edge of a road into somewhere
the road isn't. The light goes in the middle of the line, in a sling, at the height of
a man's chest. A light at your feet shows you stones. A light at your chest shows you
the road."

"And if the tide comes early?" said Pell.

"Then we are in sixty miles of water to the waist and the light goes out and we are all
very sorry," said Isolde. "Which is why we carry the tide-table and not the cook."

"Hey," said Pell.

"Two more. Nobody walks ahead of the light. Nobody looks back for the ship." She held up
a finger before anybody could ask. "That last one is not superstition, it is arithmetic.
The Ash follows what is lit and the ship is dark, and if we look back we will want to
signal, and a signal is a lantern, and a lantern is a road into a boat full of people
who cannot run."

"Who carries it?" said Mira.

Isolde looked at her for a moment. "You do. You're the only one who can see the stones."

---

Rook came to her at the end of the second watch, when the ship was quiet and the rain
had gone to a fine cold mist that got into everything.

He had his surcoat on. That was the first thing — folded in three, puts it on for
duties — and he had washed his face and combed his hair with his fingers and he stood
in the middle of the hold with his hands at his sides like a man about to be sentenced.

"I have a request," he said. "It is not a large one, and it will embarrass you."

"Go on."

"A vow requires a witness." He said it plainly. "In the order, the witness is a
brother. I have no brothers. I have a captain who is not my captain and a crew I am not
sworn to and a woman I owe a great deal of money to in a currency I can't pay. So I am
asking you."

Mira sat down on the hatch cover. "I don't know the words."

"There aren't any words. That's rather the point." Rook looked at his hands. "The Long
Night has three questions. Will you keep what is not yours. Will you go where you are
sent. Will you be seen. When I answered them at fourteen I meant them, and I have been
answering them every morning since like a man winding a clock, and I have done what I
was sent to do to a great many people, and the thing I have never once done is the
third one." He looked up. "I'm going to say them again. Not to the order. To you, as
the keeper, as a witness, and I will do it badly, and you are not to be kind about it."

"I'm not a kind person," said Mira.

"You are extremely kind," said Rook, "and it is your worst quality."

Then he stood up straight and said his vow.

"Will you keep what is not yours?"

"I will."

"Will you go where you are sent?"

Rook stopped. He was quiet for so long that Mira heard the ship working, and the goat
moving about somewhere forward, and Sallow's voice up on deck saying something to Teo
that made Teo laugh.

"Not any more," said Rook, and his voice was not steady. "I will go where it is needed.
I will ask first. I will ask *why*, and if the answer is bad I will say so, and I will
not do it because a paper says so, and if that costs me my cloak and my blade and my
name in the Roll then —" He stopped. Started again, more slowly. "That is not the
answer to the question. I know. I'm sorry. I'm doing it badly."

"Do it badly," said Mira.

"I will go where it is needed," said Rook. "That's the answer. Not where I'm sent."

"Will you be seen?"

"Yes," said Rook. And then, because he was incapable of leaving a thing the right size:
"Yes, and I'm frightened of it, and I'm going to do it anyway."

Nobody clapped.

Pell was in the doorway with his arm strapped and he said "amen" in completely the wrong
place, and then went red, and then said "sorry, ser" and then, "no, actually," and stood
there.

Isolde, from her barrel, with her mug: "That's the worst vow I've ever heard. My
grandmother's was better and she was a smuggler. Nettle, get the man a drink and a
chair."

Marrow said, "Good."

One word, in a voice like a door, and Rook's whole face came apart for a second and he
put it back together with his hand.

Sallow was in the corner with his back against the bulkhead and his arms folded, and he
did not make a joke. He said, "I've heard better. I've also seen men die for worse, and
the ones that died for worse had nicer words," and then he went up on deck.

---

Mira wrote it down that night, on the back of the page with the four names on it, in the
cramped hand she used when she was trying not to feel anything.

*Ser Cadan Rook, of the Emberwatch, said a vow tonight with me for a witness. He said it
badly and he meant it. I am the only person alive who has it written down. If I forget
this page, then nobody will know he did it, and I find that I mind that more than I mind
losing it myself.*

Then Teo came down with two mugs and sat next to her and did not ask, and she read it to
him, and he read it again over her shoulder, the way he always did.

"He'll want it back," Teo said. "The order. Not the cloak and the blade. The thing where
somebody tells him what's right."

"He knows."

"He'll go back anyway." Teo turned the mug in his hands. "They'll offer him a job in a
warm room where the right thing is easy and the wrong thing is written on a form, and
he'll go, because he's that kind of man, and then he'll find out the room is on fire, and
he'll be very sorry about it and very brave."

"Are you telling me not to trust him?"

"I'm telling you he's the best man I've ever met," said Teo, "and he's going to hurt you
by accident, and you should write down that you knew it was an accident."

---

Up on deck, later, when the mist had gone and the sea had come back to something that
would pass for calm, they raised the jury rig and got three knots out of her, and
Isolde lined her up for Candlewick.

Mira stood at the rail and looked out at the water with the bucket between her feet.

"One thing," said Isolde, appearing at her elbow. "Candlewick's a tavern now. The man
who runs it is called Orren Beck and he's a good man and he's been offered a great deal
of money for that lens by people who want to hang it in a hall in Saltmere." The captain
took her hat off, looked at it, put it on. "He won't sell it to me. He might sell it to
a keeper. But he's going to want the thing you're worst at giving."

"What's that?"

"Proof," said Isolde, "that it matters."
