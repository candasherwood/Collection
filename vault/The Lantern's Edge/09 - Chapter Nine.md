# Chapter Nine

The Mother's woman came out of the water at the turn of the tide, which was the
polite thing to do, and stood at the foot of the tower with her hem in the shallows
and her hands folded.

She was not wet. That was the first thing. The sea came up to her knees and went
through her and came out the other side, and her grey dress did not darken.

"Captain," she said, to Isolde, in a voice like a door closing in another room.
"Your hour has come round."

"I know it has."

"Eleven years is eleven years. The bell is still on my bottom and the debt is still
on my book." She had not blinked. Mira watched her for a full minute and she did not
blink; the tide went in and out of her and she did not blink. "You have brought me a
lit lantern. That was the arrangement."

"He's got a lantern," said Pell, from behind the boat, and Nettle put a hand on his
shoulder without looking at him.

The woman turned her head. It was the first movement she had made and it went all the
way round, further than a neck goes, and then her face was pointed at Alys in the
lamp room window and she said, "Yes. I can smell it from here."

---

They read the contract on the quay because the woman would not come up the rock and
Isolde would not go into the water.

It was not paper. It was a length of rope, tarred, with the terms inked into it in a
fine close hand that Mira could not read upside down, and the woman held one end and
Isolde held the other, and between them the rope hung straight with nothing holding
it up.

"Read it," said the woman.

"I know what it says."

"Read it aloud, captain. It is in the terms that it is read aloud."

So Isolde read it, in the flat voice of a woman reading her own accounts.

*The hull of the lugger Mercy Bell, raised from the bottom of the Reach, with her
crew, on the understanding that the said hull shall be returned at the end of eleven
years, or the debt settled in the light of a lantern, freely given.*

"There," said the woman. "Now the schedule."

"There isn't a schedule."

"There is a schedule. It is on the other side."

Isolde turned the rope over. She read for a while. Then she stopped reading and stood
looking at the words with her jaw set, and Mira saw her thumb move on the tar as
though she wanted to put it down and could not.

"Read it," said the woman.

"*Any lit lantern within the reach of the debtor may be surrendered in settlement,*"
Isolde said. "*Including a lantern that is a keeper, if the keeper is lit and willing
and has no wick left.*"

The harbour was very quiet. Somewhere behind them the dog was sitting by the bench
with its chin on its paws.

"Say the rest," said the woman.

Isolde said the rest.

"*A refusal spoken at the Fall, by a keeper of the Ninth, before the ninth night of
the ninth month, releases the debtor and transfers the debt to the one who
refused.*"

"Transfers it to me," said Mira.

"Transfers it to you," said the woman, and for the first time she smiled, which was
worse than the not blinking. "Or," she said, "you may give me the burning woman, and
go west with your ship and your captain and your boy, and nobody has to refuse
anything at all."

---

"The arithmetic," Isolde said, when the woman had gone back into the water, and the
water had closed over her like a door.

"Don't," said Mira.

"I'm going to say it once, and then I'm going to do whatever you tell me, which is
not a thing I have done for anybody since I was nineteen." The captain sat down on a
bollard. "She's nearly out. You saw her. A month, two months, and the light comes
out of her and there's nothing left to hold it. If she pays my debt on the way, then
the ship stays afloat, the crew keeps breathing, the coast keeps its one light until
it goes out anyway, and the woman on fire gets to stop being on fire. That is not a
wicked trade. That is a *good* trade."

"It's her life."

"It's her life either way," said Isolde. "The only question is whether it buys
something."

Mira looked up at the lamp room window, where the amber light was steady and wrong.

"Ask her," said Isolde. "Go on. Ask her, and then tell me no, and I'll take the no
and we'll go to the Fall. But ask her first, because if you refuse for her without
asking, you're no better than the Warden with his pen."

---

Mira went up the sixty-two steps and asked her.

Alys listened with her arms crossed and her ring catching the light that was coming
out of her, and when Mira had finished she said, "No."

"Just like that?"

"Girl, I have been on fire for eleven years to keep a light on a rock for people I
can't name. If I hand it over to a woman at the bottom of the sea, then the last
eleven years buy a pirate's boat and a keep that goes dark the same night." She
shook her head. "No. I'll hold it, and when it goes out it goes out here, over the
Fold, with my family in the kitchen, and your captain can pay her own debts at the
Fall like a grown woman."

"That's not fair on her."

"Nothing about this is fair. That's not a reason to make it *tidy*." Alys picked up
her pencil. On the wall by the lens there was a list in her own hand, and the letters
were getting worse: *Perrin. Sela. Tobe. Liss.* "I'm going to say their names out
loud at supper. Will you be there? It's better with two."

---

The supper was at dusk, in the kitchen of the twelfth house, and there were five
places laid at a table that had been laid for five every night for eleven years.

The food was salt fish and potatoes and a loaf, and it had been put on the table by
somebody who had done it a thousand times and then stopped in the middle of doing it.

Alys went out to the harbour log, and the boathouse, and the bench, and asked them one
at a time, in the voice she used for reading the names out. "Perrin. Supper." And they came in and
sat down, and Perrin put his hands in his lap, and Sela put her hands in her lap, and
the children put their hands in their laps.

Then Alys stood at the end of the table and said their names.

"Perrin," she said. "Sela. Tobe. Liss."

Nobody answered. Four faces turned up to her politely and waited.

"That one is that one." She touched the back of a chair, then a shoulder, then a head.
"That one is that one. That one is that one. That one is that one."

Mira sat in the fifth place with her hands in her lap too, because there was nothing
else to do with them.

"Say them," said Alys. "If I stop, you say them."

So Mira said them. She said them four times, and on the fourth her voice did the thing
it had done in the tower, and she had to stop and start again.

"Good," said Alys. "Now eat."

They ate. Tobe reached for the bread and did not eat it and put it down again in
exactly the place he had taken it from, and Alys watched him do it with her chin up,
the way a woman watches weather coming.

"He does that every night," she said. "I think he's being polite."

Nobody said anything for a while. The sea went on outside, being the sea.

Then Perrin stood up, and Sela stood up, and the children stood up, and they went out
of the door one behind the other and back to their posts — the log, the boathouse, the
bench — and Alys stood in the doorway and watched them go and did not say anything at
all.

Mira helped her wash up, because it was that or sit down, and because the plates were
the only thing in that house that still worked.

"Thank you for coming," said Alys, at the door. "It's better with two."

"I'm going to come back," said Mira, "and say it again."

"Don't promise me that. Promise me the names."

The fifth place had been laid every night for eleven years and nobody had ever sat in
it, and Mira only found that out afterwards, from Nettle, who had it from the woman in
the boathouse on a night when the woman had answered a question.

---

The Understudy arrived while they were loading the boat, which is what he does.

He came up the far side of the quay on foot, from the eastern arm, where there was
nothing but rock and a path that went nowhere, and he was whistling. Red-brown coat,
too good for the coast. Two swords, worn low and badly, like a man who has learned to
fight from a book. A card in his hand.

"Good afternoon," he said. "Corbin Sallow. People call me the Understudy. Not because
I'm second choice — because I'm always the one still standing when the cast falls
down." He looked round the harbour. "Well. This is a *set*. Somebody's built a set."

Nobody said anything.

"Nobody's going to ask how I got here? Fine. Professional courtesy, I'll tell you
anyway: I was dropped on the east arm by a boat that has already left, because the
man who paid me did not want to be seen paying me, which is the surest sign in this
business that the money's good." He held up the card. "I'm here for the keeper."

"Get off my quay," said Isolde.

"Can't. Contract." He turned the card over. "Signed by the Warden of the Kindling,
which is a very impressive signature, and I know it's genuine because I watched him
make it. Deliver one Alys Wren, Bright, to the Kindling before the Crown's men reach
her. Alive. Unlit." He squinted up at the lamp room. "That's going to be the tricky
part."

"Warden's men," said Rook, from behind him.

The Understudy turned round and looked at him for a long moment, and then at the
surcoat, and his face did something complicated and pleased.

"Oh," he said. "Oh, this is *good*. You're the one who wrote in the margin."

Rook went very still. "How do you know that?"

"Because the Warden read it out to me. Six words in the margin, signed. He was
laughing when he read it, in the way a man laughs when he's been handed the end of
something." Sallow put the card away. "You're not in trouble, brother. You're the
reason I got paid twice."

"Twice," said Isolde.

"Once to bring her in. Once — different purse, same street — to make sure the Crown
doesn't. Do you know what happens when you take money from two men who want opposite
things? Nothing. It's the safest job in the world. Both of them think they've bought
me and neither of them can afford to have me killed." He grinned. "I've been dead
four times. It's a great story. I'll tell you at supper."

It was the word *supper* that did it.

Marrow came round the corner of the boathouse with a coil of cable over his shoulder
and stopped, and the two of them looked at each other across twenty feet of swept
stone.

Sallow's grin went out like a lamp.

"Hello, Marrow," he said.

Marrow put the cable down.

"You left," he said.

"I know."

"You left the week after she lifted us."

"I know that too." Sallow's hands had come down out of the air, which Mira had not
seen them do before. "You could have come with me."

"You could have stayed," said Marrow.

The tide went out. Nobody said anything. Pell looked at Nettle. Nettle looked at the
water. Isolde took her hat off, which was the nearest she came to prayer.

Then Sallow picked his grin back up, badly, like a man picking up a hat that has been
stepped on.

"Well," he said. "Nobody's the villain yet. That's the trouble with the first act."
He clapped his hands once. "Right! I can't take her to the Kindling, because you'll
fight me, and I can't let the Crown take her, because I've been paid not to, and I
can't kill anybody on account of the paperwork. So I'm going to come with you
instead, and we'll call it an escort, and I'll invoice the Warden for the mileage."

"You're not coming with us," said Isolde.

"Captain," said Sallow, "I've been paid by two governments to be in this story. I'm
in the story."

---

They sailed on the evening tide, because Isolde would not spend a night at anchor
with a creditor standing in the water, and because the woman had said *the ninth
night of the ninth month* and nobody wanted to find out what happened on the tenth.

Alys stood in the lamp room window with her hands down and the light coming off her
in a long amber road across the water, and Mira stood on the deck with the notebook
open and wrote down four names in her own hand, in the order they had been said, so
that somewhere on the Reach there would be two copies.

Behind them the Fold went dark by degrees, the way a lamp does.

"Eight weeks," said Teo, at the wheel, because he had already done the sum.

"Eight weeks," said Mira.

And in the bows, the quiet one and the loud one sat on either side of the same hatch
cover, not talking, which for one of them was normal and for the other was the
loudest thing he had ever done.
