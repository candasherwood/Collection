# Chapter Thirty

Nobody in the fleet died at the Fall.

That was not the plan. The plan had nine holes in it and a warrant with a few hours left on it and a
boy on a rock with a sword that had never lit for him before Thursday, and every man aboard had spent
night waiting to be told which of his friends was gone. Nobody was. Nine hundred and forty men sailed
to the lip of the world and nine hundred and forty came off it, and it was the only thing that went
to plan and nobody said a word about it, because twenty people had gone over the edge and every one
of them had been counted.

The sun came up on the tenth of the ninth month on a shore with eight hundred and eighty people
standing on it, and there was no authority on the Reach at all.

That was the first thing Vey said, and he said it before anybody had had breakfast.

"Midnight," he said, standing on the *Surety*'s quarterdeck in yesterday's shirt with his ledger
under his arm. "The term ended at midnight. The covenant says the ninth night of the ninth month and
it means the ninth night, and the Crown is not the Crown on the tenth, and the warrant is paper, and
the warrant of sufferance is paper, and I am not a licensed keeper of anything until somebody with a
seal says I am." He looked almost happy, which was frightening. "I have spent nine years being a
licensed man. I find I have no idea what to do with my hands."

"You could write it down," said Mira.

"That is what I am going to do with them," said Vey.

Behind him, at the mouth of the bay, the *Vigil* lay with her one lit window and no flag.

Nobody had struck the flag. Voss had come aboard at eleven the night before, put her gloves on the
chart table, and said, "I am out of authority in fifty minutes and I have a great deal of paper to
sign in it," and had gone into the cabin and shut the door. She had signed for an hour. When Hale
looked in at midnight she was sitting with the pen in her hand and nothing in front of her, and the
page in the fleet's book said:

> *Dismissed. The lights of the coast withdrawn at the ninth hour by warrant of the Admiral of the
> Fleet, whose authority ended at midnight, and who has no successor and has not named one.*

Sallow wrote that. He wrote it twice, because the first time his hand shook and the Admiral said
"again", and then she took the page and read it and said, "That is the truest thing in the book,"
which was not a compliment to anybody.

---

The Scarlet Assize came up out of the north-east at first light.

Pell saw it. He was in the foretop of the *Surety* with a hooded lamp and a cold breakfast, and he
said, "Sail," and then, in a different voice, "Black sail. Three lights."

Nobody moved for a moment. Twenty-two ships at anchor in a bay full of empty people, and a black
hull standing in from the grey at four knots with three red lights at the masthead, and every man in
the fleet knowing exactly what those lights had done in the yard at the Kindling.

Kettle came aft with his hat in his hand.

"Admiral," he said. "Do we run?"

"No," said Voss, from the rail. She had come up without anybody hearing her. "We do not run from a
ship that has come to look."

"Admiral, with respect, that's an ordinator of the Scarlet Assize and his hold's full of men who
have taken a season out of every room they've ever stood in—"

"He has come to look," said Voss. "Look at the shore, Mr Kettle. Nine hundred people standing in the
rain with their faces to the water. He has come to see whether the Ash ate this coast or whether it
paid. There is nothing in his doctrine for a coast that paid."

The black ship came in to half a mile and stopped, and stayed there while the sun came up over the
Fall and the mist went pink and then white, and the fleet stood at the rails and looked at it and it
looked at the fleet.

An hour. Then the three lights went down one at a time, and the black hull came round, and the wind
took her north and she was gone.

"Forty men in that ship," said Hale, who had counted her boats, "and a blade that takes the year out
of a room. He could have come in."

"He could," said Voss. "He has seen the account closed and he has gone to find a coast that hasn't
paid. Write the hour, Mr Sallow. And write that he dipped his lights, because I want that in the
book where the Kindling can read it."

At eight the window on the *Vigil* went out.

It did it the way a lamp does when the hand that kept it is not there any more: a minute of the old
colour, and then nothing, and the glass black. Nobody had to be told what it meant. Kettle looked at
the ship for a while and said, "Well," and that was the whole of the funeral the Ash got.

---

Here is what they had, standing on the shingle at eight in the morning with the sea going over the
lip in the ordinary way for the first time in eleven years: eight hundred and eighty people who could
not be spoken to, twenty-two ships, no authority, four towers standing dark between Saltmere and the
Sill and an old man in a fifth who would not put his out, a coast of empty houses from Barrowfoot to
Wren's Fold, and no money at all.

And a boy on the north rock with a small light in a cup of oakum.

"He's still there," said Pell, who had been up the shingle at dawn. "Miss, he's been there all night.
He's got the light trimmed and the lens wiped and he's been asleep sitting up with his hand on the
housing."

"Get him a coat and a hot thing and leave him where he is," said Mira.

"Miss—"

"He is the only light on the north end of the world, Pell, and he is sixteen, and if we take him off
that rock tonight there is nothing at the Fall at all." She looked at the rock, and the small thin
colour of deep water on it. "And find out what he wants to be called. He's not a novice of the
Assize any more. He can be anything he likes and nobody's got a list."

"Like what?"

"Keeper of the Fall," said Mira. "Somebody has to be."

---

The argument about the eight hundred and eighty happened on the shingle in the wind, with Hale
standing on a coil of rope because she was the only one who could be heard over it.

"Saltmere is four days north with this wind," she said. "I can take the manifest and the covenant
and the fleet's book, and I can put it in front of the Throne, and the Throne can read the clause it
wrote itself: *the lights shall be restored, or the covenant renewed, or the matter otherwise
determined.* It has been otherwise determined. It has been determined by a girl and seven rings and a
boy with a sword, and if they want to argue with the paperwork they will have to argue with mine."

"And the eight hundred and eighty?" said Kettle.

"That," said Hale, "is not a thing the Throne can do anything about. That is a thing we do."

Nobody had a plan. Rook had a plan, and it was the worst plan in the meeting, and it was the only
one.

"They followed a light down here," he said. "Every one of them walked from their own village to this
shingle because something lit the way and did not stop. They can walk home the same way. We put a
light in front and we walk north, parish by parish, and the ships carry the ones from the far
coasts, and we put them in their own houses."

"They won't turn off the road," said Odo Pike. "They walked past their own doors for four days."

"Then we walk them past their own doors and we go in first and we light the lamp, and then we stand
in the doorway and we call them by name." Rook looked at the shingle. "Every one of them has a name.
It is in the parish books, it is in the Crown's roll, it is in Mr Sallow's ledger. They cannot use
their own names. We can."

"It will take weeks," said Vey.

"It will take a season," said Rook. "It is a coast. It has taken longer."

Mira stood in the wind with her hands in her coat and looked at the eight hundred and eighty and
knew, with the particular clarity of the very tired, that this was the actual work and that the ring
and the Fall had only been the price of admission to it.

"Two lights," she said. "Not one. One at the front and one at the back, so anybody who stops is
between them. Guides on the flanks calling the road. Ships go ahead to the parishes with the parish
books and the list of names, and they light a lamp in the biggest house in each village and leave a
man on the door, and when we get there we walk them in."

"And the ones with no house left?" said Kettle. "There's a hundred or more from the Hollow Bell and
Barrowfoot, and Barrowfoot is a hole in the ground."

"Then they come to Carrow," said Mira, "and they stand in the square, and we find out what a coast
does with a hundred people who cannot be left and cannot be kept."

"Aye," said Kettle, after a moment. "That's an honest answer. I'll take the *Ivy* north with the
parish books and the first of them, if you'll have somebody aboard who can read the roll out loud,
because I've never learned and I'm not starting at fifty."

"Mr Vey will write you a list," said Mira. "He is very good at lists."

"I am," said Vey. "It is the one thing I am still fully qualified to do."

---

At ten in the morning the Admiral of the Fleet came down the shingle on foot with her gloves off and
stopped in front of an old woman sitting on a rock with a stick across her knees.

Every man on that shore found something to do. Nobody went away. Eight hundred and eighty people
looked at the water and listened with the backs of their heads.

"Mrs Calloway," said Voss. "I have a thing to say and it has to be said to a witness, and there is
only one witness on this coast who has the standing."

Ede looked up at her for a while.

"You want me to hear it," she said.

"I do."

"Because a vow that's broken puts the light out until a true thing is said in front of somebody."

"Yes."

"And you've been carrying a dark lamp for eleven years," said Ede Calloway, "and you've come to the
end of the world to find somebody to say it to." She settled her stick. "Say it, then. I'll not
make it easy and I'll not make it hard. It's not my forgiveness you're after and we both know it."

Voss stood with her hands at her sides.

"Eleven years ago I came to your kitchen in the snow with a receipt and a writ and a company of
marines on the road behind me, and I told you the light was a door and the dark was a harbour and
that if the door was shut the child would live. I did not lie to you. I have never once been able to
say I lied to you, and that is the worst of it. I believed it and I was wrong and I have spent eleven
years being certain in public." Her voice did not change. "I put out the light of Carrow. I put out
seven more after it. I put a stipend schedule in front of an order that had nothing and I made it
complicit, and I signed every quarter myself so that nobody else would have to, and I told myself
that was courage. It was bookkeeping. The coast paid for it in eleven years and my brother paid for
it at the turn of the sixth hour, and I have never said any of this out loud to anybody, and I am
saying it now, to you, in front of a fleet and eight hundred and eighty people who cannot hear me."

She stopped.

"That's the true thing," she said. "There isn't a better one. I would do it again, and I would be
wrong again, and I know that now, and I did not know it then, and I am sorry."

Ede sat with it. The sea went over the edge.

"I know," she said at last. "I wrote it in my book the night you left. I wrote *she believes it* and
I underlined it twice, and then I sat in that kitchen for eleven years and held a ring round an
island with no light in it, and do you know what I was doing?" She looked up. "I was disagreeing
with you. That's all. Every night for eleven years I stood on that wall and disagreed with the
Admiral of the Fleet, and it held, and it held because it couldn't be studied and not because I was
right."

"Mrs Calloway—"

"You'll want something to do with your hands," said Ede. "Everybody does, after." She fished in her
coat and brought out a thing the size of a finger: a brass wick-knife, worn to the shape of a hand,
with a hook at one end for lifting a wet wick out of a drowned cup. She held it up. "That's the
Second's room you're thinking about. Konrad's. It'll be full of salt to the height of a man and
there'll be a lens in it with three hundred pounds of green glass in a frame that's rusted through,
and there is no light on this coast that will go in it this year or next. But the room can be
cleaned."

Voss looked at the knife for a moment. Then she took it.

"I have no authority to enter a Crown tower," she said.

"There isn't a Crown," said Ede. "You told me so yourself at midnight, and Mr Vey wrote it down, and
Mr Vey's paperwork is the only law this coast has got."

---

She gave the fleet away at noon, on the *Surety*'s deck, with the crews mustered and the free
crews in everything they had.

"I am out of authority," she said. "The Crown's term ended at midnight and I did not renew it and I
would not have been allowed to. There is no Admiral of the Fleet. There is a coast with eight
hundred and eighty people on a beach and twenty-two sail and four dark towers and one that never went
out, and there is a
marshal of the Emberwatch with a covenant in her pocket and a clerk who can write down anything you
like." She looked along the deck. "Marshal Hale has the fleet. Mr Vey has the paperwork. Mr Kettle
has the north road and the parish books. The *Vigil* takes me to Brinehollow. She is nobody's ship now
— she has been the Ash's ship and she has been mine, and she is a hull with a bad name and a window
nobody can light — and I would be obliged if somebody would sail her to Saltmere at the end of it and
hand her over with my compliments and a bill for eleven years."

Nobody said anything for a moment.

Then Kettle took his hat off.

It went along the deck like a wind through barley: the free crews first, because they had never
taken orders from her and had voted against her plan and then sailed anyway, and then the Crown's
captains, who had followed her into the Cradle and would have followed her further, and then the
marines, and then Hale, who took her hat off last and held it against her chest and looked at the
deck.

Voss looked at them all for a moment.

"Put them back on," she said. "There's a coast to walk."

---

She went north at two o'clock with Sallow and eleven men and a ship with no window lit, and
before she went she came aft to where Mira was writing at the chart table.

"Keeper."

"Admiral."

"I am not that." Voss put a folded paper on the table. "My brother. Mr Sallow has the hour in the
fleet's book and the fleet's book goes to Saltmere, and the Throne will file it in a cellar and it
will be read by a clerk in forty years who will not know what a keeper is. So I am asking you for
the other one. The one that gets carried."

Mira opened the notebook.

She wrote it on the page after the verse, in ink, in a fair hand, because it was going to be read by
somebody who had not been there:

*Konrad Voss. Keeper of the Second, at Brinehollow. Nine years. The Admiral's brother. He went into
the water the year the light went out and came up out of it hollowed. He was in Brinehollow when the
sea came up through the second floor of it; he has sat in a chair aft of his sister's flagship for
eleven years with a chessboard and a window, and he has trimmed a wick every day of it, and he has
never once asked a question. On the ninth night of the ninth month
he walked down a shingle at the foot of the Fall in a good coat with his hands at his sides, and when
his sister spoke to him he turned his head, and he knew her for a moment, and he said her name.*

She read it back to herself. Then she read it out loud, because the Admiral had asked for the one
that gets carried and carrying it meant saying it.

Voss listened with her hands behind her back.

"He said *Ilse*," she said.

"He did."

"In my book it says he was collected at the turn and that there is no account of the Fall."

"Then your book is wrong," said Mira, "and mine has him in it, and mine is the one that goes in a
coat."

Voss looked at the page for a long time. Then she took her gloves off the chart table, and put them
on, and went up the ladder, and that was the last anybody on the Reach saw of the Admiral of the
Fleet for a year and a half.

---

They started the walk at four in the afternoon, because it took that long to find out how to start
it.

Two hundred and forty of the Hollowed were from the far coasts — Barrowfoot and the Hollow Bell and
the villages under the Sill — and the ships could take those, with a guide aboard each and a parish
book open on the hatch. The other six hundred and forty were from the coast road itself, which runs
from the Fall to Saltmere with a village every eight or nine miles, and those had to walk.

Except that they did not walk. That was the difficulty. Eight hundred and eighty people stood in
rows on a shingle with their faces to the water, and Rook put a lamp on a pole and walked north up
the shingle with it, and nothing happened at all.

"They're not following," said Pell.

"No," said Rook.

He came back and stood with the lamp and thought about it for a while, and then he said, "It is not
the light. It is that there is a light and it is going somewhere." He turned to Odo Pike. "When the
Ash took them south, what did they follow?"

"The window," said Odo Pike. "The one lit window on the *Vigil*. They followed it from Barrowfoot to
the Fall, four days, and it never went out, and they walked behind it."

"Then we want a window," said Rook, "and we want somebody in front of it who is not going to stop."

So they did it with the last thing on the coast anybody would have thought to use: the *Vigil*'s
launch, with a lamp in the bows and a canvas dodger rigged behind it so the light stood up like a
window, rowed north along the shore at the pace of a walking man, and the Hollowed came off the
shingle and followed it up the coast like water finding a slope.

Rook walked on the shingle abreast of the boat with his dead sword on his back and his boots full of
water and did not stop for nine hours.

---

They made eight miles the first day and eleven the second, which everybody agreed was good going for
a coast with no carts on it.

At the first village — four houses and a smoke-house and a slip, and a woman in a doorway who had
been standing there for a week — the guides went in first.

Odo Pike did that part. He had eleven guides with their sleeves rolled up and their bands lit, and
they went into every house on that street before the column came, and they lit the lamp in the kitchen
of each one and stood in the doorway with the parish book open, and when the Hollowed came up the
street the guides said their names.

"Tomas Rudd. This is your house. Go in."

And Tomas Rudd, who had walked sixty miles and stood four days in the rain and could not have told
you his own name, turned off the road and went in and stood in his kitchen with his hands at his
sides until the guides came back for him in the morning.

That was the whole of the method and it worked on nine houses out of ten, and the tenth was the one
that had nobody left in it to call a name.

"Write it down," Mira said to Pell, who had been given a notebook of his own and a pencil, because
somebody had to count doors and she was going to be busy. "House, parish, name if we have it. If we
haven't got it, write *no name in the book* and put the parish."

"How many will there be?"

"I don't know. That's why you're counting."

By the fourth day Pell had two hundred and eleven lines and had stopped asking how many there would
be.

---

Somewhere on the second night, in the dark, with the column stopped on the road and the lamps hooded
and the sea going somewhere below them, Pell heard somebody humming.

He got up on his elbows. It was in the column, close, three or four people back, and it was not one of
the guides and it was not one of the crew, and it was not coming out of anybody's mouth that Pell
could find.

"Miss," he said. "Miss. Listen."

Mira listened.

It was four lines, over and over, in a voice with no breath in it, the way a person hums who has
forgotten they are doing it:

*Sleep, and the light will keep the rest.*  
*Sleep, and the dark will do its best.*  
*I have your hand. I have your name.*  
*The water takes us both the same.*

Pell went along the column with a lamp and looked at every face in the row it came from, and there
were nine people in that row and four of them were men and two were children and one was a woman of
about sixty and none of them was humming, and he came back with his hands shaking.

"One of them's doing it," he said. "Miss, one of them's in there and it's singing."

"Yes," said Mira.

"Then we can get them out. If there's somebody still in there—"

"Pell." She was writing it down as she said it, because that was what she did now. "If there is
somebody still in there, they have been in there for eleven years, and they are humming a lullaby in
a column of eight hundred people, and they have not asked us for anything. We will not go and get them
out. We will write down where they are and we will leave them alone, and every night from now on
somebody will sit up with the column and listen, and if it is ever singing in the same place twice we
will know a good deal more than we do now."

"That's not an answer."

"No," said Mira. "It's a notebook entry. It's what I've got."

She wrote it in the book that night, four lines and a note about the row, and under it: *somebody in
there is still somebody. Do not go and get them out. Sit up and listen.*

They never heard it again on the rest of the walk. Pell sat up every night of it.

---

Candlewick was the fifth day, and Candlewick was Orren Beck.

He had walked the whole way from the Fall because there was nowhere to put him: he was a keeper of
the Fourth, he had paid a season at the turn, and he was a big slow man of fifty-five who had come
off the water asking, very politely, whether anybody could tell him what a boat was. He walked in the
middle of the column with a blanket round his shoulders and he did not complain and he did not ask
again, and on the fourth night he said, to nobody in particular, "I had a pub."

"You did," said Mira.

"Was it a good one?"

"It was the best room on this coast," said Mira, "and I have written down what was in it."

So they brought him up the street at Candlewick at four in the afternoon with the column stopped on
the green and the whole parish out, and the pub was shut, and the sign over the door was hanging by
one hook, and the door was not locked.

Jory Beck was inside with a cloth in his hand and forty clean glasses on a shelf and had been
standing behind that bar for four days with nobody to serve, because he had heard what came up the
coast road and he had not known whether his father would be in it or on it.

"Da," he said.

Orren Beck stood in the doorway with his hat in his hands.

"This is a good room," he said.

"It is," said Jory. "It's got a good window. The sign drips."

"Does it?"

"Every time it rains."

Beck came in and put his hand flat on the bar, the way a man does in a strange house when he is
working out whether he is welcome, and looked at the shelves and the fire and the forty little brass
cups round the rim of the lens hanging over it, and Mira watched him look for the thing he had paid
for, the fog on the windows, the taproom at closing time, the last man out saying goodnight twice,
and find nothing at all.

"There's a page up there," said Jory.

Above the bottle shelf, in a space the size of a playing card cut out of the varnish, was a page in
Mira's hand from eleven months before: *Jory Beck. Born the third of Ninthmonth. Son of Orren Beck,
keeper of the Fourth, and of Maret Beck, who had a temper. He went into the water the year the light
went out, at six years old, and his father held him up for a count of forty.*

"Read it," said Beck.

"Da—"

"Read it out loud. It's what the page is for."

Jory read it. His voice did something on the last line and got it back, the way his father's had,
and the whole taproom stood still for it, and when he had finished Orren Beck put both hands on the
bar and said, "I know it in here." He put a fist against his chest. "I know it the way you know a
word is a word. I haven't got the rest."

"That's all right," said Jory Beck. "I've got the rest. I'll be saying it every morning before we
open, and you can stand there and hear it, and one of these days you'll know it out here as well."

Beck looked at him for a long moment.

"Who are you, then?"

"I'm Jory. I'm your son."

"Huh," said Orren Beck, and then, because he was a big slow man who had been rained on for thirty
years and had walked ninety miles in six days, he sat down on the nearest stool and put his face in
his hands, and his son came round the bar and put an arm across his shoulders and let him.

---

Mira wrote it up that night at the bar with the fire going and the parish in and out and the column
bedded down on the green, and Ede beside her with her stick against the brass rail.

"He can't hold a lens," said Mira. "He hasn't got the hands for it yet and he hasn't paid a season
and he may never have the money for an Ember, and the Fourth's cup is empty and the Fourth's frame
is rusted and there's no light in Candlewick at all."

"Then he keeps the lens over the bar," said Ede, "and he lights it with candles like his father did
for eleven years, and every soul who comes in that door sees it, and when the Crown gets round to
cutting new Embers he'll be first in the queue with a clean room and a written record and a father
who kept the Fourth before him." She tapped her stick on the floor. "That's a keeper, girl. It isn't
always a ring."

So Mira wrote it in the notebook, on the page after Konrad Voss:

*Jory Beck. Keeper of the Fourth, at Candlewick, in the room his father kept it in. Taught by his
father, who does not know him and knows the trade, which is the strangest arrangement I have ever
written down. The lens is over the bar with forty candles in the rim and a page pasted above the
bottle shelf, and every morning before he opens he reads it out loud, and his father stands in the
door to the back with a tray in his hands and does not come in and does not go away.*

*Nobody has told Orren Beck that he taught his son that. He thinks it is the boy's own idea. Ede says
leave it. I am leaving it.*

---

They gave Isolde her ship back at Candlewick on the sixth morning, and it took four lines and a
stamp and a man who could not read his own handwriting.

"The entry lapsed at the term," said Vey, on the quay, with the ledger open and his finger under the
place. "The hull and her company were entered as Crown property under the term, on terms, and the
terms expired at midnight on the ninth with everything else the Crown owned on this coast. She is
yours. She was always going to be yours. I wrote the terms myself and I wrote them badly and it is the
best bad writing I have ever done."

Isolde stood on the quay with her arms folded and looked at ninety feet of black hull with her crew
on the yards.

"I've got a ship," she said, "and no name for her. I know it had a bell in it. I have been standing on
a quay for six days listening to people talk about the *Mercy Bell* and thinking, that's a good name,
whose is it?"

"Yours," said Mira.

"Is it?"

"It is now." Mira took out the pencil. "Mercy Bell. Two words. I'll write it on the bell for you and
you can look at it whenever you like."

Isolde Sable looked at the water for a moment, and then she said, "That is a terrible name for a
ship," and went up the plank, and they heard her telling the crew it was a terrible name all the way
for'ard.

---

That night they were three days short of Carrow with a fair wind and the coast on the starboard bow,
and Teo was in the bows with his feet over the stem and his eyes shut.

Mira sat down beside him with the notebook and did not open it.

"I can't see it," he said. "The road. I keep looking at where it ought to be and there's water."

"You had it for a season."

"I had it, and I spent it, and I would spend it again tomorrow, and that is not the same as still
having it." He opened his eyes. "What
are we, now? You're a keeper with no vow and I'm a navigator who can't navigate and Ede's
seventy-one and Rook's got a dead sword and a ring he can't give back. That's the whole crew of the
Ninth. That's the coast's last light."

"Yes," said Mira.

"You're supposed to say something better than yes."

"I've been saying better than yes for a year," said Mira. "It's how I got here."

Teo laughed, once, and then stopped.

"Say the thing," he said. "The thing we said at the bow."

"I forget things," she said. "That's the whole of what I do now. I have paid eight times and every one
of them went out of me like a hand out of a pocket and I did not know until later, and one of these
days it will be you. Not the words. The *you*."

"Then say the thing."

"If I forget you—"

"Then I'll introduce myself again," said Teo.

And that was all of it, and it had cost nothing, and it was the only vow on that ship that either of
them could still say.

---

Later, when it was properly dark and the lamp was hooded and the watch had gone for'ard, she said,
"There's another one."

"Another what?"

"Ede's verse. Under the long form. She wrote it at nineteen." Mira had it on the page in front of
her, and the lamp was hooded, and she could not have read it if she had wanted to, and did not need
to. "I'm going to say it and you're going to say it and we're going to get it wrong, and then we'll
have said it."

"That's not how verse works."

"It's how everything else works."

So they said it, badly, in the dark, in the bows of a black ship three days short of Carrow:

*You are my left, I am your right.*  
*You hold the dark, I hold the light.*  
*If one of us goes out tonight,*  
*the other one keeps walking.*

"When," said Teo.

"It says if."

"It says if and it means when and you know it means when."

"It means *if*," said Mira, "because I have to be able to say it, and I can't say when."

Teo was quiet for a moment. Then he said the second stanza, and he got the third line wrong on
purpose so that she would have to correct him, and she did, and by the fourth line they were both
saying it and neither of them was laughing.

*No ring, no crown, no oath to bind —*  
*just: I will not leave you behind.*  
*Two sparks that never learn to fight,*  
*and out of the two, one steady light.*

"That's Ede's," said Teo. "The last bit. That's the bit she wrote at nineteen."

"No," said Mira. "That's the bit I wrote at midnight at the Fall, and I put it under hers, and I
haven't told her yet, and she is going to have something to say about the rhyme."

---

She said her name for him twice that night, and then he said it for her, which was the arrangement
they had made without either of them proposing it.

"Mira Calloway," said Teo. "Keeper of the Ninth. Lit Carrow in Sixthmonth. Walked a road in the
Gloaming with a lens in a bucket. Paid eight times and kept a notebook and asked everybody's name."

"That's a lot to say every night."

"I've got the whole voyage," said Teo. "I'm not the navigator any more."

Below, in the cabin, Ede was awake with the lamp turned down, and Mira went down and sat on the
chest and said, "Say it for me."

"Light what you can," said Ede Calloway. "Count what you lose. Answer for what you see."

Mira said it after her. She got *light what you can* and *count what you lose* and then she was at
the third line and there was nothing on the other side of it, the way there had been nothing at the
turn, and she sat with her mouth open in a cabin on a ship on the Reach and could not finish three
lines that fit on a matchbox.

"Again tomorrow," said Ede.

"Again tomorrow," said Mira, and wrote it down, because that was what she did now.

---

Carrow came up out of the sea at six in the morning on the ninth day, and it was the smallest island
Mira had ever seen, which was strange, because she had lived on it for eighteen years.

Four hundred people had lived there in Sixthmonth. There were none of them now. The doors were open
the way the whole coast's doors were open, and a hen was in the road, and the boat sheds had the
smell of tar and nothing else, and the tower stood up out of the hill with its lamp room dark and its
gallery rail rusting, exactly as it had stood on the morning she climbed it with a box of matches.

Ede came off the boat with her stick and did not go up the hill.

She went along the harbour to the sea-wall instead, and stood where she had stood every night since the dark, and took off her ring, and put it down on the stone.

Mira stood beside her and did not say anything, because there was nothing to say that the ring was
not already saying.

"I kept it ready," said Ede. "That's all it was. Four miles of nothing, held, every night, in case.
And in case came." She looked at the ring on the wall. "I'll not take it up again. I'm seventy-one
and my hip's gone and I have been doing it for eleven years and I would like to sit down."

"Then sit down."

"I intend to." Ede sat on the wall with her back against the bollard. "Go on up. It's a hundred and
twelve steps and you know every one of them."

---

It is a hundred and twelve steps and she knew every one of them.

The lamp room was warm. That was the first thing, the thing she had never been able to explain to
anybody, the thing she had written in the notebook at the top of page one: a stone room at the top
of a tower in the wind, and it was warm, and it smelled of brass and oil and the sea, and the floor
was clean because Ede had been up with a broom on the last morning before the coast emptied.

The housing was on its bracket. The wick was trimmed. The lens was clean.

The Ninth's Ember came out of the bucket and into the cup the way it had gone in the first time, and
she set the wick to it, and got the matches out of her pocket, and then she stood there with a match
in her hand and could not say the vow.

She tried. She tried the long form and got as far as *in the lit hour* and the rest of it was a
staircase with the middle steps taken out. She tried the short form and got two lines.

So she said the bit she could say, which was seven words out of the middle of it, and she said them
out loud to a lamp in an empty room at the top of a tower:

"I am here, and I can see."

She struck the match.

The wick took it the way a wick takes a match, which is to say like a thing that has been waiting.

And the lens took the light and threw it, and the light went out over Carrow and along the mole and
up the hill and into the windows of four hundred empty houses, and out over the water for nine miles,
and it did not cost her anything at all.

She stood at the glass with her hand flat on the frame for a long time, waiting for the bill, and the
bill did not come.

That frightened her more than any of the others had.

She got the notebook out and wrote it down with the light on the page.

*Lit Carrow at twenty past six on the eighteenth of the ninth month, with seven words out of the
middle of the long form and a match. It cost me nothing. I have checked twice. I do not know why it
should cost nothing and I am writing it down before I find out.*

Then she went down a hundred and twelve steps, and out into the yard, and Ede was sitting on the
sea-wall with her stick across her knees and the light on her face.

"You said it," said Ede.

"I said a bit of it."

"The lamp heard the meaning," said Ede Calloway. "It never heard anything else in four hundred
years. Now sit down and tell me what you're going to do about the dark towers, because I have
been thinking about it on the wall and I have got opinions."

---

It took a season to light the coast, and it was done by people who had each lost the thing they were
best at.

Saltmere, the last night of the ninth month. Ossian Vey, licensed keeper of the Fifth for nine
years and a clerk for nine more, climbed his own tower with a Crown sergeant and a clerk of Hale's and
lit the Fifth with an Ember out of a strongroom that no longer had an owner. There was a crowd in the
square. Nobody made a speech. He said the long form from beginning to end, out loud, without a pause,
and the wick took it, and he came down and dictated the entry to the clerk because he could not read
what he had written.

"Read it back," he said.

The clerk read it back.

"Then it says what I meant," said Vey, and went to find his supper.

The Sill, the third night of the tenth month. Meridew, sixty years old, with a bad hip and eleven
years of nothing in her head, lit the Third at a drowned tower with two guides holding the ladder, and
said the short form, and got to the third line, and stopped.

"Answer for what you see," said Odo Pike, behind her.

"I can't see her," said Meridew. "I can see the girl in the green dress and I can't see the name at
the top of the page."

"Then answer for what you can see."

So she did, and the light went up, and afterwards Mira wrote a page for her with a blank line at the
top of it and the words *Meridew's daughter, name unknown, to be found* in the middle, and Meridew
kept it in her coat, and took it out at the Sill every quarter when the relief ship came, and asked
every man aboard whether he had ever heard of a girl in a green dress with her hair wet.

Candlewick, all winter. The Fourth's tower stood dark with a rusted frame and no Ember in it and
no prospect of one, and the Fourth's lens hung over a bar with forty candles in the rim, and Jory Beck
lit it at six every evening and read the page above the bottle shelf out loud first, and his father
stood in the doorway to the back with a tray in his hands.

Brinehollow, the fourth night of the tenth month, and every night after it for a year and a half.
Ilse Voss, with no authority of any kind, cleaned a lamp room full of salt to the height of a man,
and got the glass out of the frame, and wrote the hour of her brother's going-in on the inside of the
door in pencil, and then wrote underneath it the thing she had said to Ede Calloway on a shingle at
the end of the world, in full, in her own hand, because there was nobody there to hear it and a true
thing said to nobody is still said.

The Gull's Teeth. The keeper of the Sixth got a letter in the spring, in a fair hand, with a copy
of the account enclosed and a note that said: *You were right, and you were also wrong, and both of
those are in the book. The lights are ours again. You may put yours out.*

He wrote back on the back of the warrant, in a hand like a fence, four lines: *It has been lit two
years and I will keep it lit eleven more. If you are ever in the north, the door is not locked. Bring
your own oil. There is nothing living under the bridge any more.*

The Fall. A relief cutter goes down every quarter with oil and salt beef and a letter, and a boy
of sixteen keeps a small thin light in a cup packed with oakum on a rock at the end of the world, and
is called, by everybody on the coast and eventually by the Crown's own ledgers, the Keeper of the
Fall.

---

Hale came back from Saltmere in the spring with the fleet's book under her arm and four lines from
the Throne, and she read them out on the quay at Carrow to nine people and a dog.

*The term having expired and the matter having been otherwise determined, the Crown recognises the
keepers of the nine lanterns, restores the guides and the drowned to the Glass Roll, and cancels the
stipend schedule. The Crown will supply stone, oil and iron to the value of the schedule for one
year. The Crown will not apologise. The Crown observes that there is no Admiral of the Fleet, and asks
by whose authority the coast is kept.*

"And you wrote back?" said Mira.

"I wrote back," said Hale. "I said: *by the authority of nine keepers and eight hundred and eighty
people and a boy on a rock. Find another arrangement and see how it holds.*"

"Did they answer?"

"They appointed a commission," said Hale. "Commissions are what a Throne does instead of an answer.
The commission will sit for two years and produce a report, and in the meantime the stone and the oil
will arrive, because I signed for them, and the report will say what I tell it to say, because I am
going to write it."

---

On the last page of the notebook, on a night in the spring with the light going round and the window
open and the sea making its ordinary noise nine miles out, Mira Calloway wrote this:

*Nine keeps. Four lit: the Sill, Saltmere, the Gull's Teeth and Carrow. Candlewick keeps forty
candles in a lens over a bar. Brinehollow has a clean room and no light in it, the Kindling keeps its
fire small on purpose, and Wren's Fold has nobody in it at all. The Hollow Bell is lost, and its bell
rings with no wind when the Ash is close, and it has not rung since the ninth night. That is the
coast, and it is not what it was, and it is ours.*

*Not one keep on this coast is held by a person who has everything. One by a girl who cannot say the
vow, one by a woman who cannot remember her daughter's name, one by a man who cannot read his own
hand, one by a man who cannot remember his son, one by a boy the Assize left behind, one by a woman
cleaning a room she is not allowed to light. Ede says that is not a weakness in the arrangement. Ede
says a light is not kept by a person who has everything; it is kept by a person who has paid, and
knows the price, and gets up anyway.*

*Light what you can. Count what you lose. Answer for what you see.*

*There are eight hundred and eighty of them on this coast and four hundred and eleven have a name in
Pell's book and the rest have a parish and a door and no name in the book, and we are going to find
them all. Pell has an inventory of the dead that would do credit to a treasury, and he sits up every
night in case somebody sings.*

*Teo has started a notebook. He cannot see the road any more so he writes down what he can see, which
is mostly me, and the weather, and the way the light comes round the gallery at four in the morning.
He says a navigator who cannot navigate is still a navigator if he keeps the record. I have not
argued with him. I have paid eight times and I am not paying for that one.*

*Konrad Voss, keeper of the Second, who said his sister's name.*
*Orren Beck, keeper of the Fourth, who held his son up for a count of forty.*
*The nineteen others who went over the lip on the ninth night, whose names I have in the parish books
and in Mr Sallow's ledger and will write out properly before the winter.*

*I have lost my grandmother's face and her voice, the smell of the kitchen at Carrow and the smell of a
lamp, three years of school and the neighbours and a rhyme, my mother's name, the winter I was
twelve, and the joining-up of the words I have been saying since I was six.*

*I have kept the notebook. I have kept every name anybody gave me. I have kept the road, and the
light, and the coast, and him.*

*The vow does not say win. It says answer for.*

*Mira Calloway.*
*Keeper of the Ninth.*
*I lit it.*

---

She put the notebook in her coat, and went up a hundred and twelve steps, and trimmed the wick.

Below her Ede was asleep in the kitchen with her stick against the chair, and Teo was on the gallery
with a lamp and a pencil, writing down the weather; and out beyond the mole the sea went over the
edge of the world in the ordinary way, exactly as it had done before there was anybody on that coast
to see it, and would go on doing it after the last of them was gone.

The sea did not mind.

The light did.
