# Chapter Twelve

The Gull's Teeth were two rocks standing a hundred yards apart with a tower on each,
and a rope bridge between them, and water going through the gap at eight knots on the
ebb.

"Six hours ago," said Isolde, "I would have said this was the best place on the Reach
to be caught."

The *Vigil* was four miles west and closing, three-decked, brass at the masthead and
no lantern anywhere on her, and behind her the Crown's boats were already down and
rowing.

"Now?" said Mira.

"Now it's the best place on the Reach to be caught by a man who has to explain
himself to a board afterwards."

They went in on the flood, which is the only way in, with the light in the bucket
hooded and the whole crew on deck and Pell in the chains calling the water in a voice
that had gone up an octave and stayed there.

The Teeth had been a light once, the Sixth: two towers on two rocks, a lamp on each,
and a rope bridge for the keepers to cross so that the bridge itself could be seen
from the sea and the channel marked by a thing men could look at. The bridge was still
there. The towers were still there. The something under the bridge was still there,
which was the reason no crew had salvaged a rope's worth of either tower in eleven
years.

"What is it?" said Mira.

"Nobody knows," said Nettle, tying off. "It eats wrecks. Wood, rope, iron, men who
get too close. It has never once come ashore and it has never once left."

"How do you know it's the same one?"

"Because it's been there forty years," said Marrow, "and it's grown."

---

The Crown's boats came in behind them into the channel, because the channel was the
only way in and because Vey — standing at the *Vigil*'s rail with his hat off looking
exactly like a man doing his job — had spent the morning reading the chart.

Eight boats. Two of them were rowed by Hollowed, which Mira knew because the oars went
in time: no curses, no counting, no cursing of the counting, just eight oars in the
water and out again, perfect, patient, and wrong.

"Grapples!" Pell shouted.

"Let them come," said Isolde. "Boarding a lugger in a six-knot channel with a rope
bridge above you and something in the water under — no. They'll try the bridge."

They tried the bridge.

The first four men came over the rope bridge from the eastern tower, roped together
like climbers, and they were halfway across when the thing under the bridge came up.
Mira never saw it properly. She saw the bridge go slack, and the four men go down, and
the water open and close like a mouth, and then she saw the rope bridge hanging in two
pieces, its near end still tied to the western tower and its far end swaying over
nothing.

Two men were left standing on the eastern tower with nothing to do.

"Vey's not sending any more over the bridge," said Teo.

"He's not," said Isolde. "He's waiting for the tide."

The tide was the trouble. At slack water the channel would open, and the *Vigil*'s
boats could come alongside on both sides, and the *Mercy Bell* would be a wall with
men on top of it.

"How long?" said Mira.

"An hour," said Teo. "Maybe two."

---

They spent the first hour being brave.

Rook cut the first grapple with his ember-blade and it went out when he did it, because
a man swinging a sword to cut a rope is not a man with a conviction about the rope, and
he stood there looking at his own dark blade like a boy who has dropped a bowl.

"Just cut it with the knife," said Isolde.

"The knife is smaller. The knife is worse." He cut the second grapple with the knife.
"Ser Rook," he said, to nobody, "would like it noted that he is aware of the
indignity."

Pell took a splinter through the forearm and swore in a manner that made Nettle laugh,
which was the first time Nettle had laughed on the voyage.

Sallow stood at the stern with his two swords out and his shoulders down and did not
make a single joke for forty minutes, which frightened Mira more than the *Vigil*.

And the light in her ring got warmer.

Not brighter. Warmer, the way a hand warms when somebody takes it. It had been there
since the tower and she had told nobody about it and had looked at it every night
before she slept, and she had come to understand two things about Alys Wren's last
gift: that it was not a lamp, and that it wanted to be *used*, and that she did not
know how.

---

Then the tide turned, and the boats came, and there was no more brave.

They came from both sides. The grapples went up, six, eight, twelve, and the *Mercy
Bell* heeled and the planks came down and the Crown's men came over the rail in three
places at once.

"Chain! Pell, chain — no, *left* —"

Mira was by the mast with her back to the bucket and a boat-hook in her hands and no
idea what to do with it. Isolde was at the rail with her long knife. Rook was at the
break in the rail with a dark blade held two-handed and no light in it at all and was
nonetheless putting men back into the water, one after another, in a way that made a
great deal of sense: conviction is not the same as strength, and a man who has already
decided he will not move does not need to glow.

Somebody knocked the hood off the bucket.

The light came out white and level, straight along the deck, and in it Mira saw the
whole thing at once: two men going over, Pell on his back with a spear-point at his
throat, Isolde's knife caught on a cutlass, Sallow killing a man with a bored
expression, and, at the stern rail, Nettle with her bag open and her sleeves rolled
up, counting the boats the way a surgeon counts beds.

Mira's hands went cold.

Not now. She could not afford it now.

The coal in her ring turned over.

And that was the moment Mira understood what it was for — not because she was clever,
and not because anybody had told her, but because she was standing in the middle of a
deck with her ears full of noise, and the noise itself was a thing the light could
hold.

"Rook!" she shouted. "Sing!"

"What?"

"*Sing the hymn!*"

"I—"

"Do it!"

So Ser Cadan Rook, of the Emberwatch, twenty-six years old, holding a dark blade with
two hands and bleeding from the shoulder, sang.

*"By the wick that is not mine, by the fire I did not light —"*

Off every note available. Loud as a damaged horn.

And Mira took her hands off the boat-hook and put them flat on her own chest and
pulled, and the coal came out of her ring and went up her arm like a swallow of hot
water, and she opened her mouth and took the note off him and made it hers.

It did not feel like singing. It felt like holding a rope, exactly like holding a
rope: a heavy line of something pulling at the small of her back and the backs of her
eyes, and she got both hands round it, and she pulled it out of the air and pushed it
away from her.

The light left her hand.

It went out over the water in a single line — and then it was not a line, it was a
*ring*, a widening ring, like the ring that comes off a struck bell, and the ring had
a sound in it, and the sound had her voice in it and Rook's voice in it and, after a
moment, Marrow's, and then Pell's, and then, of all people, Sallow's.

The note reached the first boat.

---

A boy from the farms on the eastern moors, fifteen years old, a Crown oar in his hands
and a Hollowed man's hands on the oar in front of him, heard, in his own voice, in his
own accent:

*You were never going to be a fisherman like your father, and this is not a boat, and
you could put the oar down.*

He put the oar down.

---

The note reached the second boat. The third. It went past them and through them and up
the cliffs and out across the water, and it kept going, and it did not stop at the
*Vigil*, because nothing has ever stopped at the *Vigil*.

On the *Vigil*'s deck, thirty men stopped working, and a captain stopped shouting, and
the Admiral herself came out of her cabin and stood at the rail and looked at a light
that had no lantern in it and did not say anything for a long time.

On the fourth boat, the Hollowed men at the oars stopped.

All of them. At once. Eight oars in the water, still, and eight faces turning to the
sound, and then the eight of them opened their mouths and began, very quietly, to make
the shape of it — no words, no tune, nothing but the shape a mouth makes when it is
trying to remember a word — and the boy from the farms climbed over one of them to get
away and the man did not notice, because for the first time in eleven years something
was happening inside him and it was not a job.

---

And on the *Vigil*'s deck, at the rail, where the chart was weighted down under a
brass paperweight, Ossian Vey heard his own voice.

He was nineteen. He was at the Fifth. A captain of the fleet had put his glove over
the glass and said it was for the good of the coast, and Vey had been holding the wick,
and Vey had said nothing, for eleven years, at every board and every assessment and
every signature, and the voice that came back to him over the water was his own voice
saying it again, in his own accent, at the volume he had used at nineteen.

*Nothing.*

His hand went to his ring. The glass was cracked straight through the seam, and the
brass was showing, and it had been brass all along and everybody on the water could
see it now.

"Stop the boats," he said.

"My lord?"

"*Stop the boats.*"

The boats stopped.

---

The toll went out of Mira like the tide going out of a harbour. She was on her knees
on the deck with her hands in her lap and her mouth open and no sound coming out of
it, and the ring on her finger was green-black and cold and empty.

"He's breaking off," said Teo, from the wheel, in a strange flat voice. "He's
signalling the boats back. West. He's going west."

"He's not going west," said Isolde. "*Vigil* doesn't go west."

"She is," said Teo. "She's going west."

Mira got up. She got up because the deck was full of men who had just been trying to
kill her and were now standing about with their arms down, and because Pell was
crying quietly over his arm, and because there was a thing she needed to check.

She went to the rail and looked at the water.

The channel at the Teeth was black and flat and full of broken oars. Nothing was
singing. The Hollowed men in the fourth boat were rowing again, in time, eight oars,
perfect, patient, wrong.

She tried to remember the winter she was twelve.

It had been the long one — the winter the boats did not go out, when the whole of
Carrow ate salt fish and the fever went through the east end and her grandmother made
a soup with a burnt onion in it because there was nothing else, and Mira had been ill
for nine days, and her grandmother had sat with her and told her the story about the
lighthouse and the ring and the boy who was not there, and the soup had been
disgusting, and she had asked for it every night for a year afterwards.

She remembered none of it.

Not the fever. Not the soup. Not the nine days. Not the story — and the story she had
known better than her own name, because it was the only one her grandmother ever told
twice.

There was a fact in her head where the winter had been: *you were ill for nine days
when you were twelve, and your grandmother made soup.* A fact, in her own voice, flat
as a page. No kitchen. No fever. No hands.

"Oh," said Mira.

There was a note in that, too, and she heard herself make it, and it was a small,
undignified, animal noise, and Teo got to her before she sat down.

"Tell me," he said. "You promised. Even the ones that don't sound like anything."

"A season," she said. "I paid a season."

"Which one?"

"Twelve." She looked at her ring, at the cold green stone, at the coal that was not
there any more. "The winter I was twelve. I can't find it."

Teo sat down on the deck beside her in the middle of the wreckage of the boarding and
held her wrist, hard, as though he could stop it going any further.

"Rook's hymn," he said. "That's what you used."

"It was the only song I knew. It was the only thing on this ship everybody—" she
stopped. "It worked because everyone sang it. It doesn't work if it's one person."

"Then we keep singing it," said Teo.

"It's a *terrible* hymn."

"It's the worst hymn I have ever heard in my life," said Teo, with feeling. "We're
never going to stop singing it."

---

The *Vigil* was a mile west and still going when the boat came.

It came alone, no flag, six rowers and an officer standing in the bows in dress grey,
and it stopped twenty yards off and the officer put his hands behind his back.

"The Admiral's compliments," he called. "She would like to speak with the keeper. This
afternoon. Aboard the *Vigil*, under a flag of truce, with no more than two of your
people, and she gives her word that the girl will come back."

"Her word," said Isolde, from the rail. "That's the funniest thing anyone's said all
day."

"The Admiral says to tell her," the officer called, "that she has heard the sound too,
and she would like to know what it was, and she is prepared to pay for the answer in
something other than a dousing fleet."

Mira looked at her hands. Then at Teo. Then at the empty ring.

"Tell her yes," she said.
