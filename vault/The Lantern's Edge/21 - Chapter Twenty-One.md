# Chapter Twenty-One

First light at Carrow is not an event. The sky goes from a black thing to a grey thing, the
gulls start, and the sea inside the wall begins to move again.

Ede Calloway stood with her hands out for a count of a hundred after that, and then she put
the ring down, and Mira felt it go the way you feel a door open in another room.

The water inside the ring got its head up. That was the only way to say it: four miles of
sea that had been lying flat as a table all night stood up and began to slap at the wall and
run at the shingle and make the small ordinary noise a coast makes, and it was the loudest
thing Mira had heard in a week.

"Eleven years," said Ede, "and I still expect it to be quiet when I let go."

"Does it hurt?"

"No." The old woman flexed her hands, one and then the other, and looked at them with mild
disapproval, the way you look at a tool that has got above itself. "It's like putting down a
bucket. The bucket doesn't hurt. It's the not putting it down that does."

Nine miles out, the *Vigil* lay at her anchor with her yards square and one window lit.

She had not moved all night. The four boats were still on the booms, and nothing had come
down them, and nothing had come up the road, and the ring had not been touched. The Ash had
sat nine miles off a coast it could not see the edge of and had done nothing at all, which
was worse than the bell, worse than the chains, worse than fifty-two people walking out of a
town in single file.

"It's waiting," said Pell.

"It's learning to wait," said Mira.

Rook came up the shingle with a bucket in each hand and set them down and did not say
anything about the ninety steps. His shoulder was wrapped and his surcoat was off and he had
been up and down the green twice before the sun was over the hill.

"Ninety steps," said Mira.

"A hundred and four," said Rook. "Six of them are wide. I have counted them, because I am
that sort of man, and because counting is the only thing I can do with my hands just now."

"You don't have to keep it."

"I said every night."

"That was a vow made at midnight to a woman holding four miles of sea. It doesn't have to
survive breakfast."

"No," said Rook. "It does."

---

The tower at Carrow is a hundred and twelve steps and a door with no lock, and the lamp room
at the top of it smelled of brass polish.

Mira stopped in the doorway with her hand on the frame and could not make the room agree
with the last time she had been in it.

The last time had been in Sixthmonth, in the dark, with a dead man's matches and the sea
full of ships that were not there. The glass had been black and the brass had been green and
the floor had been grit and bird lime and there had been a chair with a broken back.

Now the glass was clean. The brass was polished and the rails were polished and the great
lens stood in its frame with every ring of it clear, and the floor had been swept, and the
chair had a cushion on it worn through in the middle by eleven years of somebody sitting
down.

There was a log on a lectern by the window, open, with a pen beside it, and a stub of candle
in a saucer.

"Who did all this?" said Pell.

"Me," said Ede, from the stairs.

She came up the last six steps slowly and without any fuss, one hand on the rail, and stood
in her own lamp room for the first time since the night she put it out and looked around it
like a woman checking a room she had let to strangers.

"You kept it clean," said Mira.

"I kept it ready." Ede went to the chair and sat down in it and put her hands on her knees
the way she sat on the wall. "There's a difference. A clean tower is a thing you do on a
Tuesday. A ready tower is a thing you do in case you are told."

Mira looked at the lens. It was the Ninth's own, green-black, a hundred rings of it, with the
brass cup in the middle where the Ember went, and the cup was empty.

"It would take the light," she said. "It's a good lens. It's the best one left."

"Yes," said Ede.

"One match and we'd have nine miles of road."

"Yes," said Ede.

"Don't," said Rook, from the doorway, and then, because he had said it too fast for a knight:
"Ma'am. May I see the log."

Ede turned it round on the lectern with one finger.

The log of the Ninth had been kept by her family for two hundred and forty years, in
hands that got worse as the century went on, and the last eleven years of it were in one
hand and one sentence.

*Dark. Wick trimmed. Nothing to report.*

*Dark. Wick trimmed. Nothing to report.*

Mira turned the pages. The paper was good paper and the ink was good ink and there were five
hundred and seventy of them, in the same words, week after week, and the hand got no worse,
because the woman writing it had decided that this was the one thing she would not let go.

Near the back, a date, and an entry that was not the same.

*The eighteenth. A light on the water, south by the Cradle, at the second hour. Steady. Not
a ship's lantern. I have not seen a light on this water since the winter and I stood on the
wall until it went.*

Mira turned the page. The next entry was the same four words as all the others.

"She saw you," said Teo. He was on the top step with his hand on the frame. "That was the
lane. That was Candlewick."

"That was a light," said Ede. "I didn't know whose. I wrote it down because it was the first
thing in eleven years that was not the same as the day before."

---

They read the names at the top of the morning, because Ede said she had said them at first
light and she liked to hear them said by somebody else before the day got into her.

There was no ceremony in it either. She sat in the chair with the book open on her knees and
Mira sat on the floor with her back against the lens frame, and Ede handed her the book and
said, "Read them out. I'll say them back."

The book was fifty years old and had been re-covered twice in sailcloth. The names were in
columns, with the date at the head of each column and a mark against some of them, and the
first thing Mira noticed was that the columns were not the same as each other. Names would
appear in one and not the next. The names that disappeared did not come back.

"Tarn, Ossa."

"Tarn, Ossa," said Ede.

"Tarn, Hesk. Tarn, the little one, born in the spring."

"Hesk Tarn. The little one, born in the spring."

"Pellow, Ruth. Pellow, Mattias. Pellow, the dog, which is not a name but he answers to it."

"He answers to it," said Ede, and wrote something in the margin.

They went on. There were four hundred people in Carrow and Ede had about a hundred and
forty of them written down, which was everybody she had ever spoken to twice, and she said
every name back in a flat, careful voice, and after forty or fifty of them Mira understood
what the book was for.

It was not a memory. It was a net. Every morning the woman cast it and counted what came up,
and what did not come up had been taken, and she wrote the name again anyway, because a name
written down is a thing you can still hand to somebody.

"Gannet, Ruth," said Mira. "Gannet, Owen. Gannet, the boy."

"The Gannets," said Ede.

Mira looked at the page. Ruth Gannet, Owen Gannet, and under them a boy with no name in the
column, only *the boy*, in a hand that had pressed too hard.

"The family next door," said Ede. "To your grandmother's house. There was a gate."

"There was a gate," said Mira.

"The boy used to climb it. You were both always in trouble about the gate."

Mira sat on the floor of the lamp room with the book open and waited to feel something, and
the feeling did not come. She could see the gate. She could see the fence and the gap in the
hedge and the place where the hinge had come loose and her grandmother's month of being smug
about it. She could not put a face to *the boy*, and the name Gannet was three sounds.

"Say it again," said Ede.

"Gannet, Ruth. Gannet, Owen. Gannet, the boy."

"Good," said Ede, and did not ask her whether she remembered, which was the kindest thing
anybody did all week.

The names went on. There were pages at the back where the hand gave out: whole columns
written over each other, the same six names in a spiral, the pen going off the line. Ede
said, "That was the second winter," and turned the page herself.

And on the last page there was one name, and it was written four hundred times.

*Tess Calloway. Tess Calloway. Tess Calloway.*

Not in a column. Down the page, over the page, at angles, in three inks, and a line through
most of them — a single stroke, the way you cross out a word you have written wrong — and
then the same name written again underneath, and again, and again.

Mira's mouth was dry.

"Who is Tess Calloway?"

"My daughter," said Ede.

Mira looked at the page.

"She went into the water the night the light went out," said Ede. "I have that written too. I
don't have her face. I have the name, and I have the date, and I have the fact that she went
in, and I have written it four hundred times because I could not think what else to do with
an evening."

Mira sat with the book open and read the name again, and it was a name.

"Say it," said Ede.

"Tess Calloway."

"Again."

"Tess Calloway."

"Good," said Ede, and took the book back and closed it, and looked out of the window at the
ship. "Now you know what it's for. You're the only one of us who can still learn a name and
mean it. Don't waste the morning."

---

They argued about the tower at midday, in the lamp room, with the ship in the window.

It was Rook who put it best, because it was Rook's job to put things in the worst possible
way and call it duty.

"Nine miles of light," he said. "That is what a tower is for. If we light it, that thing out
there comes to the door — and the door is a rock and a reef and a ring of stone we can hold.
It cannot cross her edge, and it has spent a whole night proving it. Light the tower and it
comes in to a wall it cannot pass, and we have it in one place instead of loose on a coast."

"And it stays there," said Mira.

"It stays there."

"With four hundred people behind the wall and one woman holding it."

"Yes," said Rook.

"Ser," said Mira. "How long can she hold?"

Rook did not answer.

"I have one season," said Ede, from the chair. "Maybe two. That's not a figure of speech and
it's not a guess. I can feel the bottom of it the way you feel the bottom of a barrel."

"Then we light the tower and end it in one night," said Rook.

"You'd end it in one night," said Ede, "and I'd be holding a lit tower inside a ring, and the
thing would sit out there and never leave, because there'd be a light behind a wall and no way
in and all the time in the world. It would wait for me to die. It's very good at waiting,"
she said. "It's the best thing it does."

"It learned to wait last night," said Mira.

"It has always been able to wait," said Ede. "It has never had anything worth waiting for."

Sallow had been standing at the back with his hat in his hands, which was as close as he came
to a respectful posture, and he cleared his throat.

"May I," he said, "strike a professional note."

"No," said Pell.

"Mr Sallow," said Mira.

"I have been writing your movements down for six salt a week," said Sallow, "and I could tell
you where that ship will be before it gets there, and I have read the same reports you have
and I have written better ones. It is not here for her."

The room was quiet.

"Go on," said Mira.

"A woman holding a wall with no light in it is the least interesting thing on this coast,"
said Sallow. "It cannot see her. It has never been able to see her. If it were here for her it
would have come ashore anywhere on four miles of wall and hunted along it in the dark like a
man feeling for a keyhole. It has not. It has come to the one road, twice, and it has come
with a ship, and it is sitting out there with its boats on the booms because it is waiting for
something to be lit."

"It's waiting for me to put it down," said Ede.

"It's waiting for the girl," said Sallow. "She lit the Ninth in Sixthmonth and it turned
west. She lit a road at Candlewick and it came up out of the water with thirteen ships on
it. She stood on a quarterdeck and stopped six hundred men rowing, and by the end of the week
it had a three-decker. That is not a wall it is testing. That is a light it is following."

Mira looked at the window.

"If that's true," she said, "then every day I stand on this island I am the reason it's here."

"Yes," said Sallow.

"And if I take the light and go—"

"Then it goes with you," said Sallow, "and this parish goes back to being a dark island with
nothing behind the door, and nobody will ever know why."

Ede Calloway sat in her chair with her hands on her knees and did not say anything for a
while, and then she said, "Well. That's the first useful thing anybody's said in this room in
eleven years," and Mira could not tell whether she was furious or relieved.

---

They left at dusk, because that is when a thing like that has to be done.

The day went on the boat. Marrow carried the lens down a hundred and twelve steps on his
back with a rope through the frame and did not stop and did not let anybody help, and Pell
packed the chimney and the cask and the spare pins.

Teo was on the wall with his hand flat on the stone, and he had been there since the argument,
and he did not turn round when she sat down.

"You'll count in fives," he said.

"What?"

"Tonight. When it's bad." He shifted his hand on the stone. "You count in fives when it's
bad and in tens when it's good, and I can hear the difference from the other end of a boat,
and you'll be no good by the second watch, so I'll take the first."

Mira looked at the water. Nine miles out the ship sat with her boats on the booms.

"Don't let me sleep through the turn," she said.

"Then wake me at the turn and I'll be no good with my eyes open, which is how you like me."

Teo laughed, once, badly, and then stopped laughing and said, "What if it's not following the
light. What if it's following you."

"Then it's following a girl in a boat with a chipped lens, and it's going to be very
embarrassed in about three days."

"That's not a plan."

"No," said Mira. "It's a heading. Plans are for people with a ship."

Ossa Tarn brought the loaf, and a jar of something, and a blanket nobody had asked for.

"You'll not be back," she said.

"I don't know," said Mira.

"No," said Ossa. "Nobody ever knows. That's why we don't ask." She shoved the loaf at her.
"Take it. She'll not eat it and I'll be baking another one by Thursday, and I'd rather it
went to sea than to the gulls."

Mira held the loaf.

"Ossa. Why did you never ask her?"

The woman looked at the wall, where Ede was standing with her hands at her sides waiting for
the light to go.

"Because I knew the answer would be a job," said Ossa Tarn, "and I've got a shop."

Rook came down the green with the last bucket and set it by the boathouse door, and Ede was
waiting for him at the top of the slip with a small thing in her hand.

"It's no use to me," she said. "I've worn it for fifty years and I'd not know myself
without it, but I'd rather it went somewhere it might do something."

It was her keeper's ring. Green-black, worn thin on the inside, dead as a stone.

Rook looked at it for a long moment. Then he took the iron thumb-ring off his right hand and
put the glass one on in its place, and stood with his hands at his sides, the iron in his left
and the glass on his right, like a man carrying both halves of an argument.

"I keep no light of my own," he said.

"I know," said Ede. "That's why you get the empty one."

---

Mira went up the slip to say goodbye, and found that she had no idea what to say, which had
not happened to her at a gangway yet.

"Ede."

"Miss Calloway."

"I'm going north. There's a fleet at the Kindling and eight lanterns and an Admiral with a
writ, and I think I can stop some of it, and I don't know if that's true."

"Then go and be seen," said Ede. "It's the one thing they tell a keeper never to do, so it's
probably the right thing."

"Will you hold?"

"Until I can't."

"And then?"

"And then I'll be a woman on a wall with nothing left to hold, and you'll be somewhere else,
and that will have to be the arrangement." Ede put her hand out and touched the coat, at the
shoulder, the way you check that a thing is real. "What do I call you?"

"Mira."

"I know that. It's at the top of the book." She said it without any impatience. "What do you
call me?"

Mira stood on the slip at Carrow with her hands at her sides.

"I've been calling you Ede all day," she said.

"That's my name."

"It isn't the one I want."

Ede waited.

"Grandmother," said Mira.

The old woman shut her eyes.

"Say it again."

"Grandmother."

Ede got the book out from under her arm and the pencil from behind her ear and asked for the
date, and Mira gave it, and she wrote the date down, and then she wrote a line under it that
Mira could not see.

"I have it," she said. "I'll say it in the morning, with the names."

Then she tore a page out of the back of the book, and it came out badly, and she folded it
once and put it into Mira's hand.

"It's no good to me written four hundred times," she said. "One page won't matter. And you
can still learn a name and mean it, so learn it."

It was the page with her mother on it.

"Ede —"

"Go on," said Ede Calloway. "You're burning the last of the light and I've a wall to hold."

---

At dusk the ring went up, and Mira watched it from the boat, and it was the strangest thing
she had ever seen: an old woman putting both hands out at the waterline and four miles of
edge closing round an island without one spark of it showing anywhere.

Then she lit the lens.

The Ember went into the brass cup and the chimney took the light and the lane came out
across the water in a bar nine miles long, and the road came up under it in gold, and the
whole of Carrow stood up out of the dark behind her — the quay, the hill, the tower with its
glass black and its door open — and every window in the parish had somebody at it.

"North," said Teo. "Keep it north and a point west. There's a wall at forty feet and then it's
clean to the headland."

"Hold it," said Pell. "Miss — hold it."

She held it.

That was the whole of the trick and it was the hardest thing she had done: not a door, not a
hatch, not a rope. A bar of light nine miles long, steady, on water, with the boat moving,
for as long as it took.

And it took longer than she thought, because a ship at anchor does not move quickly. She
heard the capstan before she saw anything: the click and the groan of it coming up through
the water, and then the sound of a hundred and fifty tons of oak getting its head round, and
Pell said, "She's catting," and then, "She's drawing," and then nothing at all.

The one window stayed lit. The yards came round. The *Vigil* stood south and then stood
east and then stood north, into the lane, with her sails full of a wind that was going
somewhere else, and her bow wave came up white in the light.

"Nine miles," said Pell.

"Don't count it," said Mira. "Row."

Marrow rowed. Teo called the wall at forty feet and then the clean water past the headland,
and Mira held the bar out ahead of them over four miles of road and then five, and the ship
came on behind, and the island went dark astern of them, and the ring held, and nothing came
ashore at Carrow that night.

She held it for a count of four hundred, which she did in fives.

Then the lane went out, and the boat was dark, and her hands were shaking, and she found that
she had been saying a name to herself the whole time, over and over, the way you say a thing
to keep a rhythm.

*Tess Calloway. Tess Calloway. Tess Calloway.*

She reached for it.

"Who's Tess Calloway?" she said, out loud, and heard how the question sounded, and stopped.

Nobody answered. Ede was four miles astern with her hands out.

"Miss," said Pell, carefully. "You said it eleven times."

"I know," said Mira. "I know I did."

She got the book out. There was a page in the back of her coat, torn out of a bigger book,
with one name written four hundred times and a line through most of them, and she read it in
the dark by feel, letter by letter.

*T-e-s-s. C-a-l-l-o-w-a-y.*

It was a name. It was somebody's name. It had been her mother's this morning.

---

They put the lens up in the bows and pulled north, and the Ash's ship came on behind them
with one window lit, and the road ran out ahead of them under three feet of water. In the
morning they would need the light again, and the morning after that, and it would be three
nights to the Kindling.

Mira got her notebook out and wrote it up in the stern, by feel, with the pencil.

*Carrow is behind us and dark and the ring is up and nothing came ashore. I have seen the
lamp room of the Ninth with the brass polished and the wick trimmed and five hundred and
seventy weeks of one sentence in the log, and I have read out a hundred and forty names to a
woman who says them back.*

*The family next door was the Gannets. Ruth, Owen, and the boy, who has no name in the
column and climbed my grandmother's gate. I have the gate and the fence and the hedge and the
month of being smug, and I have no faces to go with any of it, and my grandmother says their
names every morning and means them.*

*My mother was Tess Calloway. She went into the water the night the light went out. Ede has
it written down and no face to go with it and I had the name for twenty minutes and spent it
holding a lane so that a ship would follow me instead of a parish.*

*That is the trade and I made it with my eyes open and I would make it again. It is also the
worst thing the light has ever done to me, and I have paid it a face, a voice, the smell of my
kitchen, the smell of a lamp, three years of school, a rhyme and the neighbours, and none of
it was this.*

*Rook has her ring. He put it on his right thumb where the iron used to be and carries the
iron in his left hand now, and says an empty ring is the correct equipment for a knight of
the watch.*

*Sallow says it is not here for her, it is here for me, and he is a man who has been paid six
salt a week to be right about where I am, so I believe him. Which means the Ash is behind us
for three nights and it is coming to the Kindling, where the Admiral is lighting eight
lanterns with a writ and a company of marines, and it will be the brightest place on this
coast by Thursday.*

*I had better be early.*

*Cost, today: I held a lane for a count of four hundred and I have a page in my pocket with a
name on it that I cannot say.*
