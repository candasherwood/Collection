# The Scarlet Assize

*The hardest thing in [[The Lantern's Edge — Story Bible]]: an order that is not evil, is not
wrong about the danger, and empties a man in a doorway to make a point. Red is what a season looks
like when nobody offered it. Teal is what it looks like when everybody did.*

## Who they are

Two hundred years before the Crown's clause, the Emberwatch struck the **Guiding** off its roll —
the bands came off the wrists, the guides were disbanded, and the order decided that a light you
carry is a light a parish can use and therefore a light the Throne cannot count.

Half the chapter agreed. The other half said the light had gone soft, and left, east into the
machine-country, and did not come back for a generation. When they came back they had red blades
and a doctrine, and they called themselves **the Assize**, because an assize is not a war: it is a
session in which things are decided and somebody goes home poorer.

- **Ordinator Anselm Cray** — fifty, a schoolmaster's face, a list in his coat, and a tiredness
  that is not an act. Nineteen years ago he stood on a sea-wall at **Harrow** and watched the
  thing behind the Ash come up the road in the shape of the people it had eaten. He has been
  tempering coasts ever since.
- **Sister Vell** — forty, a face like a closed shop, the Assize's hand. She put her palm on a
  windlass and a marine sergeant sat down on the stone and stopped being anybody.
- **Toby Larch** — sixteen, novice, carries the only teal blade in the book. Left behind at the
  Kindling on purpose. See below.
- About forty in the company; twelve land at the Kindling in Ch. 25; six carry red blades.

## The doctrine (say it once, let it stand)

> "The thing in the water is a weather. It eats coasts that have forgotten how to be hard. There
> is something behind it that eats coasts that have never been hard at all… We are not the thing
> over the Fall. We are the tempering before it."

Cray is not lying and he is not mad. He is the book's central argument with the sign flipped:

- **Voss** does arithmetic to keep people alive and calls it duty.
- **Cray** does arithmetic to make people hard and calls it mercy.
- Both are certain, both are tired, both are prepared to spend a girl, and both are, in the narrow
  sense, correct about the danger.
- The difference the book is arguing for: **Voss keeps a coast that is soft and alive; Cray
  produces coasts that survive and cannot be kind.** Cray says it himself: *I have never once seen
  one come out of it able to be kind. If you can be hard and keep the kind light, you will have
  done the one thing I could not.*

He is not the final antagonist. He is the rehearsal: a hard man who tells you, accurately, that
something worse is on its way, and demonstrates on your friends what "worse" does. Do not redeem
him; let him be right about the weather and monstrous about the method. On the last night he comes
back with the whole Assize, and he is the second-hardest thing in the book, which is the point.

## Red blades

An Ember is a fist of the mother quenched in a keeper's season ([[The Light of Will]]). The colour
is the **kind of season** it was quenched in.

| | Made from | Costs | Behaviour |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Ember-gold** (the order's) | the bearer's own season, given | nothing after the quenching but the memory he chose to lose | lights when he means it; flickers; smells of hot iron; cannot be drawn by a man lying to himself |
| **Red** (the Assize's) | a season **taken** — a bargain the giver did not understand, or did not survive | **the room**: it draws from whoever is nearest, bearer included | six come up at once, flat, steady, no flicker; the air goes cold; a man beside one loses first the hour, then the year, then his name |
| **Teal** (the Given) | many seasons **given knowingly**, at once — a village's worth | nothing. That is the difficulty: it cannot be hurried | starts grey; lights dim; **grows over years** if the bearer keeps giving; rare |

Three rules, and the book should never break them:

1. **A red blade takes its price from the room, not from the hand.** That is why the Assize can
   fight six against forty and win, and why they are careful where they stand. It is also why
   their own people are strange: they have all stood next to one too long.
2. **A red blade is a door nailed shut.** Light comes out and nothing goes in, so the Ash cannot
   use it and does not follow it. At the Kindling the Ash's window goes dark, comes back a mile
   south, and is gone — the first time in the book it has ever run from anything. This is the most
   frightening sentence in the notebook and it should stay that way.
3. **Given light can put out taken light.** Cray says it out loud in Ch. 25 — he has seen it once,
   and the man who did it was never the same. That is Rook's door in Ch. 26 and it must cost him
   something the reader has watched him keep.

   The mechanics, fixed in Ch. 26 by the order's own annals: **it does not fight, it opens.** The
   red blade is a season held shut; a given blade does not cut it, it makes it let go. Everything
   the red was holding passes *through* the man holding the given light — every hour, in no order,
   at once — and afterwards the given blade goes dark and never lights again. Precedent: **Ser
   Aldous Mere, at Ostrey, in the year of the ninth chapter.** Fever, three days; could not say the
   vows; released; nineteen years as a lay brother and would not be called ser. His marginal note is
   the only instruction the book offers: *if a man does this he should have somebody with him who
   will not ask him questions after. I did not. I have written this for the next one.*

## Teal blades and Toby Larch

A teal blade is a **gift with a great many givers**: seventeen people of a village called Ostrey
stood at a trough and gave a season each, knowingly, and the thread went grey-teal and stayed grey
for four years because it was in the hands of a boy who had not understood anything yet.

- It lights for **understanding**, not for need, and never for anger.
- It does not take from the room. It does not take from the bearer. It is, in the Assize's own
  phrase, *a waste and a superstition* — which is exactly why Cray made one for his novice, and
  exactly why he cannot say that sentence without looking away.
- Its promise, stated once and never explained: **it can become a great light.** Not a bigger
  blade. A light. This is the book's hope in object form, and it is sixteen years old and
  frightened and sitting on a bollard.
- In Ch. 25 it lights when Mira comes near, because somebody in the last four days told the truth
  in front of him, and it is the first thing all day that got brighter instead of going out.
- Cray left him at the Kindling deliberately: *stay and learn what a kept coast is, and when
  you're done, come and tell me.* It is the cruellest kind thing he does.

## Rules of use

- **Never let them enjoy it.** Cray is courteous and tired; Vell is economical; nobody gloats.
- **Show the bill on civilians first.** A barrow-put-down, a guide's cracked band, four drowned men
  who cannot find the deep road any more. The blades are terrifying because of what happens to the
  people *standing next to the fight*.
- **No monologues in a fight.** Cray stops the fight to explain, which is worse than a monologue:
  he does it as a lesson, with his blade already out.
- **The Assize is not the Ash's ally.** They hate it. That is what makes them usable and sad.
- **Do not let the teal blade solve anything.** It is a promise, not a weapon. If it ever cuts
  something, the book is over.
