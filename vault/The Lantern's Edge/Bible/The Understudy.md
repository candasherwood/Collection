# The Understudy

*The loud drowned man of [[The Lantern's Edge — Story Bible]]: a mercenary who
cannot stay dead, narrates his own life, and will do anything for money except
break a contract.*

## Who he is

**Corbin Sallow**, called **the Understudy**. One of the *Mercy Bell*'s original
crew — raised off the bottom with the rest of them, forty years dead and not
finished — who walked off the ship the week after the raising and went into
business for himself.

"Understudy" is his own joke and he tells it the same way every time: *not because
I'm second choice; because I'm always the one still standing when the cast falls
down.*

He is the other drowned man. Marrow went down and stayed; Sallow went down, came up,
and left. They do not hate each other. It is worse than that: they are the only two
people alive who remember the same nothing.

## What he does

Contract recovery. Finds things, finds people, finds out what people did. Works for
the Crown, the crews, the order, and occasionally all three in one week, because he
is scrupulous about the terms and completely indifferent to the side.

**The price list**, which he recites unprompted:

| Job | Price |
| --- | --- |
| Finding a thing | twelve salt |
| Finding a person | twenty |
| Bringing a person back against their preference | thirty, payable in advance |
| Drowning | extra, because it's boring |
| Being hanged | not offered. He has done it once and the paperwork was a scandal |

He carries the list on a card, oiled, in his coat, and updates it in front of you
with a pencil he keeps behind his ear, which is the one thing he and Mira have in
common.

## The voice

He talks. Constantly, cheerfully, in the cadence of a man who has been on stage and
never got over it, because he has: he was a player before he was a sailor, and a
player after, and the *Mercy Bell* was the only ship he ever signed onto that did not
have a curtain.

That is the whole trick of him. He is not aware that he is in a book — he is aware
that he is in a **story**, which every player is, and he addresses the house. The
room. Whoever is listening. He does not know the reader exists; he assumes an
audience does, and he is usually right.

- "You'll want to remember this bit. No — write it down. I'll wait."
- "This is the part where somebody says something true and it costs them. I've seen
  this one. It's the good one."
- "I'm not the villain. I'm the *complication*. There's a difference, and it's
  billable."
- "Nobody dies in the third act unless the playwright's lazy. I've read the play."
- "Considerable effort," he says, when he means the other thing.
- "You're the quiet one. I'm the one who explains the plot. It's a partnership."

## The second contract

He was paid twice in Chapter Nine and said so out loud: once to bring the Bright to
the Kindling, once to see the Crown did not. There is a third clause he did not
mention, because nobody asked, and because it is the least interesting one to him.

**He files a report.** Every port, every week, to a clerk with a box: who is aboard,
who is well, who is marked, what the light is doing. It is not treachery by his own
accounting. It was in the terms, it was paid for, and a man who breaks a contract on
a matter of taste is not a professional.

This is the answer to the question at the end of Chapter Eleven — *somebody on that
ship has been keeping the count for him* — and the crew work it out in Part Three.

It lands in **Chapter Nineteen**, and it lands badly: not as a discovery but as an
answer to a plain question, given in a clear voice, at a desk, while the ship goes
quiet around him. Six salt a week. *Who is aboard. Who is well. Who is marked, and what
they can do, and what the light is doing.* He does not deny a word of it, he does not
apologise, and his defence is true and not good: **nobody asked**. He wrote down Alys
Wren's coal and the toll at the Gull's Teeth and the boy who held a lane with no ring,
and all of it was in a ledger inside nine days. His own summary is the line to keep:
*forty years of that and I have never once changed anything; six salt a week and I have
moved a fleet.*

The cost is Marrow. Not a fight, not a lecture: the other drowned man stands in the
doorway and is asked to say something, and says **no**, and leaves. It is the first
thing in forty years Sallow has not been able to put a price on, and he asks her not to
tell Marrow he said so.

## How the meta works

Hard rule: **he never addresses the reader and never breaks the world.** He addresses
an imagined audience — the house, the crew, the sea, a person who is not there. Mira
can never decide whether it is a joke, a symptom, or a way of staying sane after
forty years under water, and the book never tells her.

What he does do is **talk about the shape of things**: acts, exits, curtain lines,
what a story needs. He is usually right about the plot and always wrong about the
people, which is the joke and the tragedy.

## The pairing

Marrow and Sallow are the book's comedy engine and its quietest grief, in that order.

- Marrow says eleven words a day; Sallow says eleven a minute.
- Marrow does the work; Sallow invoices it.
- Marrow stayed; Sallow left; neither of them will say which was braver.
- Put them in a room and the scene writes itself: one man repairing a rope, one man
  explaining the rope's role in the narrative.

## Rules of use

- **Never let him solve a plot problem.** He re-prices it, or he declines it on
  terms. The heroes do the hard thing.
- **He keeps the contract.** Every time, exactly. That is his one decency and it is
  never explained.
- **His jokes are aimed at the situation, never at the frightened.**
- **He can die; it is not gory and it is not free.** One line, one recovery, one
  complaint about the bill. The reader should find it funny and then stop finding it
  funny.
- **He does not know he is a character.** If a draft has him look at the reader, cut
  it. The stage is the whole of his metaphysics.
- **Less is more.** Two scenes in Part One, three in Part Two, one in Part Five, and
  in the last one he is not funny at all.

## What he is for

The book has a captain who performs to survive and a knight who obeys to survive.
Sallow is the third answer to the same question: **narrate it**. If you say what is
happening out loud, in the right voice, with a price attached, then it is a job and
not a life, and nothing that happens to you is final.

He is wrong, of course. That is why he is in the book.
