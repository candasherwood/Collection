# The Emberwatch

*The knightly order of [[The Lantern's Edge — Story Bible]]: poor knights with the
biggest ledger on the Reach, and a vow that has quietly turned into a job.*

## Who they are

Founded four hundred years ago, in the generation after the Fall, by the nine
keepers who rebuilt the lanterns. Their formal name is long and they do not use it
in public: **the Poor Knights of the Ninth Light**. The coast says *the watch*. The
Crown says *the order*, in the tone of a landlord.

- **Warden** — the head, at the Kindling. Elected by the chapter, confirmed by the
  Throne, which is where the trouble starts.
- **Marshal** — the field commander. Currently a patient woman named Hale, who has
  been transferred west three times for asking about the Eighth.
- **Seneschal** — money, stores, and the ledgers. Currently Brother Ostrand, who is
  seventy, and who has not slept properly since the Long Dark.
- **Commander** — one per commandery: a lantern, its keep, its lands, its debts.
- **Knight-brothers** — Rook. Sworn, armoured, mobile, sent where the Warden wants.
- **Sergeants** — fighting men who are not knights and are not asked about the vows.
- **Lay brothers** — wick-trimmers, smiths, copyists, cooks. Not fighters. The order
  could not light a candle without them and never says so.
- **Novices** — four years. Then the Long Night.

Nine commanderies, one per lantern, plus the fortress-forge at the Kindling. Every
one of them is bigger than it needs to be, because they were built when the lights
were lit and nobody has had the nerve to knock a wing down.

## The Guiding (the vow that was struck out)

Before there were nine towers there were **bands** — a hoop of lantern glass on a guide's
left wrist that shows the road you are on and lets anybody holding your hand see it too. The
order's first job was walking people through the dark water, and its first oath was to do it.

The Crown's clause put a stop to it two hundred years ago: *I will guide no ship that the
Throne has not counted.* The bands came off the roll, the guides were disbanded, and the
chapter houses have taught the reform as a virtue ever since. Eleven guides still work out of
a cellar under the Kindling, with the Warden's silent permission, because the coast still gets
dark at night and somebody has to walk the children to the Sill. Full detail, including the
caramel light: [[The Bands]].

Rook does not know any of this until Chapter Twenty-Two, and it breaks something in him that
the Crown never could: the order's oldest purpose was the one it is now most ashamed of.

## The three vows

1. **Poverty.** A knight owns nothing. Kit is issued, the horse is the order's, the
   blade belongs to the commandery it was forged in. No coin is carried on the
   person — that is not a metaphor, it is searched for at the gate.
2. **Obedience.** To the Warden, and through the Warden to the Salt Throne. This
   clause was added by a Crown notary two hundred years ago and nobody reads it
   aloud any more.
3. **Attendance.** The strange one, and the only one the order invented. A knight
   must be present at a lantern at least one night in nine, and must trim a wick
   with his own hands. It is why they are posted to dead keeps with their swords
   sharpened; it is why every knight in the order is, secretly and in a way they
   would deny in chapter, a little bit of a keeper.

## The dress

Ash-white surcoat over grey, because grey alone looks like weather. At the right
shoulder, a cross of four flames in ember-gold — four, not one, for the four
corners of the Reach. Black cord for a novice, ember-gold for a commander, a plain
iron thumb-ring for every knight-brother, no stone in it, because glass is for
keepers and iron is for people who have to hit things.

The surcoat is the only item a knight is allowed to be proud of, so they are all
extremely careful about how they fold it.

## The Long Night

The initiation. One night alone in an unlit lantern room with a single candle and
the rule that you may not light it. No food, no blanket, no company, and the door
locked from the outside by a lay brother who has done it himself and will not talk
about it.

At dawn, three questions:

> "Will you keep what is not yours?"  
>
> "Will you go where you are sent?"  
>
> "Will you be seen?"  

The answer to each is **I will**. That is why Rook says *I will* instead of *yes*,
and why the words come out at the worst possible moments: he has spent them once,
at fourteen, at dawn, and they are the only sentence he owns.

Nobody has asked the third question aloud since the Long Dark. There is no formal
reason. The novices simply get two questions now, and the older brothers have not
discussed it.

## The ledger

Poverty, and a bank.

A knight travels the coast with a letter and no money, and a stranger in a strange
port honours it, because the order's word is the safest currency on the Reach. The
**salt-letters** began as travel permits and became credit; the Crown borrows
against them; the Admiral's fleet is paid in paper signed by a Warden who owns
nothing. There are two ledgers at the Kindling: the one the Seneschal keeps, and
the one that is true.

The true one has a line in it, written every quarter for eleven years, in Brother
Ostrand's fine small hand:

> *Attendance at the dark keeps — eight — per quarter — 240 salt.*

That is the stipend. The Crown pays this fortress to trim wicks that are not lit and
sign the log and be **seen to be there**, because a commandery cannot be left empty and a
knight must be fed. It is not called a bribe; it is called attendance, and it pays for a
third of the Kindling and stands behind every salt-letter on the Reach. So the safest
paper on the coast is a promise secured by the rent on the dark, and a woman at Carrow who
has never held a salt-letter has been paying the whole of it, a season at a time.

Ostrand's defence, and it is the order's: *the first year the Crown paid nothing and we
sold plate. In the second year a clerk came with a schedule and the Warden signed it and we
got to eat.* Rook, hearing it, has only one line — *I have eaten at that table for twelve
years* — and no answer to it. See [[The Light of Will]] for the forge the stipend is slowly
buying, and the writ that names it.

This is the order's real scandal, and it is not the one the Crown is investigating.

## The accusation

The Throne's auditors and the new catechism have begun saying it in public: the
order worships the Ember. That they have made a machine into a god, a dark tower
into an altar, and a wick into a sacrament.

The accusation is useful. A heresy justifies a **visitation** — auditors in the
commanderies, novices refused, salt-letters called in, the Marshal posted west —
and a visitation that finds the order corrupt can be followed, quite legally, by a
reform that takes nine commanderies, nine keeps, and one very large ledger into the
Crown's hands.

The order's actual secret is quieter and worse: **they know.** Four hundred years
of attendance, and the senior brothers have known for at least two generations that
the Embers are machinery and the light is will and memory and not a miracle. They
have let the coast believe otherwise, because a miracle keeps people paying and a
machine asks questions.

Rook's faith does not break on the Crown. It breaks on that.

## The one leaf (Ch. 26)

Four hundred years of annals and one entry under *taken light, put out by given*: **Ser Aldous Mere**,
of this house, at Ostrey on the north road, in the year of the ninth chapter. His ember-blade went
dark and never lit again. He was three days in a fever, came out of it unable to say the vows, was
released from them, and lived nineteen years as a lay brother — much loved, and he would not be
called ser.

In the margin, in his own hand:

> *It does not fight. It opens. Everything the red thing was holding comes through the man who
> holds the light, and it does not stop coming, and there is no way to take it in order… If a man
> does this he should have somebody with him who will not ask him questions after. I did not. I
> have written this for the next one.*

The Warden has read it. Rook read it the evening after the Assize came, and was sick in the yard,
and then went and did it anyway in front of witnesses and a keeper with a band, which is the whole
of what the order is for and the first time in twelve years that anybody has used it that way.

## The reform clock

The visitation is already running in Part One; it is why Rook is sent to do small
ugly jobs alone. It arrives in Part Four, at the Kindling, with a Warden who has
done the arithmetic and a Throne that has brought the paperwork. Everything Mira
needs — a forge, a furnace, nine cold lanterns and a reason to be seen — is inside
an institution that is being taken apart by its own signature.

On the Thursday (Ch. 24) the paperwork is actually served: the admiral lands with the writ and
three returned Embers, and the **forge is sealed for nine days** — the Crown's seal beside the
Warden's, a Crown smith in the antechamber, no cutting, no quenching, no key. The order does not
resist, because obedience is the second vow and nobody has read the clause aloud since a notary
added it. Rook says the only thing anybody says: *attendance is the third vow. Not obedience.*

## Rook, in all this

- A poor knight down to the last coin: one bag, one book, one blade, and no money,
  and he does not notice that this is unusual.
- Keeps the rule of no private flame. On the *Mercy Bell* he sits in the dark
  because of it, and the dark, which is patient, begins to talk to him there.
- Obedient for real; conscientious late. The gap between the two is the whole man.
- His arc is the order's arc in one body: **attendance without obedience.**

## Rules of use

- Never let the order be simply wicked. The vows are good. The compromise is a
  Crown notary's clause and eleven years of silence.
- Never explain the banking. Show a knight paying for a meal with a signed paper,
  and a fisherman accepting it.
- The armoury should feel like a museum with better locks: old blades, newer
  paperwork, dust on the crusade nobody went on.
- Structural Templar borrowing only — poverty beside banking, attendance beside
  fortress-commanderies, a crown that needs a heresy before it can take the assets.
  No red cross, no Holy Land, no trial by combat, and nobody in the book has ever
  heard the word *Templar*.
