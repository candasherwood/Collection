# Tone and Influences

*How the different flavours asked for in [[The Lantern's Edge — Story Bible]] are
actually used, and what to leave out so the book stays its own thing.*

## The mix, in order of priority

1. **The light that answers to will** — the core engine, not a costume. Every
   scene about the light is really about conviction, and every use of it costs
   something irreplaceable.
2. **Nautical and piratical** — the book's texture. Salt, rope, weather, a crew
   underfoot, a captain with a scheme and a debt.
3. **Knightly and medieval** — the book's politics and its conscience. Vows,
   orders, a crown, a blade that only lights when you mean it.
4. **Science fiction, unspoken** — the world's floorplan. A broken ring, a dead
   sun, machines older than the sea. Revealed by behaviour, never by explanation.
5. **Romance** — the book's heartbeat. Quiet, competent, mutual, slow. Two people
   who are better at danger than at being cared for, and who say the true thing
   plainly, once, when the dark is listening. Full register and dialogue bank in
   [[The Two of Them]].

## Borrowing an idea, not an icon

- **The will-light is an idea, not a costume.** No green glow, no uniform, no
  oath recited in unison, no alien guardians with blue faces. Light here is
  green-black glass, cold to look at and heavy to hold, and it takes memories.
  If a scene would work with a magic sword, it is the wrong scene for a Holding.
- **The one sanctioned exception: the bands (Ch. 22).** The brief asked for green
  shimmering wristbands that guide people through the night, a corps with hidden
  powers, and a rare orange light. That becomes this: matte green-black glass at the
  left wrist, a **shimmer and not a glow**, worn by labourers with wet boots, banned
  by the Crown for two hundred years and still working out of a cellar. A band holds
  nothing — it shows a road, it can be shared, and it costs hours out of the middle of
  a life. The orange one is the **caramel light**: an amber band that got tired of
  charging its wearer and started charging the people it guided. There is one, it is
  in a case, and nobody wears it. No constructs, no flight, no shields, no corps of
  heroes in matching coats: the power is *sharing a way*, and the politics is who is
  allowed to walk where. Detail: [[The Bands]].
- **No prophecy, no chosen one, no "your destiny".** Mira is the keeper because
  her grandmother's ring fits her, and for no larger reason. The book is about
  ordinary nerve, not special blood.
- **Knights are bureaucracy before they are romance.** The Emberwatch has
  paperwork, rations, a fortress-forge and a chain of command that has been
  quietly compromised. Rook's tragedy is that he is *good at his job*.
- **Pirates are professionals, not mascots.** Isolde's crew has skills, wages,
  grief and a schedule. No talking parrots. One goat, but nobody admits to it.
- **Romance does not stop the plot.** It happens in two-hour watches, in shared
  food, in somebody remembering a name. The plot interrupts the romance, never
  the other way round.
- **Two speeches, total.** The vow appears in fragments and is only ever spoken
  whole at a threshold: the first time badly, at a cold wick, by a girl alone;
  the last time in front of a fleet. The scaffold speech is retold, never
  reprinted.
- **The first opponent is a filing cabinet with a face.** No duel with the villain in
  Part One. The first enemy is a courteous official who offers what you want and
  writes down what you say, introduced across four chapters, one odd detail at a
  time, and never in a scene where he raises his voice. See [[The Assessor]].
- **The girl who turns out to be dangerous.** Borrow the *shape* of the fire-and-steel
  heroine — the mask of politeness, the training by an old woman, the court that
  wants her in a chair, the litany said when it is nearly a lie — and refuse the
  parts that would break this book: no lost royal blood, no prophecy, no love
  triangle, no transformation scene. Her power is the ladder, and the ladder is
  paid for out of her own memory. See [[The Coat and the Fire]] and [[The Ladder]].
- **The narrator in the room.** Corbin Sallow talks to an audience that is not
  there, which is the only lawful version of that joke in a close-third book: no
  winks at the reader, no awareness of being written, no breaking of the world. He
  believes in plays, not in pages. The comedy is aimed at the situation and never at
  the frightened, and it stops working in Part Five, which is the point. See
  [[The Understudy]].
- **The marked are people, not a team.** The Fall-touched borrow the shape of a
  persecuted minority with strange gifts — a seer who cannot sleep, a man who will
  not stay drowned, a woman whose will caught fire — and then the book refuses the
  rest: no costumes, no code-names, no school with a motto, no cosmic scale. The
  antagonist is the Glass Roll and the paperwork behind it. See [[The Marked]].
- **The knightly order is borrowed structurally, not ornamentally.** Poverty set
  beside a bank, attendance set beside fortress-commanderies, and a crown that
  needs a heresy before it can take the assets. No red cross, no crusade, no trial
  by combat, no Latin, and nobody in the Reach has ever heard the word. See
  [[The Emberwatch]].

## The oath and the last word

Two more borrowed shapes, both handled the same way: keep the *function*, replace
the furniture.

- **A vow that lights the dark.** The Green Lantern oath works because it is a
  promise recited at the moment of use — the words and the power are one act. The
  Reach gets that, and nothing else: no corps, no rings that fly, no green. Here
  the vow is the *reason* a lantern needs, spoken before the wick, and the lamp
  answers what you meant rather than what you said. The Crown has its own version
  and it works, which is the horror: obedience recited sincerely lights a lamp.
- **A dying man's dare.** The captain on the scaffold is a shape, not a quote: a
  man with nothing left to lose turns his execution into an invitation, and the
  crowd leaves wanting something. Write it in the Reach's idiom — salt-scrip
  fines, an admiral in the front row, a statute against repeating it — and it
  stops being a reference and becomes local weather.
- **Inherited will.** The thing a dying person says in public does not die with
  them; it becomes a rumour, then a proverb, then a plan somebody else executes
  badly. This is a theme, not a mechanic, and it should never be explained by a
  character.
- **The treasure is a door.** Whatever was "left out there" is not gold and not
  a weapon. It is the way over the Fall, which is to say it is the next book's
  first page and this book's last.

## The rogue and the elegy

Two registers, one book, and they must never bleed into each other. Line bank:
[[Lines and Flair]].

- **The rogue** — the pirate-captain who is the most competent person aboard and
  the most frightened, and keeps the two facts apart with jokes. Steal the
  *mechanics*: misdirection instead of lying, the title as armour, the bargain as
  reflex, humour aimed at power and never at the frightened. Never the costume:
  no slurring, no catchphrase, no rum.
- **The elegy** — high fantasy's weight without its archaism. The world is old,
  the people are small, the sea does not care and the light does. It lives in
  chapter openings and part-endings, on short flat sentences, and it is the only
  place the narration is allowed to be beautiful.
- **The collision is the point.** Isolde jokes at the rail while the sea is full
  of a drowned country. Rook says four words and means all of them. Mira stands
  between them counting something, because counting is what she does with
  feelings.

## What to avoid

- Modern idiom, therapist-speak, "energy" as a feeling, anything that sounds like
  a pitch deck.
- Explaining the ringworld. No lectures, no diagram in the dialogue. Let the sky
  be wrong and let people be used to it.
- Pastiche. No borrowed catchphrases, no gravelly pirate grammar, no "one does not
  simply" constructions. If a line only works because the reader recognises where
  it came from, cut it.
- Grimdark for its own sake. There is real horror here (the Hollowed, the
  chessboard, Wren's Fold) but it is quiet, domestic and tidy, which is worse.
- Clever endings. The last chapter should be plain.

## Voice rules

- **Close third, past tense, Mira.** We know what she notices, counts and
  refuses to think about. No omniscience.
- **Interludes** are Ede's notebook, in her voice, dated, never more than a page.
- **Sentences shorten as the pressure rises.** At the worst moment, four words is
  a paragraph.
- **Concrete nouns beat adjectives.** Paraffin. Brass. Salt-scrip. A box of
  matches with the label turned to the door.
- **Numbers are a motif.** Eleven years, one hundred and twelve steps, page
  nineteen, nine lanterns, two hours of watch. Mira counts because counting is
  how she keeps her hands busy while she is afraid.
- **Humour under pressure, never at the expense of the frightened.**

## Atmosphere references, not plot references

Grey coastal light; a lighthouse at the wrong hour; the smell of a boat that has
been under water; a chandelier made of something that used to save lives; a bell
that rings when the air is still; a stranger who is polite because he has been
told to be.
