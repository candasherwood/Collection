# The Marked

*What four hundred years on a broken ring has done to a few people, in
[[The Lantern's Edge — Story Bible]]. Small, ugly, expensive gifts — and a crown
with a list.*

## The idea

The Fall did not only break the world. It left things in the water, and some of it
gets into people: the light does something to a body that spends too long looking
at it, or drowning in it, or holding it. Nobody agrees why. There are three
theories on the Reach and they are all wrong, and one of them is the order's.

The coast calls these people **the marked**, in the flat way it calls weather bad.
The Crown calls them *the assessed*. The order keeps the list and calls it a mercy.

## The four kinds

**The glass-eyed.** One in a few hundred. They see what a lit lantern reveals —
the un-ships, the drowned road, the Ash's edges — without a ring and without a
lamp. It costs them sleep first and nerve second. Teo is one, and has spent his
life being told he has a good eye for weather.

**The drowned.** People the sea gave back. They do not drown; they do not scar
properly; they remember nothing before the water. Isolde spent eleven minutes dead
and came back with a debt and a memory she will not describe. Marrow has been on
the bottom for forty years and is still, technically, on the crew.

**The Bright.** A keeper's will that caught in the glass and stayed lit without a
wick. Rare enough that the order has recorded nine in four hundred years. A Bright
does not need a lantern — the light is *in* them, and it burns what they remember
as fuel, and it does not stop when they are tired. The oldest recorded Bright held
a horizon for six days and died with her eyes open, and the sea was lit for a year
afterwards.

**The hollow-touched.** People who were near the Ash and came back with a piece
missing but still able to act. They can walk among the Hollowed and be mistaken for
them. The Crown fears these more than the Bright, and says so in writing.

## The Glass Roll

A book of names in three hands, kept at the Kindling: every marked person on the
coast, their parish, their keeper, and the date of their **assessment**. The
assessment is a hearing at Saltmere — part medical, part interrogation, part
inventory. It is not a death sentence. It is a file, and a file is how a crown finds
a person when it needs one.

The writ in Chapter Five is a Roll writ: *conveyed to Saltmere for the assessment of
her household*. Mira was never told the words meant a list.

The order has administered the Roll for two hundred years and calls it charity,
because the alternative is the Crown's assessors doing it alone. Rook has carried a
copy of it since he was nineteen and has never been able to explain the duty
out loud, which is why he is so good at obedience.

## The mentor

**Sister Ambry**, of the almshouse at the Sill: eighty, blind, a keeper for forty
years before she gave the ring up. She keeps a house of marked children, teaches
them to count and to lie, and can borrow sight through lantern glass — she looks at
a lit window and sees what it last saw, including the people who stood in front of
it. It is the closest thing to company the marked have on the Reach.

She is kind, and she keeps her own roll, and she has decided more than once that
somebody's memory would be better off lost. She is not the villain. She is what a
good person becomes when they have been the only shelter for thirty years.

## The Bright at Wren's Fold

**Alys Wren**, 34, last keeper of the Eighth. Her family are Hollowed and still at
their posts; she is not. Eleven years ago she did what Ede Calloway did — held the
Ash out alone — and where Ede had a dark ring and arithmetic, Alys had more will
than any keeper in a generation, and it caught.

She has been holding a light without a wick ever since, in a lamp room with a dead
lens, and she is burning to do it. She counts her family out loud to keep them,
because the light takes names first. She writes them down every morning.

Alys is the book's one true power, and the book must treat her the way it treats
everybody else: as a person paying. She is not a weapon and not a chosen one. She
is a woman who has been on fire for eleven years and would like somebody to take
the light from her before she forgets why she wanted it.

## Marrow

Forty years on the bottom of the Reach with a dead crew, raised with the *Mercy
Bell*, and no memory of any of it. He does not drown. He goes into the water when the
water needs going into, and comes back with rope, or a body, or something worse,
and does not talk about it. Built out of rope and old weather; hands like hooks;
quiet because he prefers it that way.

His loyalty is the least complicated thing in the book, and the most expensive.

**The other one.** Corbin Sallow came up in the same raising, left in the same week,
and has been charging for his time ever since — the loud drowned man to Marrow's
quiet one. Full note: [[The Understudy]].

## Rules of use

- **No costumes, no code-names, no team.** Nobody has a word for what they are
  that they would put on a banner.
- **Powers are narrow, physical, and cost something the person needs.** Sight for
  sleep. Will for memory. Not drowning for a past.
- **The antagonist is an institution with paperwork**, not a marked person with
  bad intentions. The Glass Roll is scarier than any Bright.
- **Never explain the origin.** Three theories, all wrong, and the order's is the
  wrongest.
- **The marked are not a metaphor for a real-world group.** Keep it about the
  light: what it makes of people who look at it too long.

## What not to import

No mind control, no cosmic fire, no berserker rampages, no school with a motto, no
aging cure that resets the cast every decade. The usable version of the idea is
smaller and worse: a coast that keeps a list, a girl who has been holding a door
for eleven years, and a man who cannot drown and cannot remember why that is a
punishment.
