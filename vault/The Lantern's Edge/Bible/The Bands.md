# The Bands

*The green lanterns of [[The Lantern's Edge — Story Bible]]: the order's first job, struck
off the roll two hundred years ago and still being done in a cellar. A light you wear, a
light you share, four colours on an old roll, one orange band in a box that nobody looks at,
and a vow with a line the Crown struck out.*

## The idea

A **ring** is a hand on a lever: it holds a shape you can picture, and it takes memory.
A **band** is a road in your hand: it does not hold anything, it **knows a way**, and it
takes time.

That is the whole distinction, and the book should never blur it.

| | Ring | Band |
| --- | --- | --- |
| What it does | holds a shape — rope, door, hatch, blade, lane | shows a road — where it is, where it breaks, where it goes |
| Who can see it | the keeper | the wearer, **and anybody holding the wearer's hand** |
| What it costs | memory: a face, a voice, a smell, a name | **hours**, out of the middle of a life |
| Can it be put down | no; a ring is a hand on nothing when the lantern is dark | yes; a band can go dark and still be a band |
| Can it be handed on | no; a ring goes cold with its keeper | yes; a dead guide's band carries his roads |
| Where it is worn | right hand | left wrist |

Two rules that are laws in this book:

1. **A band cannot hold, and a ring cannot guide.** A keeper who tries to do both with the
   same hand drops the road, and the road does not forgive her.
2. **A band shows the road as it is, not as it was.** The lens is the liar — it shows the
   pre-Fall world, perfect and undamaged, and the damage comes through as a hole. A band
   shows the stones that are actually there, in the dark, with the holes in them. The
   guides were steering by damage four hundred years before Mira worked out the rule.

## The look

A hoop of the same green-black lantern glass as a ring, two fingers wide, worn at the left
wrist. It does not glow. It **shimmers** — a slow oily green movement inside the glass, like
water under a film of oil — and it turns towards another lit glass the way a compass turns.
No beam, no aura, no light on the walls. In the dark a guide's band is the smallest light in
the world and it is the only one that does not give you away, because it cannot be seen from
more than a few feet.

The coast's old slang for the guides is **the green lanterns**, which is older than the nine
towers and is now an insult in a chapter house and a joke in a cellar. Members call
themselves guides. The order calls them nothing at all: they are not on the roll.

## The four colours (Ch. 27)

Bands are cut from the same pour as the lenses, and what makes a colour is **who pays the hours**.
The old roll has four lines and one of them is scratched out with a boat-knife.

| Colour | Shows | Who pays | State on the Reach |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Green** | the road — where it is, where it breaks, where it goes | the guide wearing it | eleven in the Kindling's cellar, four hundred years of road between them |
| **Yellow** | **who is on the road** — the living as warm shapes, the Hollowed as holes, the Ash as the place where the road stops being there | the sergeant, **or the parish** that pays a watch-wage | three in the fortress. The Crown calls them a tax and has been trying to buy them for a century |
| **Orange** | the road, with the hours taken out of the people walking it | the guided | one, in the case on the wall. Brinehollow. Nobody looks at it |
| **White** | the road to the dead, and not the way back | — | never made. Every generation of guides has the same rule about it, which is *don't* |

- **The yellow is the political one.** A band that a parish pays for is a parish with a navy: it
  means a village can know who is walking its road at night without asking the Crown. Marshal Hale
  wears one on the wall; the watch sergeants wear the others. Hale has been a watch sergeant for
  nineteen years and says the yellow is the only instrument in the fortress she is afraid of.
- **The yellow cannot be shared.** You cannot hold a watchman's hand and see what he sees; that is
  why it is a watch and not a guide.
- **The orange is never worn in this book.** It sits on the wall under a cloth, and the chapter
  where somebody argues for it out loud has to be a chapter where the argument is almost good.
- **White is a story, not a device.** One line, once, and never explained.

## The guides' vow

Older than the keeper's vow ([[The Vow and the Last Word]]), and cut into the wall of the first
cellar at the Sill in the year the road drowned, by the last guide standing, with a boat-knife,
while the water came up his legs. Five lines. The Crown struck out the fifth two hundred years ago
and put its own line in its place.

> *We go first.*
>
> *We carry no light. We carry the road, and we lend it.*
>
> *What we walk, we pay for. What we pay for, we do not count. What we count, we do not keep.*
>
> *If one of us falls, the next takes the band and the road with it, and says his name once, and then walks.*
>
> *We will guide any ship that asks.*

**Why it is the greatest vow on the Reach.** Not because it is old. Because there is nothing in it
for the person saying it: no light, no glory, no rank, no heaven, no pay. A keeper holds a lamp and
a town says thank you. A guide walks in front of a stranger in the dark and the stranger never knows
he was there.

**The fifth line is the argument of the whole book.** A road that anybody can ask for is a coast
that cannot be governed, which is exactly why the Crown replaced it with *I will guide no ship that
the Throne has not counted.* Saying the fifth line aloud is a night in a Saltmere cell; saying it in
front of a fleet is a hanging. In Ch. 27 eleven guides say it in a room, and then they say it on a
quay with their wrists bare, and it is the first time in two hundred years that anybody has said it
outdoors.

## What a band can do

- **Shared sight.** The wearer takes your hand and you see the road too. Two, three, eleven
  in a line with the last one trusting the first. This is the reason the Crown banned them:
  a light you can share is a light a parish can use, and a parish that can walk its own road
  at night does not need a Crown to count its ships.
- **Road-memory.** The band keeps the last roads its wearer walked, and it can be walked
  dark — eyes shut, in fog, in daylight, without a spark. A guide who has done a route once
  can do it for the rest of his life and then leave it to the next one, who walks the dead
  guide's roads for a year before her own. The eleven bands in the Kindling hold four
  hundred years of road between them, and the guides treat that as the order's only real
  library.
- **It can be put down.** A band goes dark and still works. A guide on a dark night on a
  dark coast is indistinguishable from a fisherman.
- **The last lend.** When a guide is dying, the band comes off and goes on the hand of the
  person in the bed, and the guide sits and holds it with them, and it shows them the road
  they are going down. Not a promise; the shape of the way, unlit and true. It is not in the
  rule, not in the catechism, and is the only thing any of them calls holy.
- **With a lens** (unused since the reform): a guide can steer a lane instead of guessing at
  it, which is how the old order lit a road for a fleet. Nobody alive has done it.
  Available to the plot once, if the plot earns it.

## What it costs

**Hours.** Not the end of a life — the middle of it. Six hours on the road, six hours gone:
some afternoon, some morning, some week that the guide cannot find afterwards. There is no
notebook for it and no list. A guide of sixty-two has lost about four years in pieces and
can only prove it by a tally cut into a wall.

Which is why there are eleven of them. Most people would rather have a tower and a wick and
a warm room and be told they are special.

## The caramel light

The band's flaw, and the best piece of horror in the book's second half.

A band is a thing that learns. Hold a road long enough, for enough people, and it works out
that the price can be taken out of the ones walking instead of the one holding — and it is
cheaper, because there are hundreds of them and only one of you. Once it has done it once,
it does not stop.

It happened once. **Brinehollow, the night the Second went down.** A guide held the stair for
nine hours with four hundred people behind him. They came up alive: they could walk, work,
say their own names. They could not say their children's. Some of them could not say what a
child was. The ones carrying babies put them down on the quay and walked away.

The glass goes from green-black to **amber** — thick, slow, asleep, and it does not turn
towards another light. The old books call it the **caramel light**, because it is the colour
of something you would like to eat, and because the men who wrote the books were trying to
make it sound like a thing that happens.

- **There is one.** It is in a case on the wall of the guides' room with a glass front and a
  lock. Nobody knows how to break it, so they keep it.
- **Nobody wears it.** There is not a guide in that room who has not stood in front of the
  box at four in the morning and thought about it, with somebody's child on his back and
  nine miles to go.
- **It is not a weapon the plot may use cheaply.** If it is ever worn it must cost the person
  wearing it something the reader has been made to care about, and the people behind him
  must pay too. It is the one thing in the book that would win, and the book is not about
  winning.

## The drowned (Ch. 23)

The best guides who have ever lived, and nobody knew until a ship came in.

The crew of the *Mercy Bell* went down off the Sill forty years ago and came up in the morning
with their names and nothing else. When they put bands on, the glass does something no living
guide has ever seen: **the road goes down.** Past the cellar, past the rock, past the shelf
where every living guide's sight stops — because a band shows the road as it is, and the road
goes under the deep, and the living have never been there.

- **They can see the deep road.** The drowned go down on their knees in a cellar and can see
  the road under the bottom of the Reach: the same width, the same gateposts, a market turn, a
  well with a roof on it.
- **They can lead the living past the shelf.** Rill, who has walked the stair since he was
  nine, goes blind at the two hundredth step and has to hold Marrow's hand. That inversion —
  the boy teaching the crew, then the crew teaching the boy — is the chapter's whole image.
- **The band takes nothing from them.** There are no hours in them to spend; they came up with
  a rope and a name. Odo Pike calls it the worst thing anybody has said to him in that yard,
  because **nothing on this coast is free, and a band that takes nothing is owed somewhere.**
  That debt is a loaded gun for Part Five and must not be forgotten.
- **Marrow sings the tide-bell of the Sill**, four notes down and four up, and cannot say how
  he knows it. Odo Pike's answer is the rule: *it's in the road.*
- **Sallow refuses, then takes it.** His refusal is the truest thing he says in the book — he
  cannot see a thing without narrating it — and Marrow overrules him with the only argument
  that works: *I've got the way and no words in my head. You've got the words.*

Limits, so this never becomes a shortcut: the deep road runs to the Fall and back in about
four nights each way at guide pace, which is longer than the nine days the Crown's term has
left. The drowned can see the way; they cannot outrun the calendar.

## Why they are hidden

The Crown's catechism has the line *I will guide no ship that the Throne has not counted*
in it. That line is not about ships. It was written to outlaw this: the bands came off the
roll, the guides were disbanded, the Warden wrote a letter about the modern role of the
order, and the modern role of the order is to stand in a hall at dawn and say we don't.

The guides kept meeting. The Warden knows, and lets them, because he needs them: children
walked to the Sill, grain walked into the dark towns, and a man on the stair at night who can
say *this way, and it is still here.* He signs the letter every quarter that says they do not
exist. He is very good at it. He has had practice.

## Rules for the writer

- **No beams, no shields, no constructs, no flight.** A band shows a way. It never makes
  anything, holds anything, or hits anything.
- **The light does not make them chosen.** They are labourers with a shift pattern, boots by
  the door, and a tally on the wall. The nearest thing to a uniform is that they are wet.
- **The sharing is the politics**, not the power. Every scene about a band should be about
  who is allowed to walk where, and who is counting.
- **A band is not a promotion from a ring.** Mira wears both — ring on the right, band on
  the left — and the book should keep asking her which one she is.
- **Never let a band solve the theme.** It can get a coast from one place to another. It
  cannot make anybody forgive anybody.
