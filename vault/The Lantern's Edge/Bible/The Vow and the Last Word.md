# The Vow and the Last Word

*Two pieces of language that do mechanical work in
[[The Lantern's Edge — Story Bible]]. Neither is decoration. One lights a lamp;
the other starts a hunt that outlives the man who started it.*

## Part one — The Keeper's Vow

### The long form

Written on the inside cover of Ede Calloway's notebook, on a page pasted over a
page, in a hand that is not tidy:

> *In the lit hour and the last hour,*  
> *on water that keeps no road,*  
> *I hold this light as keepers hold —*  
> *not for the crown, not for the coin,*  
> *not because the dark is cruel,*  
> *but because I am here, and I can see,*  
> *and what I can see, I answer for.*  

Under it, smaller, in the same hand:

> *Say it before the wick, not after. The lamp can tell the difference.*  

### The short form

What keepers actually say at a kindling, with one hand flat on the glass chimney:

> **Light what you can. Count what you lose. Answer for what you see.**  

Ede wrote it on page one. It is the whole religion of the order and it fits on a
matchbox.

### What a vow does

[[The Light of Will]] says a lantern needs a keeper, a ring, a flame and **a
reason**. The vow is the reason, said out loud. That is the only magic in it:

- **The lamp hears meaning, not syllables.** A keeper can recite the Crown's
  version and mean the old one and the wick will take, because the lamp is not
  listening to the words. This is why the oath cannot be faked, and why it works
  for men like Rook, who mean every word of the wrong oath.
- **A lie at the wick lights nothing.** Not a flicker. Cold brass and a wet
  match. Every keeper learns this in their first week and nobody talks about it
  afterwards, the way nobody talks about the first time they were afraid.
- **A broken vow puts the light out.** Do the thing you swore against and the
  ring goes cold until you say a true thing in front of a witness. Not a
  confession — a *true thing*. Most keepers never manage it. The Hollowed, by
  definition, never do.
- **You cannot swear for someone else.** Four hundred years of the order have
  tried. It is the reason there is one keeper per lantern and not a garrison.

### The Crown's version

Taught at the Kindling now, in the flat voice of a man reading a receipt:

> *By the Throne that keeps us,*  
> *by the Warden who keeps the fire,*  
> *I will keep my wick low and my mouth shut,*  
> *I will be silent in the light and obedient in the dark,*  
> *and I will guide no ship*  
> *that the Throne has not counted.*  

Then, and this is the line the whole book is arguing with:

> *A keeper who is seen is a keeper who has failed.*  

### The older vow

The keepers' vow is not the oldest oath on the Reach. That is the **guides' vow**, cut into a
cellar wall at the Sill in the year the road drowned, five lines long, with a fifth line the Crown
struck out two hundred years ago and eleven people still say in a cellar: *we will guide any ship
that asks.* Full text and history in [[The Bands]].

It is the counter-argument to the catechism and it is deliberately unglamorous: no light, no rank,
no reward, and no promise that anyone will know your name. Mira quotes it at the fleet in Ch. 27
before she quotes her own, which is the only ordering that could make her speech honest.

### The line that was about something else

*I will guide no ship that the Throne has not counted* reads like a shipping rule. It is
not. It was written two hundred years ago to outlaw the **green bands** — the guides who
walked people through the dark road with a light on their wrists that anybody could share
([[The Bands]]). The clause worked: the bands came off the roll, the guides were disbanded,
and the order has been taught ever since that giving them up was a virtue.

Which is why the line lands the way it does in Chapter Twenty-Two: a hundred knights say it
in a hall at dawn, and eleven people three floors below them are doing the thing it forbids,
with the Warden's silent permission, because the coast still gets dark at night.

Ede's copy is illegal. Possessing the long form is a fine; saying it in public is
a night in a Saltmere cell; saying it at a lit wick is a charge the Crown calls
*rekindling*. Rook has the catechism by heart, and the first time he hears the
old vow, in Chapter Twenty-Two, he recognises the shape of it and cannot think
why it makes him angry.

## Part two — The Last Word

### The scaffold

Captain Alder Vane was hanged at Saltmere eleven years ago, in the same week the
Crown put out the eight lanterns, on the charge of *bearing a light against the
Throne*. He had taken a lit lamp out past the Ninth and come back claiming he had
been over the Fall and back again, which was the part the Admiral could not
forgive, because it was the part she believed.

Four thousand people came to watch. As the custom is, he was asked whether he had
anything to say.

> "I have. I left it out there. All of it. In one place, and I didn't hide it  
> well, and I'm not going to tell you where it is." He looked at the crowd until  
> it stopped moving. "The way over the Fall. The other half of the sky. The reason  
> the lights went out the first time. You want it? Then do what I did. Light  
> something the dark doesn't want lit, and follow it. That's the whole map."  

Then he grinned at the Admiral, and the crowd — which had come to watch a man die
— stayed to watch a man win, and the Crown has been paying for that ever since.

### What the words cost

- **The Vane Statute.** Repeating the Last Word in a public house is a fine of
  forty salt-scrip, or a night in the cells. Every sailor on the Reach can say it
  from memory. The fine is the reason it is said.
- **The Long Hunt.** A generation of crews who sail for the Fall looking for what
  he left. Most of them find dark water, debt, or nothing. A few do not come back,
  which the ones who remain take as evidence.
- **"That's the whole map."** A proverb now, used for any plan that is mostly
  nerve. Isolde uses it twice, both times badly.
- **A name.** Vane is the commonest salt-name on the Reach. Four thousand people
  heard it on a scaffold and a few hundred boys have been given it since, from
  the story, by docks and orphanages and mothers with a sense of humour. Teo has
  never once been asked whether he is *that* Vane. He has an answer ready.

## Rules for using either

1. **The vow is spoken at thresholds, never in triumph.** Before a light, before
   a fight, before a goodbye. Never over a victory.
2. **The Last Word is quoted by liars and believed by the frightened.** If a
   character quotes it, we should know which one of those they are.
3. **Nobody explains the homage.** No one in the Reach has read a comic or a
   manga. The reader can hear the shape; the characters never can.
