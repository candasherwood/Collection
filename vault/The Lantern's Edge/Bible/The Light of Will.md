# The Light of Will

*The magic of [[The Lantern's Edge — Story Bible]], written as a contract with
the reader: these rules are the same in Chapter Five as in Chapter Thirty.*

## A lantern

A lantern is a tower with a lens at the top and an **Ember** at the heart of it:
a shard of the Circlet's dead sun, no bigger than a fist, warm as a horse.
The tower is chimney and lens both — it gives the light a shape and throws it
nine miles out to sea.

There are nine. One per keep, along the Edge. The Ninth is at Carrow, which is
the last light before the Fall, and it has been dark for eleven years.

A lantern cannot be lit by accident. It needs a keeper with a ring, a flame to
start it, and — this is the part the Salt Throne leaves out of the catechism —
a reason.

## The mother, and the forge (Ch. 23)

Every Ember is cut from one stone. Under the Kindling, at the bottom of the chimney,
there is a wheel forty feet across turning in a room with **no fire in it**, and in a
cradle of iron bands at the heart of the wheel there is a grey stone the size of a cart.
It is warm. It gives off heat and nothing else. It is what is left of the sun that was
inside the Circlet, and the order calls it **the mother**.

- **Nine sockets** ring the cradle, and all nine are empty, because each one's mate is in
a tower. The ninth is in the brass cup of a chipped lens, in a canvas bag on the *Mercy Bell*.
- **Cutting** is a day's work for four masters: a fist of the mother, split off and
  shaped. **Quenching** is the part nobody writes down. A keeper stands at the trough and
gives **a season** into the glass — the weather and the food and the people in it, the same
price as a great Holding — and the Ember takes it. After that the Ember gives light when it
is lit and nothing when it is not, and costs its keeper nothing more, ever.
- That is the whole trick the nine towers are built on, and it is why there have been
**nine Embers in four hundred years** and not nine hundred: there have never been more than
nine keepers at once willing to pay a season for a lamp that would not be theirs.
- The Crown forbade the cutting in the first month it held the towers, and put an Admiralty
clerk at the top of the stair with a key. Not out of piety. **You can count a ship. You cannot count a girl at a trough**
who can make a light in a year, in a place you have never heard of, with a ring and no
licence. Nine made this coast. Nine hundred would make it ungovernable.
- **The forge is the prize, not the heresy.** The visitation that arrives in Part Four is a
valuation: the writ names the forge, the mother and the trough. A Crown that owns the trough
can conscript keepers and cut Embers by the hundred — which is the reform, stated plainly,
and the reason Chapter Twenty-Seven has to happen in front of everybody.

The senior brothers have known all of this for two generations. That is [[The Emberwatch]]'s
real secret, and Rook's faith breaks on it, not on the Crown.

## A ring

Edge-rings are hoops of lantern-glass, green-black, cut from the same pour as the
lantern's own lens. Every keeper is given one at the Kindling and it is not a
decoration and it does not come off.

A ring does not store light. It **focuses** the wearer's will into a Holding.
With the lantern lit behind you, a ring is a hand on a lever. With the lantern
dark, it is a hand on nothing.

Some people, ringless, can see what a lit lantern reveals. It is rare, it is not a
gift, and it is slowly ruining Teo Vane's sleep. The coast has a name for them and
a crown has a list: see [[The Marked]].

## A band (Ch. 22)

The other instrument, older than the towers, and the one the Crown spent two hundred
years pretending did not exist.

A **band** is a hoop of the same green-black glass worn at the left wrist. It holds
nothing. It **shows a road**: the road under your feet as it actually is, holes and
all, unlit and plain, and it can be shared by anybody holding the wearer's hand.
Where a ring is a hand on a lever, a band is a hand on a shoulder in the dark.

- It **shimmers** and does not glow; it cannot be seen from more than a few feet, and
  it turns towards another lit glass like a compass.
- It **keeps the road it has walked**, so a guide can do a route blind for the rest of
  his life, and his band goes to the next guide when he dies.
- It **takes hours**, not memory: six hours on the road, six hours gone out of the
  middle of a life, with no note and no face to point at.
- It goes **dark and still works**, which is the whole reason it was banned: a light a
  parish can carry is a light a parish can use.

And the flaw, which is the worst thing in the book's second half: a band that holds a
road for enough people, long enough, learns to take the price out of the *guided*. That
is the **caramel light**. Full rules, the eleven guides, and the box nobody looks at:
[[The Bands]].

## The colour of a blade (Ch. 25)

Every Ember is a fist of the mother quenched in a season, and the **colour is the kind of season it
was**.

- **Ember-gold** — the order's blades. Quenched in the bearer's own season, given. Lights when he
  means it, flickers, smells of hot iron, and will not come up for a man lying to himself.
- **Red** — quenched in a season **taken**: a bargain whose giver did not understand it, or did
  not survive it. It does not take its price from the hand; it takes it from **the room** — the
  hour, then the year, then the name, out of whoever is nearest. Six red blades at once go the air
  cold and stop the sound of a yard. It is brighter than gold, faster than gold, and cheaper in
the way stolen money is cheaper.
- **Teal** — quenched in many seasons given **knowingly**, at once, by a whole village. Starts
  grey. Lights dim. **Grows for years** if the bearer keeps giving, and can become a great light —
not a bigger blade, a light. Rare enough that the Assize calls it a superstition and keeps one
anyway.

The teal is also, at the Fall, the second **coin**. The Mother takes a season out of a keeper, or —
the only other thing she will take — **a light that has been given**. A teal blade is that and nothing
else: seventeen seasons given knowingly and none of them kept. Stood point-down in a housing with the
cup packed with oakum it *is* a lamp, and it counts, and the Receiver takes it as the ninth line of an
account that was short by one. It goes **up** instead of out and leaves a small light on the rock
behind it, which is either the mechanism or the joke.

And the rule that follows from all of it, which the book turns on: **a red blade is a door nailed
shut.** Light comes out of it and nothing goes in, so the Ash cannot use it as a door and does not
follow it. The Ash runs from the Assize. A taken light is the one thing on the Reach the dark will
not touch, and that is what makes the men who carry it so certain they are right.

Full codex: [[The Scarlet Assize]].

## Holding

A **Holding** is the keeper's will given a shape and made visible. It is the
central image of the book, so the rules have to be exact:

- **Shape follows intent, but only a shape you can picture with your hands.**
  You can hold a rope, a hook, a door, a stair, a bird, a blade. You cannot hold
  "safety" or "a fleet" or "the way home".
- **It is as strong as you are certain.** Doubt makes it flicker. A lie in the
  keeper's mouth makes it fail outright — which is why no keeper can bluff, and
  why the ones who learn to lie are dangerous.
- **A practised lie is invisible.** The light reads certainty, not truth. A lie the
  keeper has told so often he no longer flinches passes a Holding clean — so the
  dangerous liars are not the ones who lie well but the ones who lie *fluently*.
  Teo's rings are the small case (Ch. 16); Ossian Vey is the large one.
- **A lens with no tower takes its shape from whoever stands in the beam.** A tower
  is chimney and lens; a lens alone will not point, and a person standing in it can
  be made to do the pointing. This is what the third column of the Glass Roll —
  *lens-capable* — is for, and why a marked man can hold a light with no ring, and
  pay with nothing between him and the price (Ch. 16).
- **It is heavy.** A rope across a gap feels like a rope across a gap. The light
  does not spare your shoulders or your knees.
- **It burns memory.** Minutes of holding cost minutes. A great holding costs a
  season: a face, a name, the smell of a kitchen. Keepers carry notebooks
  because the light takes the wick, and the wick is what you remember.
- **What is taken stays taken.** Reading your own notes afterwards does not give
  it back. You recognise the handwriting, not the person in it. Notebooks keep
  you behaving like yourself; they do not keep you.

## What light reveals

A lit lantern shows the sea as it was before the break:

- **The Drowned Road** — the old coast road, running under three feet of water,
  lit up like a seam of gold.
- **The un-ships** — vessels from before the Fall, still sailing, still crewed
  by nothing. Page nineteen of the notebook: *When the light comes back on,
  count the ships that are not there.*- **The Circlet** — the ring overhead, banded in dark metal and dead lights, in a
  sky that has no other business being that close.

## The flaw (Ch. 17)

A lens that has been broken shows what it has broken *into*. A chip, or a sheared
ring, splits the beam, and the same patch of sea is printed twice, side by side, a
hand's width apart at twenty feet and a boat's width apart at a hundred.

The copy is not a ghost and not a trick. It is the glass's own memory, repeated, and
that makes it **perfect** — which is the danger. The real road has four hundred years
of damage on it: a fallen wall, a silt bank, a lugger that went down in a storm, the
roof of somebody's house. None of that is in the glass's memory, because the glass
remembers the road as it *was*, so the present comes through as a **hole in the
gold**.

Two working rules, and both are the book's thesis in a small room:

- **You do not steer by the road. You steer by the damage.** The hole is the truth.
- **The copy repeats.** A copyist's eye slips, and a copied landmark comes twice —
same chip out of the same corner, a hundred and forty feet on.

And one the ears get before the eyes: a wall of stone in shallow water makes a flat
*tock* when a swell reaches it, and the shape of it stands about a foot and a half
off. A gap makes no noise. A copy of a wall makes no noise at all, because it is a
picture and not a wall — which is why a blind navigator can hear the true road and
cannot hear the false one.

## People in the glass (Ch. 18)

The same rule, pointed at a crowd, and the worst thing the book has shown so far. A
town under the lens comes up gold — walls, quay, roofs, the road up to the keep — and
then the present begins to show: **holes**, upright, the size and shape of people,
standing in the street where the people are standing. Nothing is being hidden. The
light is remembering a road with nobody on it, and whoever is on the road now simply
is not in the memory, so the gold goes round them the way water goes round a stone.

Which means a reader — and later a keeper — can tell a crowd of the Hollowed from a
crowd of the living at nine miles, and the count is the same count. Sixty men go up a
hill; four come down; **fifty-six holes** stand on the road; one shape lies across a
doorway and does not move.
Do not have anybody explain this in dialogue twice. Mira says it once, badly, to Pell,
and is glad nobody asks her to say it again.

## A light with no colour (Ch. 20)

Ede Calloway holds four miles of edge round Carrow every night with no lamp, no wick,
no lens and no ring — rung four of [[The Ladder]], paid a season at a time — and the
thing that makes it work is that **it does not shine**.

- **A Holding takes the shape you can picture with your hands**, and a ring is the
  easiest shape there is: a hoop, a circle, a closed line at the waterline.
- **A ring has no ends.** An edge can be walked round; a ring cannot. The Ash comes up
  the drowned road, meets the wall of it, goes along it looking for a way past, and
  there isn't one, so it goes home and eats a little of the road on the way out.
- **Brightness is the flaw, not the power.** A beam is a thing that can be studied: it
  has an origin, a direction and an end, and the Ash is patient enough to find all
  three. Colourless light has no origin to look at. So the wall is invisible, the Ash
  never learns where it stops, and eleven years of it have been eleven years of the same
  night.
- **A keeper can feel another keeper's Holding** if they are close enough to it, the way
  you feel a door close in a house you thought was empty. There is no light on the wall
  to see. Mira feels it go up and is frightened by how ordinary it is.
- **The price is one season a year**, unchanged by the size of the hoop — which is the
  trap. A larger ring is not more expensive, only longer to hold, and a woman with eleven
  seasons in her can hold four miles for eleven years and not one night more.
- **It cannot be handed on.** The hoop is her will at the waterline; the moment she puts
  it down, the sea inside it is only sea again. There is no version of it that a knight
  can do, or a navigator, or a fleet.

## Dousing

A lantern goes out three ways:

1. **Salt and time.** The sea gets in; the Ember cools; eleven years of weather
   is enough.
2. **A broken ring.** Instant, enormous, and it takes the keeper with it.
3. **A keeper who stops believing.** No flame, no shape, no light. This is the
   only method that leaves the tower intact.

The Ash can do none of these things itself. That is why it needs people.

## The Ash

Not a god. A weather.

The Ash is what was on the other side of the Circlet when the ring broke: an
unmaking that eats will and leaves the shape of a person behind. What it leaves
are the **Hollowed** — walking, working, polite, and empty. They keep their jobs.
Some of them keep their families.

- It cannot cross a lit edge — and it cannot cross an edge it cannot see, which is why
  Carrow has never been touched.
- It is patient. It will wait eleven years in the water outside a dark tower. It has
  learned to wait with a hull under it, which is worse.
- It is not cruel. It does not want you to suffer. It wants you to stop, and it
  has all the time there is.

## It takes a ship (Ch. 19)

For four hundred years the Ash walked ashore and stopped at the water, and the coast
built its whole defence on the difference between a place and a way of leaving. Rook
says it out loud on the *Vigil*'s deck, an hour before he is proved wrong: *a ship is
not a place; a ship is a way of leaving; it cannot take a way of leaving.*

At Barrowfoot it took a three-decker at anchor without a blow struck and without one
of her company dying. It did not fight. It **worked**. The Hollowed came up the chains
and took in slack and coiled rope and washed a pot, and the ship's company stood and
watched them do it until there was nothing left on that deck that was theirs to do.

The lesson, which Part Four runs on: the Ash does not need to beat a fleet. It needs
to be **hired** by one. A routed crew can be replaced; a crew that has been relieved
of its duties cannot be got back, because there is nothing to appeal to.

And the bell counts. That is not decoration: the Hollow Bell struck once every six
seconds, fifty-six times, and stopped when the last of its people was aboard —
including the four who had been on the ship all along, whose places it had been keeping.

## It follows the light (Ch. 21)

The other half of the rule, and the one the whole book is built on: **the Ash does not
want a coast. It wants a door, and a lit thing is a door.**

Eleven years of a wall with no light behind it taught it nothing, and it left Carrow
alone — until a girl lit the Ninth for one night in Sixthmonth and it turned west, and a
lens threw a road at Candlewick and it came up out of the water with thirteen ships on
it, and six hundred men stopped rowing at the Gull's Teeth and by the end of the week it
had a three-decker.

Which means the siege at Carrow was never a siege. In Chapter Twenty-One, **Sallow** is
the one who says it, because a man paid six salt a week to write down where people are
is the only person in the room thinking about where the Ash will be: *that is not a wall
it is testing. That is a light it is following.* The light leaves, and the siege goes
with it, and Carrow is left dark and uninteresting behind a ring nobody can see.

The cost of the trick, and the reason it belongs to this book: to draw the Ash off a
coast you have to be **seen**, which is the one thing the Crown's catechism forbids —
*a keeper who is seen is a keeper who has failed.* Mira spends Part Four being the
brightest thing on the Reach with the dark behind her, and every mile of it is a
declaration that the catechism was written by people who were afraid of exactly this.

## Ember-blades and relic-tech

- **Ember-blades** are knight's weapons with a thread of Ember in the fuller.
  They glow when the wielder means it. A knight who lies to himself cannot draw
  light — which makes Rook's blade a running honesty meter, and a problem.
- **Glass-sight** is a brass tube with lantern-glass for a lens: it shows the
  un-ships to anyone, at the cost of a headache and bad dreams. The Emberwatch
  issues them. The Salt Throne issues more of them than it admits.
- **Saltlocks** are the closest thing to firearms on the Reach: heavy crossbows
  that fire a glass bolt packed with brine. They put out small fires and
  unlucky keepers.
- No printing press, no telegraph. Everything is copied by hand, which is why the
  Reach's history is thin and why losing a notebook matters.

## Limits, for plot

- One keeper per lantern. The ring bonds; you cannot lend it. A lens can be made
  to take a stand-in, which is not the same thing and is worse.
- A Holding cannot leave the keeper's sight.
- Nine lanterns. Eight are dark. The Ash has been eating at them for eleven
  years and it is very close to the middle.
- Memory loss is permanent and cumulative. Track it.
