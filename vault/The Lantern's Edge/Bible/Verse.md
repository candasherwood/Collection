# Verse — the two lights

*Poems for [[The Lantern's Edge — Story Bible]]. In this world verse is not decoration: a vow is
said in verse, the short form fits on a matchbox, and half the guides' road-calls are sung. So a
poem in this book is always **somebody's mouth** — copied into a notebook, called across water, said
at a wick. Never an epigraph, never a chapter heading, never the narrator being lyrical on its own
account.*

## The seed draft

The first one came in whole and is kept exactly as it arrived, because the thing it gets right is the
thing the whole book is about — that a light held by two people is steadier than a light held by one,
and that the two do not have to be the same kind of thing to hold the same ring:

> One holds the dawn, one holds the dusk,
> Between them, nothing breaks or rusts.
> Two sparks that circle, never fight
> And make a single, steady light!

That is the seed. Everything below grows out of it: dawn/dusk, two sparks, a left and a right bound
together, and the plain arithmetic at the end — one light out of two.

## The rule of the pair

The book already runs on twos, and they are all the same shape:

| The left | The right | What holds them |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The south anchor on its Ember | The north anchor with a boy's teal blade | Nine miles of ring, both ends on rock |
| Ede at Carrow, holding four miles with no light | Mira at the Fall, holding nine with one | A ring with no ends; a shore made of ends |
| Rook's dead iron in his left hand | Ede's empty ring on his right thumb | *An empty ring is the correct equipment for a knight of the watch* |
| Teo's eyes, which see the road | Mira's hands, which hold it | The lane in the dark, said out loud |
| The green band (the way) | The teal light (the gift) | Nobody has ever held both at once |
| The vow Mira can no longer say | The vow Ede says after her every night | *It isn't the words that keep a light. It's the saying.* |

Warcraft's phrase is the right one for the register, not the plot: **the right and the left are bound
forever.** Two things that cannot win alone and are not asked to. The dark does not beat a pair; it
beats a gap.

## The poems

### The Two Anchors — Ede's hand, under the vow (Ch. 29)

*Ede wrote it at nineteen under the long form and it has been under the vow for fifty-two years. Four
lines of the seed, four of her own, and the last line is the trick she teaches in Chapter Twenty-Eight.*

> One holds the dawn, one holds the dusk.
> Between them, nothing breaks or rusts.
> Two sparks that circle, never fight,
> and make a single, steady light.
>
> You keep your end and I keep mine,
> and let the dark come up the line.
> A shore has ends. A ring has none.
> Two rocks, and nine miles held as one.

### The Road-Call — the guides in the shallows (Ch. 29, or book two)

*Half song, half instruction, sung low so the water does not carry it far. Odo Pike's grandmother's
version; the last line is the fifth line of the guides' vow with the Crown's strike taken out.*

> Left hand the wall, right hand the light,
> count the stones and not the night.
> Hold your end and call it plain —
> the road goes on, and we go with it.

### The Short Form, in verse — page one of Ede's notebook

*What keepers actually say. Three lines, and the third is the one that costs.*

> Light what you can.
> Count what you lose.
> Answer for what you see.

### A Hollowed Lullaby — the shingle at the Fall (Ch. 30)

*Nine hundred people with their faces to the water, and one of them humming. Nobody in the fleet ever
finds out which one. It is the only singing on that shore and it is not for the living.*

> Sleep, and the light will keep the rest;
> sleep, and the dark will do its best.
> I have your hand. I have your name.
> The water takes us both the same.

### The Pair's Vow — Mira and Teo, said badly, in a boat (Ch. 30)

*Not a marriage and not a promise of safety. Two people agreeing to be each other's way home, which
is the only vow either of them can still say. Deliberately plain: the boy cannot see the road and the
girl cannot say the long form, so they say this instead.*

> You are my left, I am your right.
> You hold the dark, I hold the light.
> If one of us goes out tonight,
> the other one keeps walking.
>
> No ring, no crown, no oath to bind —
> just: I will not leave you behind.
> Two sparks that never learn to fight,
> and out of the two, one steady light.

### The Left and the Right — Rook, over the dead iron (Ch. 30)

*Said to nobody, in a yard, with a sword that will not light and a ring that was never his. Four lines,
flat, the way he says everything that costs him.*

> Iron in the left, and ring in the right.
> One of them is dead and one is a loan.
> I will carry both until it is light,
> and the left and the right are bound, and I go alone.

### The Ninth Light — for Toby, if he ever gets a chapter of his own

> Seventeen gave it. One let it go.
> It did not dim, it did not die —
> it went up out of a cup of oakum
> and left a small light on the rock behind.

### What the Light Keeps — the price, in four lines

*Close to the rule in [[The Ladder]]: the light takes the thing you were using to hold on. Never
explained in the manuscript; this is the bible's shorthand.*

> It takes the hand that held the rope,
> the voice that said the name,
> the smell of a kitchen at four o'clock —
> and leaves you the words, and the shame.

### The Fall Goes Quiet — the last cadence of Part Five

*If Chapter Thirty wants a closing quatrain rather than a flat sentence. Use one or the other, not
both: the plain line is the book's usual move, and this is the exception that earns it.*

> The noise went out of the world at seven.
> The sea came back at nine.
> Eight hundred and eighty stood in the rain
> and none of them crossed the line.

## Where verse is allowed

- **In a notebook**, in somebody's hand, with a note about who wrote it and when. This is the default
  and it is why the book can carry poems without turning into an anthology.
- **Out loud, at cost.** A thing said over a wick, a wall, or a body. If nobody is speaking it, cut it.
- **The seed poem itself** belongs to Ede and to the pair — one stanza in Chapter Twenty-Nine, the
  full thing when Mira and Teo say it to each other, spelled out, in the dark.

## What not to do

- No epigraphs. No chapter-heading verse. No narrator reciting.
- Two stanzas maximum in the manuscript; the rest live here.
- Do not rhyme *light* with *night* more than once a book, and if it must happen, make the second one
  cost something.
- The moment a poem explains the theme, it is dead. The pair poem above says *one steady light*
  because Ede says it to a boy in a boat, not because the book needs a thesis.
