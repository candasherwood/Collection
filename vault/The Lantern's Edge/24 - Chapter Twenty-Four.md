# Chapter Twenty-Four

The sensible thing happened at first light, which is how Mira knew it was going to be a bad day.

It was Sallow's idea, and he was irritatingly pleased with it. "A boat that goes out at dawn is a
fishing boat," he said, standing on the slip with his collar up and his pencil behind his ear.
"A boat that goes out at noon is a message. The Crown has nine hundred men in Saltmere who have
spent their whole lives reading messages."

"Nine hundred?" said Pell.

"Six hundred and forty. I rounded up. It's a habit you get, being paid by the week."

So the *Tongue* was loaded at first light with the lens in her crutch and the Ember in the brass
cup, and a coil of rope, and a bucket of bait that nobody intended to use, because a boat with
bait in it is a fishing boat and a boat without is a boat with a story, and Teo got in the stern
with his hand on the tiller and Marrow got in the bows and did not sit down.

Mira walked down the slip with them and stood at the end of it with her hands in her coat.

The harbour at the Kindling is a stone basin under the east wall, and the mouth of it is narrow
enough that the fortress's guns cross twice over the water, and the sea beyond is grey and flat
and has been flat for four days. Nine miles out, the Ash sat with one window lit. It had not
moved since dawn and it had not moved at dawn and it was not going to move now, because it did
not need to. It was patient in the way that weather is patient, and it had all the time there
was.

"Say it," said Teo, with the boat still on the rope.

"Say what?"

"You've been standing there with your mouth shut and your face doing that."

Mira looked at him. He had a rope burn across one knuckle and three days of not sleeping under
his eyes, and the canvas bag with the Ember in it was between his feet where he could put a hand
on it, and he had put a hand on it about every ninety seconds all morning.

"Come back at dusk," she said. "Not before. Not if you see a sail, not if you hear a bell, not if
somebody on the wall waves a flag and looks convincing. Dusk."

"And if there's a fleet?"

"Then you go west, and you keep going, and you don't come back at all."

"That's not a plan," said Teo. "That's a will."

"It's the plan where you're alive at the end of it. I like that one."

He looked at her for a moment.

"You know what's wrong with you?" he said. "You've started talking like a woman who's going to
be somewhere else tomorrow."

"Teo—"

"Dusk," he said, and put the tiller over, and the *Tongue* went out of the harbour mouth with a
bucket of bait and the last Ember on the Reach in a canvas bag between a boy's boots.

Isolde came down the slip with her hat on and stood beside Mira and watched the boat until it was
a mark on the grey.

"You told him dusk," she said.

"I told him dusk."

"Good," said Isolde Sable. "He'll be back at dark, then. That boy has never once done a sensible
thing at the time it would have been sensible to do it."

---

The fleet came in on the turn of the tide, and there were nine sail.

They came out of the south in a long line with their topsails furled and their yards squared,
which is what a fleet does when it wants to be counted, and the *Surety* came first: a
three-decker, black and long, with a glass-sight masthead and not one lantern anywhere along her
ninety yards of deck. Behind her a transport, two cutters, and five of what a clerk would call
vessels and a sailor would call everything else, and the whole of it came up the grey water at
four knots with the gulls going round them and no flag flying except the Crown's.

The Kindling answered with a bell nobody had rung in a year.

"They've come early," said Rook, on the wall.

"They've come *on time*," said the Warden. "That's what frightens me. A man who comes early wants
something. A man who comes on time is going to do exactly what he said."

The Admiral landed at the head of the slip at the top of the tide with four marines, a
lieutenant, two clerks and Mr Vey, and there was nothing about the landing that was not
perfectly done. The barge came alongside the slip instead of the stairs, because the slip had
been swept; the marines came up in two files and went to the wall and stood with their backs to
it and their hands behind them, because it is easier to watch a crowd from a wall; the clerks
had a folding table and had it up and level and covered before the Admiral's second foot was on
the stone.

And the Admiral herself came up the slip in a plain coat and a plain hat and stopped at the top
and looked at the fortress for a moment the way a woman looks at a house she is thinking of
buying.

"Warden Halloran," she said. "You've had the masons in."

"Eleven years is a long time to look at a wall, Admiral."

"It is. I have been looking at the same four inches of my desk for eleven years." Voss took her
hat off and put it under her arm. "I would like to do this in a room, and I would like the
keeper to hear it, and I would like your guides to stop pretending that cellar is a root store.
If we can agree those three things in the next ten minutes we will not have to do any of it
twice."

The Warden considered the top of his own table.

"How long have you known about the cellar?"

"Six years," said Admiral Ilse Voss. "I have been sending the assessments to my own desk instead
of to Saltmere for four of them, which is a hanging offence, and I did it because the alternate
was to put eleven guides in a ledger and have somebody at the Throne decide what a guide is
worth." She put her hat on again. "Shall we?"

---

The great hall of the Kindling is one of the old rooms: forty feet of stone, a fire at one end
that has not been out in four hundred years, nine brackets on the east wall where the nine
lantern-keepers' shields used to hang, and a table long enough that a chapter meeting can be held
at one end of it and a chapter argument at the other.

They put the case on the table.

It was a strongbox with iron corners, three feet long, and the clerk who opened it did it the way
a man opens a reliquary, which is to say with his face turned slightly away. Inside, in cradles
of grey felt, there were three stones.

Mira had expected light. There was none. They were grey, and about the size of a fist, and warm,
and one of them had soot on it.

"That one," she said.

"That one," said Vey, without looking up from his ledger, "is the Eighth. Removed at Wren's Fold
on the ninth, in the presence of two sergeants and a licensed surveyor, the keeper being deceased
and the family being Hollowed and at their posts."

Mira looked at the soot.

She had stood in that lamp room with Alys Wren and watched a woman burn a year of herself for
four hundred people, and the thing that had been burning was in a box with a manifest.

"Three of eight," said Voss. "The Second, because it is under water to the second floor. The
Seventh, because the bell's tower is not there any more and neither is the tower's rock. The
Eighth, because the family are Hollowed and a Hollowed keeper cannot give a season, which is a
sentence no clerk in Saltmere wanted to write and I made him write it." She put her gloves on
the table. "I have five lit. A year ago I had none, and that is a number I would like to be able
to put in front of the Throne without a footnote, and it is what a formula gets you. I am not
proud of it and I am not ashamed of it. I am telling you what I have
because in nine days I have to give an account of the coast, and I would like the account to be
better than this."

"Sit down, Admiral," said the Warden.

"I have been sitting down since Barrowfoot," said Voss, and did.

What she wanted, she said, was three things, and she said them in order the way she said
everything.

The forge. The mother and the cradle and the trough, surrendered to the Crown, sealed, and
inventoried.

The Ninth's Ember, into the case, as Crown property.

And Mira Calloway, examined at Saltmere under the Glass Roll, and either licensed or listed.

"Not hanged," said Voss. "I want that on the record before anybody's face does what it is doing.
The Vane Statute is a fine and a bad afternoon. What I want is an assessment, because the Roll
says *keeper, unlicensed, Carrow*, in a hand I did not write, and I have not been able to trace the
hand, and there is no entry in the Reach that frightens the Throne more than a name nobody can
account for."

"You want to know what I am," said Mira.

"I want to know what you *cost*," said Voss. "You are the most expensive person on this coast and
I have four hundred thousand people who cannot eat a gift. You lit a tower and the Ash turned.
You sang and six hundred men stopped rowing. You have been walking around my war for a month
being *unaccounted for*, and every week of it the arithmetic gets worse, and I have run out of
ways to put you in a column."

"Put me in the column that keeps the coast."

"There isn't one," said Voss. "That's the whole of my problem. There is a column for keepers with
licences and a column for the marked and a column for the drowned and a column for the dead, and
you are in the last one as of this morning, because a clerk in Saltmere has entered you as
*deceased, presumed, Carrow, Sixthmonth*, on the evidence of a woman who saw a girl go into the
water." She turned her head. "Vey."

"Entry four hundred and nine," said Vey. "It is the tidiest entry in the book. I have been
looking at it for a week and finding it restful."

"I'm not dead," said Mira.

"No," said Voss. "You are the wonder of the Reach and a filing error, and I have nine days to
decide which one of those I am going to put in front of the Throne."

Mira looked at the three grey stones in their grey felt.

"You've been paying to keep it dark," she said.

The room went quiet in the way a room goes quiet when somebody has said the one thing that was
not on the table.

"For eleven years," said Mira. "Two hundred and forty salt a quarter, to stand in eight dark
rooms and sign the log and be seen to be there. It's in the ledger. It's the collateral behind
every salt-letter on this coast, so every letter a captain ever took was a promise secured by the
rent on the dark, and every town that ever paid in paper was paying it back with the light."

The Warden had gone very still.

"Where did you hear that?" said Voss.

"Brother Ostrand showed me the line."

"Brother Ostrand is a seneschal with a bad chest and no sense of occasion," said Voss, "and he
showed you a line I signed." She said it without any change of voice at all. "The third quarter
of the second year, and the fourth, and every quarter since. Did you imagine I did not know? I
wrote the schedule. I have a copy in the case with the stones."

"You—"

"I have been paying for the dark because I could not think of a better use for the money than a
coast that was quiet," said Voss. "That is not a defence. It is what happened. A woman came to
Saltmere in the second year with a proposal about ships and guns and a figure, and the figure was
smaller than the cost of keeping nine lanterns, and I signed it, and here is the thing that I
would like somebody to explain to me before I die: **it worked.** The Ash stopped coming ashore.
Eight towns that were going to be eaten were not eaten. I bought eleven years with two hundred
and forty salt a quarter and a receipt, and eleven years is eleven years, and I have never once
been able to work out whether I am the woman who saved the coast or the woman who sold it."

"You sold it," said Mira.

"Yes," said Voss. "Probably. But I sold it to myself, at a discount, and I have been paying the
interest every quarter in a hand I recognise."

She stood up and went to the fire and stood with her back to it, which put her face in shadow and
the room in her debt, and Mira understood that this was a woman who had been rehearsing this
conversation for eleven years and had never once been able to have it.

"Here is what I can do," said Voss. "I can take the forge today and put a Crown smith in it and
have him cutting Embers inside a year, and I can take the Ember off you and put it in a tower
with a licensed keeper and a sergeant at the door, and I can take you to Saltmere in a cabin with
a window and be praised for it. That is the writ. I have it in my coat and it is legal and it is
correct and it will half work." She turned round. "And in the half that doesn't work, the Ash
comes ashore at the Third in the spring and eats a town of nine hundred people, and I add them to
the number in my desk and I go on being correct."

"And the other thing?"

"The other thing is that I leave the forge alone until the ninth night, and I leave the light
where it is, and I go back to Saltmere and tell the Throne that the coast is being kept by a
keeper without a licence on a warrant of sufferance that I have no authority to issue." Voss
pulled her glove straight. "For which I will be relieved of my command inside a month and
replaced by a man who will do all three of the things I have just described, only faster, and
with a brass band."

Nobody said anything.

"I would like a third one," said Mira.

"There isn't one."

"There is. You said the Throne is frightened of a name nobody can account for." Mira put her
hands flat on the table, which was a thing she had learned from watching other people do it.
"So put me on a ship and take me to Saltmere and let them account for me. Leave the forge and
the light alone until the ninth night and let the argument be about me instead of the coast."

Voss looked at her for a long moment.

"You would do that."

"I have been doing it for a month. I just didn't know the paperwork."

"That is the first sensible thing anybody has said to me since Sixthmonth," said Voss, "and it
is the worst thing that could happen to you personally, which is how I know it is policy."

She looked at the Warden.

"I will give you until the turn of the tide. Not the tide tomorrow. *This* tide, at seven, because I have a fleet in a
harbour with a thing nine miles off it and I am not spending the night here."

"That's four hours," said the Warden.

"Yes," said Voss. "It is exactly four hours, and it is the only thing I have that is not
somebody else's."

---

They searched the boats at four.

It was done properly and it took an hour and a half: two marines and a sergeant to each hull, a
clerk on the slip with a list, and the *Mercy Bell* opened from the chain locker to the lazarette
and gone over by a man who knew what he was looking at and did not find it. Mira watched from the
wall and did not let herself look at the empty cup.

"Nothing?" said the lieutenant.

"Nothing," said the sergeant. "There's the Fourth's frame in the *Mercy Bell*'s crutch with the
glass out of it and the brass cup gone out of the bag, and no stone anywhere in her."

The lieutenant wrote that down. Then he looked up at the wall, at a girl of eighteen in a coat
too big for her with her hands folded in front of her, and he did the thing that lieutenants do
when they have been given an order they would rather not have been given.

"Miss Calloway," he said. "The Admiral's compliments. She would like a word on the quarterdeck at
six."

Mira made herself smile, because that was what her face did when it was frightened.

"Of course," she said. "Would you thank her for me."

---

At six they came for her, and she was polite the whole way, which was the worst sign.

They let her bring the notebook — Vey saw to that, and did it badly, with a great deal of
official noise about the chain of custody and a receipt in triplicate, so that a marine could not
possibly notice that the assessor had just done a girl a favour. Rook walked beside her to the
slip and did not say anything for forty yards, and then said, "I'm coming with you."

"You're not."

"The order—"

"The order has a Warden who is going to need every knight he has got tomorrow," said Mira. "And
your blade won't light, and you know why, and if you come aboard the *Surety* with a blade that
won't light you'll be a knight having an argument with himself in front of nine hundred sailors."

Rook stopped walking.

"That's the cruellest accurate thing anybody has said to me," he said.

"I've had a week of practice."

At the slip he put his hand out and she took it, which is not a thing knights do, and he said,
"Attendance is the third vow. Not obedience. Attendance." And then he let go and stood at the
head of the slip with his hands at his sides and watched the boat go out.

---

The *Surety* was the *Vigil* with the beeswax scraped off.

That was the thing Mira could not stop thinking, sitting in a cabin four decks up with a marine
outside the door and a window that did not open. The *Vigil* had smelled of polish and silence and
a bell with a cloth in its mouth. The *Surety* smelled of tar and men and the particular
vegetable smell of a ship's galley at six in the evening, and her decks were scrubbed but her
yards were working, and there were four hundred men aboard her who had been at sea since
Sixthmonth and looked it.

There was a chessboard on the sideboard.

That was the second thing. It was a cheap board, the sort a chandler sells, and the pieces were
carved wood and half of them had been replaced from a different set, and it was set up, and there
was a game on it, forty moves deep, and the black king was in a corner with three pieces round it
and no way out.

Voss came in at half past six and saw her looking at it.

"Konrad's," she said. "He plays white. He has played white for eleven years and he has never
once let me be black, and I have never once won."

"Admiral—"

"I am not going to interrogate you," said Voss. "I am going to tell you what happens at seven and
then I am going to ask you one question, and you should understand before I ask it that I have
already decided what to do if you answer badly, and that I will be sorry about it for the rest of
my life and that it will not change anything." She sat down. "At seven the tide turns and I serve
the writ. The forge is sealed. The Ember is Crown property from the moment it goes into that
case, and if it is not in my hand at seven it is *stolen* Crown property, which is a different
crime with a different rope. And you come with me to Saltmere, in this cabin, with that window
and a guard, and you are examined, and you are either licensed — which means a chair, a salary, a
tower and a sergeant at the door — or you are listed, which means the Roll, and the Roll is
charity, and charity is a room in a house in Saltmere with a window and a chessboard."

"Like your brother."

"Yes," said Admiral Voss. "Like my brother."

The ship's bell struck the half hour, muffled, and somewhere below a man laughed.

"Here is my one question," said Voss. "Where is my Ember?"

And that was when the window went gold.

---

It came up the harbour from the seaward end, low and slow, a lane of light nine miles long
lying across the water and over the wall and up the harbour and across the *Surety*'s own
quarterdeck, and every man on the ship turned to look at it at once.

Mira got to the window.

Out past the harbour mouth, four hundred yards off the rocks, the *Tongue* sat with her sail
down and her lens in the crutch and the light coming out of her in a bar so steady it looked
like something built. And in the lane, in the gold, the whole of the harbour came up as it had
been: the old quay under the new one, the drowned slip, the road going out under the wall and
away south, and the road going north, and — there, standing in the water where the fleet was —
holes. Upright. The size and shape of people, eighty of them, standing on the road at anchor in
the gold, where eighty men were standing on a deck.

The ship made a sound like a held breath.

"That's a light," said a sergeant, in the voice of a man reading a sign.

"That is a light," said Voss, "in my roads, at my anchorage, four hundred yards from my
quarterdeck, with a thing nine miles out that follows lights."

She was out of the cabin before the sentence was finished.

---

By the time they had Mira on the quarterdeck, the whole harbour was lit and nobody was working.

The lane lay across the water like a road drawn on the sea, and the road under it was gold, and
the *Tongue* sat at the near end of it with two men in her: Marrow in the bows with the boat-hook
against the rocks, and Teo in the stern with both hands on the lens frame.

He was shaking. Mira could see it from ninety yards: a fine shake in the shoulders that a man
gets when he has been holding something too heavy for too long.

"Mr Vey," said Voss, without turning round. "What is that in a legal sense?"

Vey had come up with his ledger already open, and he looked at the light, and at the road, and at
the eighty holes standing on the water, and he wrote something down.

"Bearing a light against the Throne," he said. "A fine of forty scrip and a bad afternoon. If the
light is Crown property and the bearer is not licensed, there is also the matter of the property,
which is a rope." He paused. "There is no entry in the book for a man holding a lane in the
roads of a Crown anchorage with no ring on his hand. I would have to invent a heading."

"Invent one."

"I have," said Vey. "I have written *vexatious.* I am not proud of it."

Voss turned to Mira.

"Is he doing that for you?"

"Yes."

"Will he put it out if I ask him?"

"No," said Mira. "He'll put it out when I'm in the boat."

The Admiral looked at her for a long moment, and then did the thing that Mira would remember
about her for the rest of the book, which was that she did not shout.

"Then get in the boat," said Voss.

They went out in the Admiral's own barge, four oars and a coxswain and a lantern hooded in the
bows, and the light lay across the water in front of them so thick that the oars made gold
splashes.

The *Tongue* was a small boat in a very large light. Marrow had the oars shipped and his hands on
the gunwale and his band going round his wrist, and he was not looking at the road; he was
looking at Teo, the way a man looks at a rope that is going to part. Teo had both hands on the
lens frame and his eyes open and he was not blinking.

Twenty feet off, the barge stopped.

"Mr Vane," said Voss.

"Admiral."

"You are four hundred yards off my bow in a boat with a light in it, and there are nine sail and
four thousand men behind me, and every one of them has just seen the road your lighthouse keeps
hidden. You have made yourself the most dangerous thing on this coast in about four minutes. Do
you understand what you have done?"

"Yes," said Teo.

"Then put it out."

"When she's in my boat."

"She is in *my* boat," said Voss. "And she will be in a cabin at Saltmere by Sunday, and you will
be at the bottom of the Fall by Monday, and this will have been a very expensive four minutes for
everybody." She leaned forward. "Why are you here, Mr Vane? You had a boat, a tide and a whole
west coast with no fleet in it. You could be past the Cradle by morning and nobody would ever
find you. Why are you four hundred yards off my bow making a target of yourself?"

And Teo, who had spent his whole life lying with exact details, who had seven rings and only one
of them a gift, who had been left on a dock as a child and had never once said a true thing out
loud when a clever one would do, said:

"Because I love her."

The water went very quiet.

"I'm sorry?" said Voss.

"I love her," said Teo. "That's the whole of it. There isn't a better answer and I've had three
days to look for one." He shifted his hands on the frame. "And I'm not going to put the light out
so you can take her to Saltmere and put her in a room with a window. I've seen what your
paperwork does to people. I watched it do it to a town."

"That is not—"

"You said it yourself. A light is a door." Teo's voice was perfectly level. "So here's the door,
Admiral. You've got nine sail in a harbour and a road full of holes where your men are standing.
Put a boat over that wall and take her, and I'll hold this lane till the glass cracks, and the
thing nine miles out will come to the brightest door on the Reach, and it won't be mine."

Nobody said anything for a moment.

Then Mira said, "Teo."

"Mira."

"Put the light out."

He looked at her for the first time.

"I'm coming across," she said. "Right now. Put it out."

"He'll—"

"He'll do what he says. He's done what she said all her life; it's the only thing she's got." She
was already standing up, and the barge was rocking, and the coxswain had a hand out to steady
her. "Put it out, Teo. I can't see your face with the light on it."

And Teo looked at her, and his hands came off the frame, and he put them over the brass cup the
way a man puts his hands over a candle, and the lane went out.

The dark came down over the harbour like a lid.

---

The terms took nine minutes and were struck in the dark, boat to boat, with the *Surety*'s bell
striking the hour behind them.

The forge sealed until the ninth night, with the Warden's seal and the Crown's on the door at the
head of the forge stair, and a Crown smith in the antechamber and no cutting, no quenching, no
key.

The guides and the drowned left off the Roll for nine days. Voss wrote that herself, standing up,
with the barge's lantern held over the paper, and it took her four attempts because the boat was
moving.

The Ninth's Ember into the case, as Crown property, in the strongroom of the *Surety*, logged,
numbered, and *unassessed*.

And Mira Calloway in a boat with a warrant of sufferance in her pocket, nine days, renewable at
the Throne's pleasure, which is to say not renewable at all.

"You will be back at Saltmere with a writ and a man with a band," said Voss, "and I will be
ashore at my brother's house explaining to a board why I let a girl and a boat hold my fleet for
four minutes."

"Will they hang you?"

"They will retire me," said Voss. "It's worse. A hanging is over in an afternoon." She looked at
the dark water where the light had been. "Nine days, Miss Calloway. Then you and I will both be
somebody else's problem."

The Ember came out of the cup. Mira took it out herself, because a keeper hands over her own
light, and put it into the Admiral's gloved hand, and it was warm, and it weighed about as much
as a fist, and it had been in the Circlet.

"Admiral," she said. "Your brother. At Brinehollow. Did he ever say anything after?"

"He said my name," said Ilse Voss. "Twice."

"That's something."

"It is nothing at all," said the Admiral, "and I have been living on it for eleven years."

---

The barge put her aboard the *Tongue* at twenty past seven, and Vey had the receipt in his hand
and his lantern on his knee before the gunwales touched, because he had written it at the table
in the cabin and had not been able to make himself hand it over in front of the Admiral. It said,
in a fine small hand:

*Item nine. One Ember, Ninth lantern, Carrow. Received into the strongroom of the* Surety *this
day, at seven in the evening, from the hand of the keeper. Condition: warm. Assessed: no.
Unassessed.*

"There," said Vey. "Unassessed. It is the same word I used for you at the Gull's Teeth, and I
have been thinking about it all week, and I have decided that it is the best word in the
language." He sanded the leaf and held it out. "It means nobody has decided what it is. While
nobody has decided, nobody can say what it is worth, and while nobody can say what it is worth,
somebody with money can make an offer."

Mira looked at him.

"That was almost kind," she said.

"It was *procedural*," said Mr Vey, and sat down in the barge's stern with his lantern and his
book. Then, over his shoulder, in the voice of a man closing a subject he had opened by mistake:
"The stone went into the case at seven. The case goes into the strongroom at eight. The book
requires the hour the keeper's hand let go of it and not the hour the door shut. It is the only
romance in my profession and I would rather you did not tell anybody about it."

---

The *Tongue* sat in the dark a hundred yards off the wall with Marrow's oars shipped and nobody
rowing, and Teo had not moved from the stern.

He had his hands in his lap. He was looking at the water where the road had been, at the harbour
mouth, at the wall with its lanterns going along it, one by one, as the watch came round.

"Teo."

"I can't find it," he said.

"Find what?"

"The harbour." He said it in a perfectly ordinary voice, which was the worst part. "I've been in
and out of it twice this week. I know there's a wall at forty feet and a bar on the north side
and the slip is on the east wall under the gun platform, and I know all of that because I have
been saying it to myself since I was twelve. And I'm looking at it now and it's just water. It's
not dark. It's *not there.*"

Marrow's hands tightened on the oars.

"How long?" said Mira.

"Since I put it out." Teo turned his head. "It took the harbour. That's what it took, this time.
Not a season, not a name. The whole shape of the place." He laughed, once, without any air in it.
"Eleven years I've been telling people I'm the best navigator on this coast and I couldn't find
my way into a harbour I've been anchored in for a week."

Mira got up and came aft and sat down beside him on the stern bench, close enough that their
shoulders were touching, and took his hand and put it on the gunwale.

"Right," she said. "Count with me."

"What?"

"Count with me." She looked out at the dark. "There's a wall at forty feet, north-north-east, and
it runs a hundred and ten yards, and then there's the bar, and past the bar the water's clean to
the slip. There's a bell on the wall at the gun platform and it strikes the half hour and it's
wrong by a count of three, so don't trust it. There's a chandler's lamp on the east wall that
isn't a light, it's a window, and there's a dog in the yard behind it that barks at the watch
and not at us. Forty feet. Say it."

"Forty feet," said Teo.

"North-north-east."

"North-north-east."

"Now count."

So they counted, the two of them, in fives, and Marrow rowed where she said, and the *Tongue*
came into the Kindling's harbour in the dark at a walking pace with a boy who could not see the
way saying the numbers out loud and a girl beside him saying them back.

Halfway in, Teo stopped counting.

"Did you hear what I said to her?" he said.

"Yes."

"Good." He put his head back against the gunwale. "I wasn't going to say it twice."

---

Isolde Sable was on the slip when they came in, with her hat in her hand and her knife in her
belt and the whole crew of the *Mercy Bell* behind her, eleven of them, wet to the knees, bands
going round in the dark.

She watched Teo come up the slip with his hand on Mira's arm and his eyes going the wrong way
along the wall, and she did not say anything until he was past her, and then she said, "Get him
in by the fire and get his boots off," and Nettle took him, and Pell took the other arm, and
Isolde turned round and looked out at the *Surety*.

"What did it cost?" she said.

"The Ember," said Mira.

"Not the Ember."

"The forge, for nine days. The guides, off the Roll, for nine. A warrant that expires on the
ninth night."

Isolde looked at her.

"That's not what I asked."

"Teo's harbour," said Mira. "It took his harbour. He can't find his way into it any more. He'll
have to be taught it again like a child."

Isolde Sable stood on the slip for a while with her hat in her hand.

"Right," she said at last. "What's it worth, then? The Ember. In a strongroom, in a case, on a
ship, unassessed."

"Mr Vey said a person with money could make an offer."

"A person with money," said Isolde. "Well. There's one of those on this slip and she's got a
ninety-foot lugger and a debt." She put her hat on. "Go and write your book, Miss Calloway. I've a
ship to value."

---

Mira wrote it up by the guides' fire at midnight, with the band warm on her wrist and Teo asleep
in a chair with his boots off and his feet wrapped in somebody's shirt.

*Thursday. The Admiral came at the turn of the tide with nine sail and three Embers in a case:
the Second, the Seventh, and the Eighth. The Eighth has soot on it and was removed at Wren's Fold
in the presence of two sergeants and a surveyor, the keeper being deceased and the family being
Hollowed and at their posts. I held that tower for one night and Alys Wren died in it for eleven
years and the whole of her is a line in a manifest.*

*She had a writ and it was legal and it was correct and it would have half worked, and she said so
herself, standing at the fire with her face in shadow.*

*She paid the stipend. She wrote the schedule in the second year and she has signed it every
quarter since, and she has known all eleven years what it was for, and she has been paying the
interest in a hand she recognises. I have been hating an office. It turns out to be a woman with
a scarred hand and a chessboard and a brother who says her name twice and then never again.*

*The forge is sealed until the ninth night. The guides are off the Roll for nine days, which is
the only thing I asked for that she gave me without arguing. The Ember is in the strongroom of
the Surety, logged, numbered, unassessed, and Mr Vey wrote the last word deliberately and told me
so.*

*Teo took the Tongue out at first light because it was sensible and came back at dark because it
wasn't, and it cost him his harbour. He cannot find the way in any more. I counted him in from
four hundred yards with the wall at forty feet and he said the numbers back to me like a child
learning them.*

*He told the Admiral he loved me. He said it to a third party, in the middle of something worse,
in the flattest voice I have ever heard, and then he told me he wasn't going to say it twice,
which is the most Teo sentence there has ever been.*

*I had offered her the third thing before he came in: take me and leave the coast alone for nine
days. He came back and made a better threat than my offer and the sum came out the other way.
She took the Ember instead of me, because a stone in a strongroom is a thing Saltmere can
understand and I am a name nobody can account for. So we have nine days and no Ember, and Teo
has no harbour, and if he had stayed away we would have had the Ember and no me. I have done
that sum four times and it does not come out in favour of the Ember, and I am not going to
pretend it does.*

*The Ash's window was nine miles out at dawn. At dusk, when the light went up, it came in to
five. It is sitting there now with one window lit and all the time there is.*

*Nine days. The forge sealed, the light unassessed, the guides off the Roll and a warrant in my
pocket that expires on the same night the contract does.*

*Cost, today: the Ember, the harbour, and a woman's eleven years.*
