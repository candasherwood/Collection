# Chapter Six

Mira woke because the ship was talking.

Not the sea. The sea was a noise she had stopped hearing at six years old. This
was the ship itself: rope working in a block somewhere overhead, a plank going
*tock* against another plank, water in the scuppers running the length of the deck
and back again like something pacing.

She was in a cupboard with a bed in it. Isolde had called it the third cabin and
had not been lying, exactly. It was a cabin, and it was third, and if you lay down
in it your feet were in the passage.

Light came through a seam in the boards. Not daylight. Gold.

Mira was out of the door before she had decided to be, in her stockings, with her
coat half on, and the deck she came up onto was full of a morning that did not care
about her at all.

The *Mercy Bell* was making north with the wind on her quarter. She was a working
lugger under the paint: two masts, a deck worn pale in the traffic lanes of it, a
bronze bell on the foredeck with a green bloom along one side where the sea had had
it. The smell was rope and tar and, underneath, the flat mineral smell of wet stone
— the smell of a ship that had been somewhere else for forty years and had not
finished drying out.

The light was where it had been all night: lashed to the mast in its bucket, with a
canvas hood over the top so that the glow came out sideways in a long low wedge and
did not point at the sky.

It was still burning. She had been afraid, in the cupboard, that it would not be.

"Morning," said Pell.

The cook was sixteen and built like a coat on a broom, and he was holding a pan with
both hands the way you hold a pan you have been told to be careful with.

"It's porridge," he said. "It's got salt in it. I put salt in it. I've been told the
sea air takes the taste out of things, so I put a lot of salt in it. Possibly too
much. I'd like it if you ate it anyway."

Mira ate it. It was terrible. She ate all of it, and Pell's shoulders came down an
inch, and that was the first thing she did on the *Mercy Bell*.

"This ship wants fourteen hands," Isolde said, coming aft with an apple and a chart
and the expression of a woman who had already been up for three hours and had
decided not to mention it. "She has six. The rest is rope and bad temper. You've
met Marrow. That's Nettle, our surgeon, who keeps a jar of teeth and will not say
whose. That's the goat. Nobody owns the goat. Do not become the person who owns
the goat."

The surgeon was a narrow woman with grey in her plait and a jar in her bag that
knocked gently against her hip when she walked. She looked at Mira's hands, and then
at her face, and said, "You're not sleeping. You'll sleep tonight or I'll make you."

"I slept."

"In a cupboard, for an hour, on a hatch cover with your boots on. I checked."

"I am the surgeon," said Nettle. "Checking is the whole of the work. It is very dull
and I am very good at it."

She took Mira's wrist, turned it over, put two fingers on it and counted, and Mira
counted too, out of habit, so that when Nettle said sixty-one Mira had got there
first and they looked at each other with the brief mutual respect of two people doing
the same useless thing.

"May I see it?"

Mira held out her hand. Nettle looked at the ring for a long time and did not touch
it, which Mira appreciated more than she could have said.

"Warm?"

"Warm."

"All the time, or since the tower?"

"Since the tower."

Nettle nodded as though that were the answer she had expected and had hoped not to
get. "I have been at sea thirty-one years," she said, "and I have met four keepers.
Two were old and could not tell me their own names. One was twenty and had paid for
nothing and was very pleased with himself, and I did not like him. The fourth was my
mother's cousin, and she paid a season a year for as long as I knew her, and she was the
kindest woman I ever met, and at the end she did not know me."

"What happened to her?"

"She went on keeping the light," said Nettle. "That is what happens to keepers.
Nobody takes the light away from them. They get lighter."

Mira looked at her own hands.

"That's not a diagnosis," said Nettle, watching her. "It's a story about four people.
I tell it to everybody who takes a ring. Nobody has ever asked me to stop, and I have
decided that means it helps."

Isolde cut the apple with a knife she had not appeared to draw. "Rules," she said.
"One. The light is the girl's. Nobody touches the bucket, nobody lifts the hood,
nobody looks into it for longer than it takes to see it's lit — one of the hired men
looked into it for a minute and a half on the way out and he's still not right —" she
gestured vaguely at a man coiling rope at the rail, who was humming.

" — two. Nobody says the word *knight* before noon. Three. If the bell rings and
there's no wind, you come and find me, and you do not go and look. Four. We do not
stop. Not for wreckage, not for cargo, not for a man waving his hat."

"Not for a man waving his hat?" said Mira.

"Particularly not that one."

The wind took the ship and the horizon came up from behind the bow and put itself
back down again. Mira stood at the rail with the porridge settling in her and looked
west, where Carrow should have been.

It was not there. She had known it would not be there, and it was not there, and
the being right about it did not help. Four hundred people, one hill, one tower.
She could not make the arithmetic between that and this grey nothing come out.

She looked back instead, over the stern, at the water they had come out of.

Behind the ship the sea was flat and the colour of old pewter, and the wake went
into it and stopped.

"Don't look at that," said Teo, behind her. "Nobody looks at that."

"You do."

"I look at it once a watch," he said. "For a count of ten. Then I go and do
something useful. That's the professional approach."

---

By the second dog watch the light in the bucket had been trimmed twice and the wind
had gone round to the north and there was still no ship and no land and no dark
water coming over the rail. Mira had learned the names of two ropes and the way to
the heads and the fact that a ship's biscuit could be used as a hammer.

At dusk Teo came up onto the foredeck with two things in his hands: a knife and a
biscuit.

He sat down on the rail with his back to the sea, which nobody sane did, and held
them both out.

"It's the good knife," he said. "And the worse half of the biscuit. Before you say
anything — I'm not being kind. The other half's gone hard and I've got better
teeth."

Mira looked at him.

"That's the worst lie I've ever heard," she said.

"It's the best one I've got."

She took the knife. It was good; a sailor's knife with the bolster worn round and a
shaft of something dark that had been somebody's hand for a long time. She had a
lump in her throat about it, which was ridiculous, so she cut the biscuit in half
instead of saying anything, and it took her four goes, and neither of them
mentioned it.

They ate. The sky went from slate to plum and the light in the bucket went from a
wedge to a beacon, and the sails turned into shapes and the shapes into a long
shadow going up and out and away.

"Ask me why I came back," Teo said.

"Why did you come back?"

"I came back for the light." He turned the knife over. "And then I stayed for you,
and I'm still working out the order."

The wind went past. Somewhere forward the bell ticked against its bracket, once,
with no hand on it.

"Everyone in Carrow thinks I was chosen," Mira said. "My grandmother's notebook,
the ring fitting, the light coming on. They'll tell it like that. They'll have it
half a story by the time we're back and a whole one by winter."

"And what do you think?"

"I think I put on a ring that fit and climbed some stairs."

"That's worse." He said it quietly. "Chosen would be a relief. Chosen means it
isn't your fault."

"Yes," she said, surprised by how much she meant it. "That's exactly what it
means."

"Nobody chose me, either," he said. "I got left on a pier with a boat name for a
surname and the sea's been choosing for me ever since. And I'm not good at this.
Staying. I'm good at leaving before it counts."

"I'm good at being needed," Mira said. "It isn't the same as being wanted. I've
worked that out about four times and it never sticks."

"Then we're both bad at the easy part," said Teo.

The bell did not ring again. The ship went on through the dark with her light out
ahead of her like a hand held up.

"I did," he said. "Choose you. That's one."

"What?"

"You said nobody chose you. I did. It's one choice, from one person, made in the
dark with no guarantee attached, which as far as I can tell is the whole business
of being alive."

Mira looked at her hands. Her hands looked back, being hands.

She got the notebook out of her coat and the pencil from behind her ear, and she
opened it at page twenty-two, where the ink said *Teodor Vane. The Mercy Bell. Both
of them are real.*

"What are you doing?" he said.

"Writing it down before I lose it."

"Can I see?"

She turned the notebook round. He read it. He read it the way he did everything
that mattered, with his whole attention and no expression at all.

"Read it out loud," he said.

"Teodor Vane. The Mercy Bell. Both of them are real."

"Again."

"Teodor Vane."

"Good," he said, and his voice had gone slightly wrong. "Now I know it works when
it matters."

She wrote one more line under it, and turned it round again so he could see, and did
not look at him while he read it, because she was not brave enough for that.

*He stays.*

He was quiet for a long time. The sort of quiet that costs him.

"Now I have to," he said at last. "You've written it down."

"Paper's a terrible thing to be bound by."

"It's the only thing on this ship that can't lie."

Mira laughed — one loud, undignified noise, out over the water, and put her hand
over her mouth.

"Say my name," she said, when she had got herself back.

"Mira Calloway."

"Again."

"Mira. Calloway."

"Right," she said. "Now we both know it works."

"Ask me the other one," Teo said. "You've been not asking it since the tower."

She did not pretend not to know. "If I forget you —"

"Then I'll introduce myself again," he said. "Every morning, if I have to. You can
count it, if that helps. It's the only promise I've ever made that doesn't need a
ring or a boat or a captain's permission."

"That's not a plan."

"It's the only one that survives the thing we're actually afraid of."

The light in the bucket went on burning. The sea went away underneath them, mile
after mile, doing what it did.

Mira sat on the rail next to him with her shoulder against his and did not make it
mean anything for a while, and then stopped pretending, which was better.

---

The shout came from the masthead a little after the change of the watch.

"Boat!" Pell, who had been sent up because he was light and because nobody else
would go. "Boat off the starboard quarter! One man in it, and he's —"

"Waving his hat?" Isolde called up.

"No," said Pell. "He's rowing. Very badly."

They came up into the wind. The light swung round and found it: a ship's boat, the
Crown's own pattern, with a man in it in a grey coat with the collar up and the oars
in a tangle that he was losing.

Ser Cadan Rook shipped the port oar, waited for the boat to stop spinning, and stood
up in it with the water slapping at his knees.

"Permission to come aboard," he said, in a voice that carried the way it had no
business carrying.

"You're joking," said Isolde.

"The writ," said Ser Rook, with tremendous dignity, "does not specify whose ship."

The *Mercy Bell*'s crew looked at each other. The goat looked at the water. Mira
looked at Teo, who had put his head in his hands.

And behind them, over the stern, the sea stayed flat as a plate and did not close.
