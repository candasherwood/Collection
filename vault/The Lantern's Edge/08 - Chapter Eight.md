# Chapter Eight

They came into Wren's Fold at first light with the lead going and Pell in the chains,
calling the depths like a boy who had been told the numbers mattered.

The Fold was two arms of black rock with a harbour between them small enough to spit
across, and the keep built into the western arm with its lamp room over the water so
that the light went out along the channel and not over the village. Somebody had
built that, four hundred years ago, and thought about it.

The village had twelve houses. All twelve had smoke coming out of the chimney.

There was washing on a line along the eastern arm, pegged out and dry, with one
shirt still hanging and its pegs still on it, as though the person pegging it up had
been called away for a minute and would be back.

"Nets are stowed," Teo said quietly. "Oars stacked. Boat's hauled and chocked.
Nobody's been out in eleven years and everything's stowed."

"Tide does that," said Isolde, and did not believe it either.

They went in with the boat: Isolde, Teo, Mira, and the knight. Marrow held the
*Mercy Bell* off the wall and was to bring the boat round when Isolde waved, and
Mira watched him not look at the water the whole time, the way a man does not look
at a dog that has bitten him before.

"Anything moves," Isolde said, "we go back to the ship and we are not brave about
it."

The quay was dry stone and swept. There was a brass bell on a post at the end of it
and a harbour log on a lectern, open, with a pen beside it, and a man standing
behind the lectern with his hand on the page.

He was perhaps sixty. He had a good coat, mended at the elbow in small neat
stitches, and the particular settled expression of a man doing his job.

The last entry in the log was eleven years old. *Wind nor'west. Four boats out.
Four boats in.* The pen was still in his hand, resting on the paper, and there was a
small grey shadow under it where the ink had dried out years ago.

"Good morning," said Rook.

"Good morning," said the man.

"I am Ser Cadan Rook, of the Emberwatch, acting under the writ of the Salt Throne."
He had the paper out and open. "This is an assessment."

"Yes," said the man.

Rook read the writ aloud. Mira had heard it before, in a lamp room, and it had been
a sentence then. It was four minutes long. It had clauses. It described a procedure
for the surrender of a light, the sealing of a lens, the transport of a keeper, and
the disposition of a household, and Rook read the whole of it, in his flat careful
voice, to a man who could not refuse to listen and would not remember it.

When he finished, the man said, "Yes."

"Your name?"

"Perrin Wren."

"Your position?"

"Keeper of the Eighth."

"You are not the keeper," said Rook, quietly. "Your daughter is."

Perrin Wren stood with his hand on the log.

"Where is the rest of your household?" Rook said.

"At their posts."

---

The village was worse than the harbour.

A woman was in the boathouse with a wick-trimming knife, trimming a wick that was
not there — turning the blade against her thumb, flicking the parings away, doing it
beautifully. Two children sat on a bench outside the last house, facing the sea, with
their hands in their laps. A dog lay by the bench with its chin on its paws.

"Nettle says they don't start anything," Teo said. "No sentences. No steps. You have
to ask."

"Then don't ask them anything," said Isolde, "and don't stand in front of them
either, because they'll follow."

Mira looked at the children. The girl was eleven or so, with her hair in a plait
that somebody had done, and the boy was smaller, and both of them were looking at
the water with the mild, pleasant, empty attention of a house with the furniture
still in it.

"Are you cold?" Mira said, before she could stop herself.

"No," said the girl.

"Do you want to go inside?"

"No."

"What's your name?"

"Liss," said the girl.

The boy did not answer, and the girl did not answer for him, and Mira understood
that she had asked two people a question and only one of them had a job.

"Tobe," said Liss.

Mira got the notebook out and wrote the two names down, slowly, in her best hand.
Neither of them watched her do it. She put the book away and it did not feel like
enough, and it was the only thing she had that would still be true tomorrow.

Mira's eyes were doing something stupid. She looked at the washing instead: one
shirt, dry, pegged, and a line of small clothes taken in and folded and left on the
wall.

"Sixty-two steps," Teo said, at the tower door, because he had counted them on the
way up the rock and because he had learned that counting was what you gave her when
she was about to come apart.

Sixty-two steps. She counted them. It helped, and it was not enough.

---

Alys Wren was standing in the middle of the lamp room with her arms out.

She was thirty-four and looked fifty, and the light was coming out of her.

Not from a lamp. There was no lamp. The great lens stood behind her with its brass
drowned in dust and its glass dead, and the room was full of a steady amber glow
that had no source and cast two shadows from every object, and in the middle of it
Alys Wren stood with her palms up and her eyes open and her mouth moving.

"Four," she said. "Four. Four."

"Four what?" said Mira.

Alys looked at her. Her eyes were the colour of the light.

"Four of them," she said. "There were four. I'm keeping the number."

"Four children?"

"Four people in this house that are mine. My father. My mother. The boy. The girl."
She said it carefully, in order, as though reciting a list of provisions. "I can't
keep their names. I keep the number and I write the names down every morning and I
read them back and then I go and look at them and I say, that one is that one."

The ring on her hand was white — not glass with light behind it; white all the way
through, like a scar.

"Eleven years," Mira said.

"Eleven years." Alys lowered her arms. The light did not change. "The day they put
out the outer lights, my father came up those stairs with the writ in his hand and
he was crying, and I said no. He said, girl, they'll break the ring. I said no. He
said, I've got a wife and two children and a boy in the house. I said no." She
smiled, and it was a tired, ordinary, human smile. "So he went down and he did his
log and he put them all to bed and then he sat in the chair in the kitchen until it
went out of him. They came up here at the end, all four, and asked me to come down
for supper. I said no."

"You held it," said Mira.

"I'm still holding it." Alys turned her hands over. In the light, her fingers were
not quite solid at the tips. "It's not a wick, girl. There's no oil in me. It's
will, and it's been burning will for eleven years, and will is made out of what
you've got. I gave it my father's face first. Then the harbour. Then the boat. Then
their names."

"Why hasn't it gone out?"

"Because I haven't stopped believing," said Alys. "That's the trick of it. You can't
doubt for one minute or the whole thing falls over, and I have had eleven years of
one minute at a time. So." She put her hands down. "You're a keeper."

"Yes."

"You've got the ring on and you've lit a lamp."

"Yes."

"Then you know what it costs, and you're still here, and that means you're either a
fool or you're the girl in the notebook." Alys's eyes came round. "Ede Calloway's
girl. I heard about you before I stopped hearing about things. The old woman used to
write to my father. Page nineteen. *Count the ships that are not there.*"

Mira's throat closed.

"Don't cry," said Alys. "It's not a kindness to me. It just makes me want to
remember something and I can't." She nodded at the stair. "Your knight's got a book
in his bag."

---

The book was the Glass Roll: names in three hands, a column for the parish, a column
for the keeper, a column for the assessment.

Rook held it open on the ledge under the dead lens. His hands were not steady. He
had been in this room for six minutes and had not once looked at Alys directly, and
Mira understood, watching him, that this was not fear; it was the way he looked at
things he was about to be told to do.

"Alys Wren," he said. "Keeper of the Eighth. Registered as Bright. Assessment
deferred."

"Deferred," said Isolde, from the doorway.

"By order of the Warden of the Kindling," said Rook. "Eleven years ago. Deferred
indefinitely." He turned the page and then turned it back, as though the next line
might improve. "The notation is in the Warden's own hand."

"Of course it is." Isolde came into the room, hat in her fist. "Do you know what
that means, boy? It means your order has known for eleven years that the Eighth is
lit and that the light is coming out of a woman instead of a wick, and they have left
her up here burning because a Bright holding a lantern *works*, and the coast has had
one light in the dark and a crown with a clean conscience, and nobody has had to
write a letter about it."

"The assessment," Rook said, "would have taken her to Saltmere."

"And she'd have been a specimen in a room with a window, and the light would have
gone out, and the Ash would have come west eleven years early." Isolde put her hat
back on. "That's not mercy, Ser Rook. That's arithmetic with a signature on it."

Rook closed the book.

"I know," he said.

---

Alys showed them what she could do at the end, because Isolde asked, and because
Alys had not been asked for anything in a long time except questions that were
really interviews.

She turned to the dead lens and put her hand flat against the brass, and the light in
the room gathered itself and went out through the glass that was not there any more,
out over the water in a long gold road, and kept going, and kept going, and showed
them the east.

Mira saw the fleet first: the un-ships, fifty of them and more, sailing in a wind
that was not blowing. Then beyond them the grey that was a shade more grey than the
sky, that moved.

And among the un-ships, near the front, a three-decker with a brass masthead and no
lantern anywhere on her, making west.

"*Vigil*," said Isolde.

"They're not chasing you," said Alys, in a conversational voice, looking at the
water. "They're going to the Ninth. They know it's lit. They've known since the
night you put a match to it. That's what a dousing fleet does: it waits for the coast
to be frightened and then it comes and saves everybody with a form."

"They'll be at Carrow in four days," said Teo.

"Three," said Alys, "if the wind holds, and it will, because I'm holding it."

The light came back into the room and the ship on the water was gone and there was
only the grey sea and the grey sky and the two arms of the Fold.

Alys sat down on the step under the lens. It was the first time Mira had seen her
sit.

"When it goes out," she said, "and it will go out, because I'm nearly out — when it
goes, will you take it?"

"Take what?"

"All of it. The light. Not the tower; the tower's a chimney. What I'm holding." She
said it simply. "It has to go somewhere or it goes out, and if it goes out here then
every ship that's counting on the Eighth is counting on nothing, and I'll have done
eleven years of arithmetic for a dark room."

Mira's mouth was dry. "I don't know."

"That's the honest answer," said Alys Wren. "Come back and say it again when you do."

---

On the stair, on the way down, Rook stopped and opened the Roll on the ledge in the
wall where the light came through the slit, and took out his pen, and in the margin
beside *Alys Wren — Bright — Assessment deferred*, he wrote six words in the small
careful hand of a man who had been taught to write well by people he no longer
believed.

*Not assessed. Left at her post.*

Then, underneath, his own name and the date, because a knight of the watch signs what
he does.

---

Marrow came up out of the harbour a little after noon with a rope over his shoulder
and water running off him in sheets and something in his left hand.

It was a lamp housing. Brass, corroded green, split along a seam that Mira had seen
before, on a desk in a tower, under a knife. The kind of housing that had held a
stone the size of a fist for four hundred years.

"Lost my knife," said Marrow, in the voice of a man reporting weather.

"Where?" said Isolde.

"Bottom." He put the housing down on the quay, where it sat and dripped and was
exactly what it was. "It's shallow out there and there's a town under it. Twelve
houses, upside down. There's a ship down there with her bell still on the foredeck
and her name still on the transom."

"Whose?"

Marrow looked at the water, then at Isolde, then at the housing.

"There's a light down there," he said.
