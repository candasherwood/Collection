# Chapter Twenty

Every parish on the Reach has one thing it does not talk about, and Carrow's is the old
woman who walks the sea-wall.

There are four hundred people on the island and every one of them has an opinion about
her, and the opinions are all versions of the same word, which is *mad*, and *mad* is a
comfortable word because it means there is nothing to be done. A parish will feed a mad
woman. A parish will leave a loaf on the wall and a blanket in the boathouse and tell the
children not to go near her after dark. What a parish will not do is ask her a question,
because an answer is a thing that might require something.

Nobody in Carrow had asked Ede Calloway a question in eleven years. That is how a coast
stays safe and a woman stays alone, and it had never once occurred to anybody in that
parish that the two arrangements were the same arrangement.

---

The road ran both ways. Nobody had told Mira that, and it was the kind of thing a road
does not bother to mention.

They had been on it two nights and a day since Barrowfoot, and the second night had been
the worst of it, because the second night was when the bodies of the men on the *Vigil*
stopped being a story and became a thing she could see whenever she shut her eyes. Pell
had not spoken since four in the morning. He rowed when he was told and he coiled the
painter when he was told and he had the face of a boy doing sums about his own life and
not liking the total.

Sallow rowed. Nobody had asked him to and nobody had stopped him, and he was bad at it
for an hour and then adequate, and he had taken the middle oar so that he had his back to
the others, which Mira suspected was the only decency left in him and which she was not
going to thank him for.

Marrow was in the bow with a rope over his shoulder and the water still coming off him,
the way it had been coming off him since Barrowfoot, and he watched the road ahead the
way other men watched weather.

Rook sat with the ember-blade across his knees, and the blade was dark, and he had
stopped apologising for it.

The lens stood in its crutch amidships with the Ember in the brass cup and the canvas
chimney laced on, and it threw the road out ahead of them in a lane nine feet wide, and
under three feet of water the old stones came up gold, and the walls came up, and the
gateposts, and the roof of somebody's house with the chimney still on it.

And beside it, a hand's width away, the copy: perfect, unmarked, the road as it had been
before the sea took it, with no damage in it anywhere. Teo called the holes. That was his
job now and he did it with his hand flat on the gunwale, listening, saying *hole, twenty
feet, left* in a voice like a man reading a tide table, and he had not seen any of it with
his eyes since Candlewick.

At the eighth bell the wind came up out of the south-west and brought a ship with it.

She came down the coast with her yards square and her wake straight, close enough that
Mira could see the figures on the yards and the yards being braced by men who had been
dead for two days. She was making eight knots in a four-knot wind and she was not
rolling.

It was the *Vigil*.

"She's to be on the north rocks by five," said Pell, in a small voice. "The Admiral said.
I heard her say it at the gangway. She said it in front of everybody."

"She was on the north rocks at five," said Rook.

Nobody said anything for a while. The road ran on under the boat and the gold went out
ahead of them and the ship came down it, a mile and a half out, and passed them going
south, and not one of the fifty-six looked over the side.

"Six bells in a watch," said Pell. "Nobody makes six bells in a watch. I've been on the
water since I was nine and nobody makes six bells in a watch."

"She's got a crew," said Sallow, "and the crew has no opinions. You'd be amazed what a
ship does when nobody aboard wants anything."

"That isn't a comfort," said Pell.

"No," Sallow agreed. "It's a professional observation. I'm told they can be told apart."

Rook had stood up, which in an open boat meant the whole boat moved. "She's south of
Barrowfoot," he said. "She's south of the Cradle. She is standing down the coast with the
wind on her quarter, and there is nothing down the coast."

"She's going home," said Mira.

They all looked at her.

"That's my island," she said, and heard how strange the word sounded, as though she had
taken it out of a pocket and found it did not fit. "That's Carrow. There's nothing south
of Carrow but the Fall, and she isn't making for the Fall with her yards square in a
following wind. She's making nine miles off Carrow, and she's got a hundred and fifty miles
of dark water to do it in."

The Ash had been at Barrowfoot for two days. The Ash had walked into a town and up a
ladder and had not fought and had not run and had done the work. And now the Ash had a
ship and the ship was sailing south, and there was exactly one place on two thousand
miles of coast that it had never come ashore, and Voss had a page of sums about it, and
Voss had asked.

*What was your grandmother doing on the night the lights went out?*

"I don't know," Mira said, out loud, to the road.

"Don't know what?" said Teo.

"Something the Admiral asked me." She got the notebook out and did not open it. "Eleven
years, and it has never come ashore at Carrow. Not once. Not a boat, not a dog, not one
Hollowed fisherman. Voss has been losing ground everywhere else since the dark and she
has a page about the one stretch of coast that nothing has ever touched, and she thinks
it's a miracle, and she wanted to know what my grandmother was doing that night."

"And what was she doing?" said Pell.

"That's the thing," said Mira. "Nobody knows. Nobody in that parish has known since
before I could ask."

She looked at the ship. She counted the brass brackets along her side, forty of them,
empty, because that was the sort of sum her hands did on their own now, and she got
forty, and then she counted the windows aft and got one lit.

"Teo," she said. "How far to the Kindling?"

"From here? Two nights, maybe less, the current's with us going north. She's fast, that
road. That's what nobody understands about it." He said it easily and then stopped and
turned his head, because he had heard her voice do something. "North," he said. "You said
north."

"I did."

"Miss," said Pell.

"South," said Mira. "We're going south."

---

Rook did not shout. That was the thing about him; the more it cost, the more formal he
became, and by the end of the first minute he sounded like a writ.

"The Warden is at the Kindling," he said. "The eight lanterns are to be relit inside
eleven days and four of them are gone. The Admiral has a fleet and a writ and a company
of marines and she has already sailed. We were to be there before her, because a keeper
is not a soldier and the whole argument of the last four hundred years is that a light
lit by a writ is a fire in a box."

"Then she can wait," said Mira.

"She cannot wait."

"Then she'll do it without me," said Mira, "and she'll be right about most of it, and
people will die in the part she isn't right about, and I'll have been somewhere else. Say
it plainly, Ser. You've earned the right to say it plainly."

Rook looked at her for a moment.

"You are asking me to choose a grandmother over a coast," he said.

"No," said Mira. "I'm asking you to notice that nobody has ever chosen the grandmother.
That's the whole of it. Four hundred years of lanterns and every single one of them was
worth more than the woman standing on the wall, and the arithmetic always comes out the
same, and it comes out the same because nobody has ever once put the woman on the other
side of the ledger and looked at what it does." She heard her voice go flat and did not
stop it. "Voss asked me what my grandmother was doing. I said I didn't know. I have been
saying I didn't know since I was old enough to be told to stop, and I am going to go and
find out, and if the coast falls down while I'm doing it then the coast can send me a
bill."

The lane of light went on ahead of them. Somewhere behind, the ship that had been the
pride of the Salt Throne went south with her yards square.

"Aye," said Marrow.

That was all he said. He did not turn round and he did not make it a speech. He shifted
his rope from one shoulder to the other and picked up the second oar, and the boat swung
as he put it in the water, and the argument was over, because the strongest man aboard
had said one word and had said it in the direction they were going.

Rook sat down.

"I will come with you," he said, and then, because he was who he was: "I am not certain I
am right to."

"No," said Mira. "That's the part I'd be worried about if I were you."

Pell said, "Are we going home?"

"Some of us."

"I've never been to Carrow," said Pell. "I've been to the Fold and Candlewick and the
Gull's Teeth and I've been to Barrowfoot and that's the whole of my life, and I'm
sixteen." He wiped his nose on his wrist. "What's it like?"

Mira thought about it. She had a photograph of a face she could not remember, a notebook
in a hand she had been taught to imitate, and a gate she could still see with a fence her
grandmother had been smug about.

"Wet," she said. "There's a hill. The sea's on the left on the way to school."

"That's not very much."

"No," said Mira. "It isn't."

Sallow cleared his throat, which he did before saying anything he had already decided to
say.

"You are turning a boat around in the middle of a war," he said, "on the word of a feeling,
about a woman who may be dead, may be Hollowed, may be a hundred miles inland, and who —
if I may strike a professional note — has not sent you so much as a line in eleven years."

"She wrote me a notebook," said Mira.

"A notebook is not a letter. A notebook is a will with the dates left out."

"Mr Sallow," said Mira. "You've been paid six salt a week to write down what I do."

"I have."

"Then write this down. And row."

---

It was three nights south and it would have been two and a half north, and that difference
is the sort of thing that decides a war, because the current on that coast runs north and
the road is under it and a boat is a thing the water has an opinion about.

The second night the road began to be wrong in a new way.

North of Barrowfoot the damage was four hundred years old: a fallen wall, a silt bank, the
roof of a house, a lugger lying on her side with her name still on her. South of it, the
holes were young. Whole miles of road were gone, not eaten at the edges but *taken*, clean
out of the middle of the lane, so that the gold ended at nothing and picked up again forty
feet on, and between the two there was black water and the copy running across it,
beautiful, unbroken, going nowhere.

"The Ash has been through here," said Teo. "Not walking. Coming back."

"Coming back from where?"

"From Carrow. It gets in and it gets turned and it goes home along this road and it eats
what it leaned on." He had his hand flat on the gunwale and his head down. "That's not a
guess. Every hole is on the south side of a wall. It comes up the road with the wall on
its left and it puts its hand out and the wall isn't there after."

Mira looked at the black water where the road should have been, and at the copy going
across it in a lane of poured gold, and she said, "How long has it been doing that?"

"Miss," said Teo, "there's four hundred years of road here and about a night's worth of
holes. Whatever's been keeping it out, it's been keeping it out recently."

They came out of the deep water at dusk on the third day and the road climbed, and the
copy climbed with it, and ahead of them on the copy there was a light.

Mira did not understand what she was looking at for four or five seconds. It was a tower,
and the tower had a lamp room, and the lamp room was lit, and the light came out of it in
a bar nine miles long across the water, steady as a held breath.

"That's the Ninth," she said.

"The Ninth's dark," said Pell.

"That's the Ninth as it was." She had her hand out of the boat before she knew it. "The
copy's got the light in it. Of course it has. The copy doesn't show me last winter. It
shows the world before the Fall, and before the Fall that tower was lit, because it was
always lit."

It had kept all of it. The town came up on the copy as they came round the headland, and
it was whole and it was full: boats at the quay with their masts in a thicket, nets on the
walls, smoke standing up out of forty chimneys in the evening air, a woman in the square
with a basket and a man on the steps with a plane in his hand and a curl of wood going up
off it. Somebody had put washing out. There were children on the shingle, running the way
children run on shingle, badly and at speed.

Mira sat in the boat in the middle of all of it and felt nothing. That was the horror, and
it took her a while to find the word for it. It was not that she did not care. It was that
she had been here, and every part of her that had ever been here had been taken out and
filed somewhere, and what was left was a girl in a boat looking at a town the way you
look at a picture of a town.

"There's a school," said Pell, pointing. "That's a school, isn't it, with the bell on the
roof. Where's yours, miss?"

Mira looked at the building. It was low and white and had a bell on the roof and a step
worn down in the middle, and she could not have said one true thing about it.

"I don't know," she said.

"You must know. Everyone knows their school."

"I paid for it," said Mira, and heard how it sounded, and did not make it better. "In the
cockpit at Barrowfoot. I held a hatch for a hundred and eighty and it took the first three
years and the rhyme and the name of the family next door, and it did not take the gate,
because it never takes the thing you were using to hold on."

Pell did not say anything for a while.

"That's not fair," he said at last.

"No," said Mira.

Teo had gone quiet in the bow. He could not see any of it — not the tower, not the town,
not the washing — and he had his hand on the gunwale and his head turned toward her.

"You've stopped rowing," he said.

"I'm sorry."

"You've stopped rowing and you're looking at something and you're not saying what." He
waited. "Mira."

"There's a woman on the sea-wall," said Mira. "In the copy. She's got a lantern and she's
walking the wall, and she's walking it the way you walk a thing you've walked every night
of your life."

"Is it her?"

"No. It's four hundred years ago and a coat of the same colour, doing the same round on the
same stones." Mira put the oar back in the water. "I looked at her and waited to feel
something and there was nothing there, and I thought it was because of what I've paid, and
it isn't. It isn't even her."

She pulled, and the boat went on, and the copy slid past her with its washing out and its
children on the shingle and a woman on the wall who had been walking that round since
before there was a parish to walk it for.

---

They came in on the last of the light and put the boat on the shingle at the slip, and the
real town was dark.

That was the thing she had not prepared for. The copy had been so full and the real place
was so quiet: no light in any window, no lamp on the quay, one dog, four boats hauled up,
and the tower above them on its hill with its glass black as a closed eye. It smelled of
salt fish and tar and the particular cold smell of a place that has been deciding not to
light anything for eleven years.

But it was not dead. There was smoke. Somebody up the hill was cooking, and there were
nets mended on a wall, and a woman was standing in the door of the chandlery with a lamp
turned down low, and a boy of about twelve was on the steps with a bucket, and they all
looked at the boat coming in.

Mira got out into a foot of water and hauled the bow up and stood there with her boots
wet, and four hundred people did not come out to meet her. About nine did.

"Is that Ede's girl?" said the woman in the door.

"It's Mira Calloway," said Mira.

The woman came down the steps. She was sixty or so, in a man's jersey with the sleeves
rolled, and she had the flat unhurried face of somebody who had been the only sensible
person in a parish for a very long time.

"Ossa Tarn," she said. "I've got your grandmother's bread in my oven. It's the third loaf
this week and she'll not eat it, but I bake it, so." She looked past Mira at the boat, at
the lens, at the knight, at Marrow, and did not ask a single question, which was how Mira
knew she had been running the place. "You'll want the wall. She'll be on it. She's on it
from dusk to first light and she does not like to be talked to while she's on it."

"Mrs Tarn —"

"Ossa."

"Ossa. Who's been keeping the light?"

The woman looked at her.

"Nobody's kept the light," she said. "There's been no light since the winter. You'd know
that better than me."

"Then why has nothing come ashore here?"

The boy on the steps said, "Nothing comes here," in the voice of a boy saying a thing he
has heard said.

"Nothing at all? Not a boat? Not a—" Mira stopped. "The Ash has been on this coast since the dark. It's had the Fold's whole family standing at their posts, and nobody
noticed. It's been at Barrowfoot since Tuesday. There are fifty-six of them sailing a Crown
three-decker nine miles off your harbour and you're telling me nothing has ever come
ashore at Carrow."

"Nothing has ever come ashore at Carrow," said Ossa Tarn. "There's nothing here to come
for. We've got no light, we've got no road, we've got four hundred people and a hill." She
wiped her hands on her jersey. "It's luck, and I'll not have it said out loud in case it
hears me."

"Luck," said Mira.

"Luck, or the saint, or the tide," said Ossa. "And in this parish we don't ask. We leave
the bread out and we don't ask."

---

The sea-wall ran from the slip to the point, four hundred yards of grey stone with the
sea on one side and the grazing on the other, and the woman on it was the only thing
moving in the whole dark island.

She was small and she walked with her hands out a little from her sides, not touching the
wall, the way a person walks on ice. She had a man's coat on over two jerseys and a hat
that had been respectable in another decade, and she had a book under one arm and a stub
of pencil behind her ear, and there were matches in her pockets, so many that the coat
pulled at the shoulder, and every ninth or tenth step she would put her hand in and touch
them, all of them, one after another, the way another woman might count a rosary.

Mira stopped forty feet away.

"Miss Calloway," said Rook, quietly. "The edge."

She had felt it before he said it. That was the thing she would think about afterwards: it
was not that she saw it, it was that her body had already stopped, and her feet had
already chosen a distance, and the hair on her arms had done something it did at the top
of the tower.

It was warm, and not the stone: the air above it, and the water at its foot, and a line in
the water that the wind came up to and did not cross. There was no light. There was no
colour, no glow, no bar across the sea, nothing at all that a person who was not a keeper
would have been able to point at. It was a door in a dark room, and somebody's hand was on
the other side of it, and had been for eleven years.

Marrow walked past her, put his hand out, and stopped with his palm flat against nothing at
all.

"It's warm," he said.

"Don't lean on it," said the old woman, without turning round. "It's load-bearing."

Mira's mouth was dry.

"Ede Calloway," she said.

The old woman stopped. She stopped the way a clock stops, all at once, and then she turned
round and looked at the six of them standing on her wall in the dark, and her face was
exactly the face of somebody being interrupted at work.

"Evening," she said. "What's the date?"

Mira told her.

Ede Calloway nodded and took the book out from under her arm and opened it against her
chest and wrote the date down, and then, underneath, in a hand that was tidy at the top of
the page and getting worse as it went, she wrote something else. Mira could not see what.

"Who are you?" said Ede.

Mira got the notebook out of her coat and opened the back cover and took the photograph out
of its paper pocket, and held it up. It was a small thing, brown at the edges, a woman of
about fifty standing in front of a gate with her arms folded and her chin up and the light
behind her, and Mira had looked at it perhaps two thousand times in her life.

"Is this you?" she said.

Ede looked at the photograph for a long time. Then she looked up.

"It was," she said.

"Do you know me?"

"No," said Ede Calloway, without any cruelty at all. "But I have your name. Everybody's is
in here."

She turned the book round. It was a keeper's book, oiled cloth, the corners gone soft, and
it was full — not of sums, not of weather, not of the things keepers write — but of names.
Columns of them, in a hand that started each morning neat and ended each night worse; the
same forty or fifty names coming round and round, page after page, with the dates beside
them and small marks against some of them, and here and there a page where the hand had
stopped in the middle of a word.

There was a name at the top of the page for that day in a much better hand than the rest.

*Mira Calloway.*

"That's my name," said Mira.

"Is it?" said Ede, with mild professional interest, the way she had asked the date. "Then
you're at the top of today. You're always at the top of today. I don't know why."

"I don't —" Mira stopped. "You don't know me. But you've written me down."

"I write the names every morning," said Ede. "If I can say them all, I've not lost anybody
since Tuesday. That's the arrangement. It's not a good arrangement, but it's the one the
light offers." She looked at the page, and then at Mira, and then at the page again, as
though checking that both of them were still there.

Mira stood on the sea-wall at Carrow with a photograph in one hand and her own name in the
other. Her grandmother was standing in front of her, five feet tall, with matches in her
pockets, having written her name down every morning for eleven years without knowing whose
it was.

"Then I'll introduce myself again," said Mira.

Ede frowned. "Say that again."

"Then I'll introduce myself again."

"That's an old thing," said Ede Calloway. "My mother said that."

"Mira," said Mira. "Mira Calloway. Keeper of the Ninth, and your granddaughter, and you
taught me to count the stairs."

Ede wrote it down. She wrote it slowly and she said each word as she wrote it, and when she
had finished she read it back, and then she looked up and said, "Say it again."

"Mira Calloway."

"I have it," said Ede, and closed the book, and put the pencil behind her ear. "It's a good
name. It's got a count in it."

---

They sat on the wall while the last of the light went out of the west, and Ede Calloway
told them what she had been doing for eleven years, in the flat listing voice of a woman
reading out a job she had got used to.

"You'll have seen the edge. You're a keeper or you'd not have stopped." She had her hands
flat on the stone. "It's a ring. Not a lane, not a bar, not a light in a tower. A ring, all
the way round the island, at the waterline, and it has no ends, and that is the whole of
it. An edge you can walk round. A ring you cannot. They come up the road and they come to
the wall of it and they go along it looking for a way past, and there isn't one, so they go
away. It takes them about a night. It's been doing that for eleven years."

"Why doesn't it see it?"

"Because I don't make it bright," said Ede. "That's the trick and it took me four years to
learn it. If it were bright they'd come and stand and look at it and work out where it
ends. There's no glass in it and no flame in it and no tower. It's only what I can hold,
and what I can hold has no colour in it, and so they walk up to it in the dark and find a
door they cannot see and they do not learn anything and they go home."

"And the ring?" said Mira. "The one on your hand."

Ede looked down at it as though it had been mentioned by somebody else. It was a keeper's
ring, green-black, and it was dead: no seam of light in it, no warmth, nothing but a hoop
of glass worn thin on the inside where a hand had turned it for fifty years.

"It's no use to me," she said. "A ring is a hand on a lever. There's no lever, and I've
nothing to put through it. I hold it with my hands and my back and my knees and the fact
that I have got up every night. The ring is a—" she turned it with her thumb "—the ring is
a thing I wear so I remember what I am."

"What does it cost?"

"A season," said Ede. "One a year. Not a name, not a face — a season. The weather and the
food and the people in it. I've had eleven of them and I couldn't tell you one. I know
that it was winter when the light went out because I wrote it down, and I know it was
snowing because the page says it was snowing, and I have no idea what snowing was like."

"Eleven seasons," said Mira.

"Eleven," said Ede. "And no more to spend. I've been spending the ones I had."

She said it the way she had said the date, and it went through Mira like a cold wire,
because a keeper could do that sum too. The light did not take a year. It took a season out
of a year, and a woman of seventy-one had a limited number of seasons in her, and there was
nothing in the arithmetic that cared.

"Why?" said Mira. "Why the ring and not the tower? You had a lens. You had a wick. You had
a whole tower with a hundred and twelve steps and it was the best light on the Reach."

Ede Calloway was quiet for a moment.

"Because the tower is a door," she said, "and the light's the handle, and eleven years ago I
was told to take the handle off." She turned a page of the book with one finger. "Here.
It's in my hand and I don't remember writing it, and I've read it four hundred times, and
every time I read it I think, well, she had a reason, she must have had a reason."

She turned the book round and set it on Mira's knees, and pointed at the page with a
fingernail that had been broken and mended and broken again.

*Third of the eleventh. Snow. I have put it out.*

*The woman with the bad hand says the rest of the coast goes dark this week. She says the
light is a door and they follow it to the door, and that the dark will be a harbour, and
that it will come here with the whole of the Fall behind it and that a lit tower on this
island is an invitation with my name on it.*

*I asked her what becomes of the child. She said: nothing, if the door is shut.*

*I have put it out. I am to write down that I did it of my own will, and I did, and the
will was to have her live. If this is the last true thing in the book, it is the only one
I would say again.*

Mira read it twice. The second time she read it out loud, because Ede was watching her mouth
and because it seemed to be what was wanted, and when she got to *the child* Ede nodded
slightly, as though a thing she had suspected had been confirmed.

"The woman with the bad hand," said Mira.

"Scarred," said Ede. "The back of it. Shiny, like a—" she made a gesture "—like a candle
that's been let go out on a table."

"Voss," said Mira.

"Was that her name? She had a ship and she stood in my kitchen and she did not sit down, and
I have her receipt in the tin with the matches." Ede put her hand in her pocket and touched
the matches, all of them, one after another. "I keep the important things with the matches
because I always know where the matches are."

Rook had not moved for some time. He was standing at the end of the wall with his arms at
his sides and the dead blade on his hip, and his surcoat was grey in the dark, and he said,
"Ma'am. In the eleventh year, the order received a writ for the Ninth. It was never served.
The Warden's clerk has it in a box and has been writing *pending* on it every quarter for
eleven years."

"Then somebody in your order was kind," said Ede.

"Somebody in my order was a coward," said Rook, "and I have been one of them for eleven
years, and last week I stood on a quarterdeck and watched a licence read out to a
woman who would not sign it, and I said nothing, because it was correct." He looked at her.
"You are one of the ones who put the lights out."

"Yes," said Ede.

Rook stood there with nothing in his hands. Then he said, "What can I do that is not a
writ?"

"You're a knight," said Ede.

"I am."

"There's a bucket in the boathouse and the pump's gone, so it's the well at the top of the
green, and it's ninety steps each way." She said it as flatly as she had said the date.
"I fetch it at first light. I don't like to leave the wall before first light."

"Then I will carry the water," said Rook.

"Every night?"

"Every night."

Ede Calloway looked at him for a moment, and then nodded once, and that was the end of the
largest thing that had happened on that wall in years, and it took no forgiveness at
all.

---

Mira asked the question at the very end, when the light was gone and the sea had gone the
colour of a wet slate and the wall was a line of grey with nothing on it.

"Did you have a granddaughter?"

Ede Calloway did not answer for a while.

"There was a child in the house," she said.

"Yes."

"I wrote her down every morning and I could not tell you what she looked like." She turned
the ring on her finger. "I have a photograph somewhere. It's a girl in a coat."

"That's me."

"Is it?" said Ede, and looked at her, and looked, and then said, politely, the way a woman
says a thing at a door: "I'm sorry. I'm no good at this in the evening. Ask me in the
morning, when I've said the names."

And Mira, who had spent years being angry at a dead woman for leaving her a notebook
and no face, said, "All right," and found that it was.

---

At full dark Ede Calloway took up her position.

There was nothing ceremonial about it. She walked to the middle of the wall where the stone
was worn into a shallow dish by eleven years of standing, and she set her feet, and she put
both hands out flat, and she breathed out once, and the ring went up.

Mira felt it go. That was the thing she would try to write down and fail: a keeper is not
supposed to be able to feel another keeper's Holding. But she felt it, the way you feel a
door close in a house when you thought you were the only one awake — a long soft soundless
shutting, all round the island at once, four miles of it, at the waterline, and the sea
outside it went on being the sea and the sea inside it lay down and put its head on its
paws.

Marrow took his hand off the stone and stepped back.

Ede did not move. She stood with her hands out and her feet set and her eyes open, and she
was not doing anything that anybody could have seen, and she was holding four miles of
edge with her two hands, and it did not shine.

Mira stood beside her and put her hand flat on the wall next to Ede's, the way the Admiral
had put her hand on the sill next to her brother's.

Then she looked up, because Pell had made a sound.

"There's a light," he said.

Nine miles out, on the black water, one window was lit.

It was a small warm square of it, low down aft, and it moved very slightly with the swell,
and behind it there was a hull with forty empty brass brackets along her side and fifty-six
people aboard her who had died at Barrowfoot and were perfectly capable of sailing a ship.

"Ede," said Mira. "That's a ship."

"Whose?"

"The Crown's. The Admiral's. She took it out of Barrowfoot with the Ash's people aboard and
not one of her own, and put it on the north rocks at five, and somebody sailed it off."
Mira's voice was steady and she was proud of it. "There are fifty-six of them. They're not
coming for us. They're going to work."

Ede Calloway did not take her hands off the wall.

"They don't come by ship," she said.

"They do now."

The old woman was quiet for a moment. Out on the water the window went on being lit, and
below it, along the ship's side, small shapes were moving on the booms, and Mira counted
them, because she counted things, and got four boats being got ready.

"Then I have been holding the wrong door," said Ede.

"Can you hold against a ship?"

"I can hold against a road," said Ede. "A road comes at you in one place. A ship comes at
you wherever it likes and it does not have to be invited. It can lie out there for a week
and put a boat down every night and I will be standing here in the same spot, getting
older."

"How long can you hold?"

"Tonight," said Ede.

"And tomorrow?"

Ede Calloway turned her head and looked at her granddaughter for the first time with
something that was not politeness.

"Tomorrow," she said, "I would like to be asked in the morning, when I've said the names."

The window stayed lit. The boats stayed on the booms. The sea inside the ring lay down and
went quiet, and the wind came up out of the south-west and went round the island the way
water goes round a stone, and four hundred people slept on a hill that had never once been
touched, and did not know why.

Mira got her notebook out and wrote it up sitting on the wall with the pencil, in the dark,
by feel.

*Carrow is dark and the parish is alive and nobody here knows why.*

*My grandmother has been standing on this wall every night for eleven years, holding a ring
round the whole island with no light in it at all. Nothing can study what it cannot see, so
the Ash comes up the road, finds a door in the dark, and goes away, and has been going away
ever since.*

*The cost is a season a year and she has spent eleven and cannot tell me one of them. She
asks the date and writes it down. She writes our names every morning so that she will know
who she has lost. Mine is at the top of the page and she has been writing it every day for
eleven years and could not tell me my face.*

*Voss asked me what my grandmother was doing that night. She was doing exactly what Voss
asked her to do, and she has been paying for it a season at a time ever since, and Voss has
never once in eleven years come to look at the woman standing on the wall.*

*Rook said he would carry the water. That is the whole of the absolution he offered and the
whole of the one she took.*

*The Ash has come south with a ship and it is lying nine miles out with one window lit, and
it is not going to fight her. It is going to wait, because it has learned that it can, and
she has spent eleven and there is not one season left in her.*

*Cost, today: nothing. I held nothing and the light charged me nothing, and it is the first
day since Sixthmonth that I have not paid for something, and I would rather have paid.*

*Dusk. She was already at the wall.*
