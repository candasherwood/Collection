# Chapter Twenty-Six

The stair had been cold since the afternoon before and it was already the coldest place on the
Reach.

That was the thing nobody had expected. Mira went down to the cellar door on Saturday morning with
a mug of tea in her hands and the cold came up the steps to meet her like water: not the cold of
stone in winter, which is a patient cold, but a *moving* cold, the way a room is cold when a
window has been open all night and something has been standing in it.

The red blade stood in the door frame where Cray had left it. It had no hand on it and no wick and
no oil, and it burned flat and steady, and it lit the top six steps in a colour like a forge seen
through a crack.

"Don't go past the third step," said Odo Pike.

"I'm not going past the third step."

"You're standing on the second."

Mira looked down. She was standing on the second.

"Rill went to the twenty-second an hour ago," said Odo Pike. "He came back up and asked me why
nobody had told him it was Thursday. He'd lost the morning. Not the memory of it — the *morning*:
he doesn't know he's had one. He's sitting in the room with a cup in his hands and he keeps
looking at the window and saying it's early."

"Is he all right?"

"He's a guide with four hundred miles of road in his head and an hour missing out of Friday,"
said Odo Pike. "He's as all right as any of us. The stair isn't. Twenty steps and it takes an
hour; three hundred steps and it'll take a year off a man before he's halfway, and it doesn't stop
at the bottom, it goes on down the road. There's a red light in the only door on this coast that
goes under the world, and until it's out, the deep road is shut, and the drowned can stand at the
top of it with their bands on and look at nothing."

Behind them, in the cellar, eleven people were standing around a table with a map of the road on
the wall in three colours and none of them were talking. Four of them had been emptied in the yard
and had nothing behind their eyes but the names on their cups, and one of them was Hask, who had
lost the deep road and sat with his band going round his wrist and his hand flat on the table as
if he were waiting to be told what it was for.

---

The Warden came down at nine with his study coat still on and stood at the third step and looked
at the blade for a long time.

"Can it be pulled out with a pole?" said Hale.

"It can be pulled out with a pole," said Halloran. "And then it will be a lit red blade lying on
the floor of my cellar, and the cold will come off it in a circle nine feet across, and we will
have gained nothing at all and lost a pole." He came back up two steps. "It is not a fire. You
cannot smother a fire that has nothing to burn. It is a season someone took, and it is spending
itself slowly, and it will spend itself for six nights and then it will go out on its own, on the
ninth night, at the same hour as everything else on this coast."

"Then we wait," said Hale.

"We cannot wait," said Halloran. "The deep road is shut and the guides are the only people on the
Reach who can walk under the Fall, and in six nights the Brine Mother collects and the Crown's
term runs out and a man with a list comes back for my forge. We have six nights and one road and
a red light standing in it."

"You have four hundred knights," said Mira.

"I have four hundred knights and one of them has a blade that lights." The Warden looked at Rook,
who had come down the stair behind him without a word and was standing at the back with his hands
at his sides. "The rest have swords. A sword is iron. Iron is a very good thing to have in a fight
and it is no use at all against a season."

Rook did not say anything.

"Say it," said Halloran.

"A blade of given light can put out a blade of taken light," said Rook.

"Yes."

"And it has been done once."

"Once that I know of," said Halloran, "and the man who did it was never the same, and I have been
standing in this cellar since six o'clock this morning working out what that sentence means,
because Cray said it to us on purpose and Cray is not a man who says things by accident."

"It means he lost something," said Mira.

"It means he lost something he could not get back," said Halloran, "and he lost it by putting a
given thing against a taken thing, and the taken thing had to give back everything it had ever
taken before it would go out." He looked at the red bar in the door. "That is what is in there.
Every hour that blade has ever taken off anybody is still inside it, and it is not gone, it is
*held*. Put a blade of given light against it and it will let go all at once, and the man holding
the given blade will be standing in the middle of it."

The cellar was very quiet.

"What's in it?" said Mira.

"Yesterday, for a start," said Halloran. "A lieutenant's morning. A forge-hand's barrow. A
guide's arm. Four drowned men's road, and nineteen years of whatever that man has been doing in
the machine-country, and Harrow, which I have never seen and have heard described exactly once by
a man who was crying." He put his hands behind his back. "And whatever else a red blade takes that
nobody writes down. The hour a man cannot remember having. The year. The name."

"And it gives it back?" said Rook.

"No," said Halloran. "It *lets go*, which is not the same. It will not go back where it came from.
It will go through the man who is standing in front of it."

---

Rook asked for the stair at noon, and the Warden said no.

He asked again at two, in the study, with the door shut and the ledger open on the desk between
them, and the Warden said no again and this time he said it at length.

"I have four hundred knights," said Halloran, "and eleven of them are worth a damn, and one of
them is a man who has been carrying my order on his back since he was fourteen because he was
told to and because it was the only shape he had. You are the last honest knight in this
fortress. If I spend you on a door, I have a door and no knight, and I have to explain to a
chapter why I spent the only man who could still say the vows and mean them."

"Then don't explain it," said Rook.

"I am not going to give you an order," said Halloran.

"I didn't ask for one."

"You asked for the stair."

"Yes," said Rook. "I asked for the stair. I have been asking for things all my life. I asked to
be a novice at fourteen and they gave me the Long Night. I asked to be sent to Carrow and they
sent me to put out a lamp. I asked what happened at Brinehollow and nobody told me for nine
years, and when they did tell me it was in a citation." He put his hands flat on the desk. "So
this is not a request. I am telling you what I am going to do, and I am telling you because you
are the only man in this fortress I would bother telling, and because I would like you to be
there."

Halloran looked at him for a long time.

"If I give you an order," he said at last, "it will not light. You know that. Your blade has never
once come up for a thing you were told to do. It comes up when you mean it, and a man cannot mean
an order, he can only obey one." He closed the ledger. "That is the whole joke of my life, Ser
Rook. I have spent thirty years giving good men orders and wondering why the light was always
somewhere else."

"Then don't give me one," said Rook.

"I won't," said Halloran. "And I will not forbid you either, for the same reason, and I want you
to notice that I have thought about it, and that it took me four hours to find the one thing I
could do that was not an order." He stood up. "Take the guides. Take the keeper. Take the whole
cellar if it helps. Be down there at dusk, because the blade is weakest when the light is going
and the man who does this should not do it at noon in front of a crowd."

"It's not a crowd," said Rook. "It's a door."

"Yes," said Halloran. "That is exactly what I am afraid of."

---

At three Mira went to the archive, because there was one thing in the fortress nobody had said
out loud yet, and she wanted to know it before dusk.

The archive is two rooms over the scriptorium with a stove in it and a smell of glue, and a lay
brother called Wick who had been copying the same ledger for nine years and had opinions about
ink. He did not want to let her in. She told him the Warden had sent her, which was a lie, and the
lie sat on her tongue like a stone, and he let her in anyway.

The annals of the Emberwatch are four hundred years long and nobody has ever read them, because
they are four hundred years of attendance, and attendance is the least interesting virtue there
is. But there is an index, because the order indexes everything, and the index has a heading for
*blades*, and under *blades* there is a subheading in a different, older hand that says:

*Taken light, put out by given. Once.*

It was one leaf. One leaf in four hundred years.

*In the year of the ninth chapter, at Ostrey on the north road, Ser Aldous Mere of this house met
a company of the Assize and put out a blade of taken light with his own. The blade went dark and
never lit again. Ser Aldous was three days in a fever and came out of it unable to say the vows,
and the chapter released him from them, and he lived nineteen years as a lay brother in this
house, and was much loved, and would not be called ser.*

And then, underneath, in a hand that was not the annalist's, small and steady and a little wild at
the ends of the letters:

*It does not fight. It opens. Everything the red thing was holding comes through the man who
holds the light, and it does not stop coming, and there is no way to take it in order. I have been
in a room with four hundred people in it and I have never been so alone. If a man does this he
should have somebody with him who will not ask him questions after. I did not. I have written this
for the next one.*

*— A. Mere, lay brother, in his own hand, at the age of sixty-one.*

Mira read it twice and then sat on the stool by the stove for a while with her hands in her coat.

"Brother Wick," she said. "Has anybody ever asked for this leaf?"

"Twice in nine years," said Brother Wick. "The Warden, in the spring. And a young knight with a
bad shoulder, yesterday evening, who read it standing up and put it back and said thank you and
went out and was sick in the yard."

"Did he say anything?"

"He said, *he did it alone*," said Brother Wick. "And then he said, *that's the part I can fix.*"

---

Mira found him in the armoury at four, sitting on a bench with the blade across his knees and
the whetstone in his hand, not sharpening it.

The armoury of the Kindling is a museum with better locks: old blades on the wall, newer
paperwork in the drawers, dust on the crusade nobody went on. There was a helmet on a shelf with
a dent in it from a war that had ended before his grandmother was born, and a rack of spears that
nobody had touched since the Crown took the towers, and a smell of oil and cold iron and the
particular sadness of a room full of things that used to matter.

"You're not sharpening it," said Mira.

"It's sharp," said Rook. "It's been sharp for eleven years. It's the only thing about me that has
never needed anything."

She sat down on the bench, not too close.

"Tell me what it costs," she said. "Not the poem. The arithmetic."

Rook put the whetstone down.

"An ember-blade is a thread of Ember in the fuller and it lights when I mean it," he said. "It is
not a light. It is a *statement*. When I draw it and the gold comes up, the blade is saying what I
am, out loud, in front of everybody, and I cannot lie to it." He turned it over. "I have drawn
this blade four hundred times and it has come up three hundred and ninety-six. The four times it
did not, I was doing something I had been told to do and did not believe in, or saying something
kind that was not true.

"Now. A red blade is a season somebody took. If I put mine against it, mine is the given thing and
it will hold, and the taken thing will have to let go of everything it is holding, all at once.
And it will let go through me, because I am the only thing standing there with a light in my hand."
He looked at the blade. "Cray said the man who did it was never the same. I have been trying to
work out what that means and I think it means exactly what it sounds like. It means he was
standing in a room where every stolen hour came back at once, and he was the only thing in the
room it could go through, and after that he had heard so much that he could not go back to being
a man who had only heard his own."

"And that's the price," said Mira.

"That's the price," said Rook.

"Then don't."

He looked up.

"You are the third person today," said Rook, "after the Warden and the Marshal, and you are the
only one who has said it plainly, and I am going to tell you why it does not work." He put the
blade down on the bench between them. "At Wren's Fold there was a woman on fire in a tower and I
stood in the yard with this sword in my hand and did nothing, because I had been sent to bring
back a piece of paper. At Barrowfoot there were fifty-six people walking up the chains of a ship
and I did nothing, because the order was to hold the boat. At Brinehollow I was nineteen and I
held a door for a town and the door did not hold, and I have been decorated for it, and the
citation is in a drawer in this room." He said it without any heat at all, which was worse. "I
have been obedient for twelve years. It has cost other people everything and me nothing. So there
is a door downstairs with a red light in it and for once in my life the thing that is needed and
the thing I want are the same thing, and I am not going to be talked out of it by three people who
love me."

Mira looked at the dead crusade on the wall.

"I wasn't going to talk you out of it," she said. "I was going to ask whether you wanted me to hold
the band."

Rook was quiet for a moment.

"Yes," he said.

---

They went down at dusk: Rook, and Mira, and Odo Pike, and six guides with their bands covered, and
Marrow because the deep road was Marrow's business now, and the Warden, who came last and stood on
the third step and did not go any further, because a man of seventy-eight on a stair in the cold is
a liability and knows it.

The red blade was brighter at dusk. That was the thing that made Mira's skin crawl: it had been
the same flat steady bar all day and now, with the light going out of the world above, it looked
like the only lit thing on the Reach.

"Don't go past me," said Rook.

"Don't be stupid," said Mira. "You'll be standing in it."

"I'll be standing in it," said Rook. "You'll be three steps up with a band on your wrist and your
hand on my shoulder, and when it lets go you will see it, because a band shows the road and this
thing is standing on one." He turned round. "And when it's done, if I'm not — if I'm sitting down
and looking at my hands, do not ask me my name. Ask me in the morning."

"Why?"

"Because I would like to know whether I still have one," said Rook, and knelt down on the step.

He put the blade in front of him, point down, both hands on the hilt, the way a knight does when
he is going to say something.

The Warden's voice came down from the top of the stair.

"Ser Cadan Rook."

"Father."

"Three questions," said Halloran, "and you do not have to answer them, and I am asking them
because they are the only thing my order has that is worth anything, and because if you are going
to go down there and be a knight I would like it to be on the record that you did it in the old
way and not in the Crown's." He cleared his throat. "Will you keep what is not yours?"

Rook did not answer.

The silence went on for a while.

"Will you go where you are sent?"

Rook's hands were flat on the pommel and his head was down.

"Will you be seen?"

"I will," said Rook, and the blade lit.

It came up gold, and it was not the coaxing candle Mira had seen before: it came up all along the
fuller in one rush, and the stair filled with the smell of hot iron and thunder, and the red bar in
the door frame leaned towards it like a weathercock in a wind.

Then Rook put the gold into the red.

---

Mira saw it through the band and she wrote it down afterwards four times and never got it right,
and the version in the notebook is the shortest one because it was the only one she could bear to
read back.

The two lights did not fight. That was the first thing. There was no clash and no sparks and no
sound of swords. The gold went into the red and stayed there, and the red went *pale*, the way a
bruise goes pale, and then it began to give things back.

They came out of it in no order at all.

— a woman at a window saying *mind the step, it's the loose one* —

— a man counting barrels on a quay, *eleven, twelve, thirteen*, and somebody laughing at a dog —

— a boy in a boat saying *I'll be back before the tide* to a woman with grey in her hair, and the
woman saying *you won't* —

— a lieutenant in a doorway saying *the Admiral's compliments. She would like a word on the
quarterdeck at six* —

— an old man in a lamp room reading a log out loud in a flat careful voice —

— a village at a place called Harrow, and a great many people, and a sound like weather —

Rook was on his knees with both hands on the hilt and his head down, and Mira had her hand on his
shoulder with the band going round her wrist, and she could feel it going through him. Not pain.
Something worse than pain: a man being *told*. Every one of those hours was going through him the
way water goes through a net, and he could not turn any of it away, and the sword was the only
thing holding him up.

Then the last of it came.

— *Ser Rook. Ser Rook, the water's coming up the second floor* —

And Rook's own voice, nineteen years old, saying: *Hold the door. Hold the door. Hold it—*

And then the sound of it not holding. Four hundred people, and water, and a town going under in
the dark, and a boy of nineteen on a hill above it with his hands over his ears, and the citation
that would be written for him on the Monday.

Rook made a sound Mira had never heard a man make.

And the red went out.

---

The stair was dark and quiet and cold, and the cold was going out of it, and it went out the way
a tide goes out: slowly, and then all at once, and then there was only stone.

Rook was sitting on the step with the sword across his knees.

The blade was not lit. He had both hands on it and his eyes open and he was looking at it, and
Mira could see that he was waiting for it to do the thing it had done four hundred times, the way
a man waits for a clock to strike.

It did not strike.

"Rook."

He looked up. His face was the same face. That was the horrible part.

"How long was I down there?" he said.

"Four minutes."

"It was longer than that."

"Yes," said Mira. "It was."

Odo Pike came down the steps with a lantern and put it on the stone, and the guides came past them
with their bands going round and their hands out, and one of them said, "It's clear. The stair's
clear," and another one said, "It's clear to the shelf," and then they were moving, and Marrow went
past with his hand on the wall and his band showing him a road that had been shut for fourteen
hours and was open again.

Halloran came down last, slowly, one hand on the rail.

He stopped in front of Rook and looked at the sword.

"Put it up," he said.

"It won't light."

"I know."

"It's never not lit," said Rook, and his voice did something at the end of the sentence that Mira
would hear again in her life and did not want to.

"I know," said Halloran.

He knelt down, which took him a while and cost him something in the knees, and he put his hand on
the dead blade.

"Ser Cadan Rook," he said. "Will you keep what is not yours?"

Rook opened his mouth.

And nothing came out.

He sat there with his hands on the sword and his mouth open and the answer he had given at
fourteen and at nineteen and at every chapter since standing in the front of his head, and he
could not say it, because it was not true any more, and a knight of the watch cannot say a thing
that is not true in front of a witness.

"Will you go where you are sent?"

Rook shut his mouth.

"Will you be seen?"

"I don't know," said Rook.

The stair was quiet.

"Good," said Halloran.

Rook looked up.

"I have been asking those questions for forty years," said the Warden, "and I have heard *I will*
from six hundred men, and about nine of them meant it, and not one of them had the sense to say
*don't know* when it was the true answer. You are not less of a knight for it. You are the first
one I have confirmed twice." He got up, with a hand on the rail. "You will not say the vows again
until you can say them and mean them, and until then you will not wear the surcoat, and you will
keep the sword, because a man who has spent his certainty on a door is entitled to keep the iron
he did it with."

"And my place in the order?"

"Your place in the order," said Halloran, "is the same as it was this morning, which is that there
is a chair at my table and nobody else sits in it, and if the chapter does not like it they can
elect a Warden who does." He started up the steps. "Come up. There is tea, and there is a boy on a
bollard who has been asking about you since noon, and I have a door to bar with iron, because the
drum is cut and my ninth pin holds nothing now."

---

They barred the door that night with a beam of oak and two iron straps and a bell on a chain, so
that if anything came up the road in the dark it would ring, and the guides took the first watch,
and the drowned took the second, and Hask sat at the top of the stair with his band on his wrist
and his hand flat on the stone and nothing behind his eyes.

Toby Larch was in the guides' room with a cup of tea he was not drinking.

"He's not going to be all right, is he," said Toby.

"He's going to be something," said Mira. "I don't know what yet."

"The Ordinator says a man who does that never comes back the same." Toby looked at the sword on
the table, the dead one, with the thread dull in the fuller. "He said it like it was a fact. Like
the tide." He turned his own blade over on his knees. It was grey. "Is that what he meant? That
you go in and you come out and you're not the same person, so you might as well not be?"

"No," said Mira. "I think he meant that you come out knowing something you can't un-know, and
that's not the same as being dead. It's the same as being told."

Toby was quiet for a while. Then he said, "What did he get told?"

Mira looked at Rook, who was sitting by the fire with a blanket round his shoulders and the dead
sword across his knees and a cup in his hands, and who had not said a word for two hours, and who
was, she thought, listening to something.

"Everything," she said. "All at once. Every hour that blade ever took, and one of them was his."

Toby put his hand on his own hilt.

And the teal came up. Not bright — a thread of green-blue in the fuller, dim as a lamp three rooms
away — but it came up, and it stayed up, and it did not go out when he took his hand away.

Toby stared at it.

"What did I do?"

"You listened," said Mira.

---

Rook came and stood by her at the door of the guides' room at midnight, with the blanket still
round him and the sword under his arm, and looked down the corridor at the barred door and the
bell on its chain.

"I can hear it," he said.

"Hear what?"

"Nothing. That's the thing." He shifted the sword. "I keep waiting for it to light and it doesn't,
and it's not the not-lighting that's wrong. It's that I used to know what I was. I had three
answers and a coat and a rule about the dark and a man in a study who signed things, and now I've
got a dead sword and a chair at a table and a Warden who won't let me say the vows, and I find I am
not frightened of any of it." He looked at her. "That's what it took. Not the pain. The pain went
through in four minutes. It took the thing I was standing on."

"What are you standing on now?"

Rook thought about it for a long time.

"I don't know," he said. "But I said it out loud in a stair in front of you and the Warden, and I
didn't die of it, so I expect I'll find out."

---

Mira wrote it up at two in the morning, at the long table, with the bell on the door behind her.

*Saturday. The red blade took the morning off Rill in twenty steps. The stair was shut for
a day and the deep road with it, and the guides sat in the cellar with their bands on and
nothing to walk.*

*The Warden would not order it and would not forbid it, and he was right about why: a blade of
given light comes up when the man means it, and a man cannot mean an order. So Rook asked for the
stair three times, and was refused three times, and went down anyway, which is the first
disobedience of his life and the only thing he has ever done that lit the sword.*

*The three questions, on the stair, with the whole cellar listening: will you keep what is not
yours; will you go where you are sent; will you be seen. He could not answer the first two and he
answered the third, and the blade came up gold all along the fuller like a struck match the size of
a man.*

*Then the red gave everything back. I saw it through the band and I have written it four times and
this is the shortest and the only one I can read. A woman at a window. A man counting barrels. A
boy in a boat saying he'll be back before the tide. Lieutenant Airey saying the Admiral's
compliments. An old man in a lamp room reading a log out loud. A village at Harrow. And then
Brinehollow, and Rook at nineteen on a hill saying hold the door, and the door not holding, and
four hundred people, and the citation that was written for him on the Monday.*

*It took four minutes. He has not said a word about it and I do not think he will, and I have
decided that is allowed.*

*The sword is dead. It will not light. He keeps it because the Warden says a man who spends his
certainty on a door is entitled to keep the iron, and because a knight with a dead sword is still
a knight, and because — this is my own guess and I am writing it down as a guess — because he
would rather carry the thing that failed than be given a new one that might work.*

*He could not say the vows. The Warden asked him the three questions in the stair and he could not
answer, and Halloran said good, and confirmed him twice, and told him he would not wear the
surcoat until he could say them and mean them. I have never seen a man look so much like a man
being let out of something.*

*Toby's blade came up tonight. Dim. It stayed up when he took his hand away and it was still up
when I came in here. He asked me what he did and I said he listened, and I do not know whether
that is true, and it is the truest thing I have said all day.*

*Cost, today: a knight's certainty, four minutes of a stair, the whole of Friday morning out of
Rill, and a blade that will never light again.*

*The door is barred with oak and iron and a bell, the drum is cut, the ninth pin holds nothing,
and there are six nights left.*
