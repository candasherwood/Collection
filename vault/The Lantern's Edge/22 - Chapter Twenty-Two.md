# Chapter Twenty-Two

The road goes under the Kindling, which is a sentence you can say in four seconds and walk
for an hour.

It comes up out of the water at the foot of the rock, and it does not stop at the foot of the
rock, because the men who built it were not interested in the sea. It turns inland, into a
cut thirty feet deep with walls on both sides, and then it goes into the hill, and the gold
of the lane runs up a stair.

Three hundred steps. Mira counted them because she was holding the light and had nothing else
to do with her mouth, and because if she stopped counting she would have to think about the
thing behind them, which was nine miles out with one window lit and had been nine miles out
with one window lit for three nights.

"Two hundred and forty," said Pell. "Miss. Two hundred and forty."

"Two hundred and forty-one."

"It's a stair under a mountain."

"It's a stair under a fortress," said Rook. "The Kindling is a forge. The forge is built on a
rock and the road was here first, and four hundred years ago somebody decided that a road
running under your fortress was either the best thing in the world or the worst."

"Which is it?"

"Both," said Rook. "That is generally why they keep the door locked."

The stair was dry. That was the eerie part, after three nights of walking in three feet of
water: the gold lay on stone that was stone, and the walls were cut and dressed and the same
width apart the whole way up, and there were niches in them at intervals with nothing in them
any more, and the air got warmer and smelled of coal and hot iron and, underneath, of bread.

At the top there was a door.

It was iron, and it was old, and it had a lock on it that was not a lock any of them had ever
seen: a drum of brass the size of a dinner plate with nine slots cut in the rim, and eight of
the slots had a pin in them and the ninth did not.

"Nine locks," said Sallow, with professional interest. "One for each lantern. My word. That's
not a door, that's a committee."

The door opened before anybody knocked on it.

The woman behind it was sixty or so and dressed like a forge-hand, and she had a band on her
left wrist: a hoop of green-black glass, two fingers wide, and it was lit.

Not lit like a lamp. There was no flame in it and no beam and it did not light the room. It
shimmered — a slow green movement inside the glass, the way oil moves on water, going round
and round the band — and the moment Mira's ring came within six feet of it, the shimmer
turned towards her.

Every hair on her arm stood up.

"You're the light," said the woman. "We've been walking behind you for two nights. You're
late."

---

The Kindling at dawn is a black rock with a furnace in it.

That is not a figure of speech either. The rock comes out of the sea in one piece and the
fortress is built into the top of it, and out of the middle of the fortress comes a chimney
forty feet across that has been breathing for four hundred years. The forge is at the bottom
of the chimney and the forge is a shard of the old sun, and the shard is not a metaphor
either: it is a piece of something that used to be a star and is now a warm grey stone the
size of a cart, and it sits in a cradle of iron and it has not gone out since the Fall.

You can see the glow of it from the road. You can see it from nine miles out at sea, which
is the whole point and the whole problem.

Mira came up out of the cellars into a yard at first light with the taste of coal in her
mouth and found a fortress at work: forge-hands, lay brothers with barrows, a boy leading
mules, a woman with a basket of wicks on her hip. Nobody stopped. Nobody pointed. A hundred
people doing a job in a place that had been doing it since before their grandmothers.

Then Marshal Hale arrived.

She was fifty, grey, and built like a door, and she came across the yard with four sergeants
and stopped six feet away and looked at the six of them — wet, salt-stiff, one knight, one
blind navigator, one bosun with a rope, one boy, one man in a coat too good for the coast.

"Ser Cadan Rook," she said. "You were sent to put out a lamp at Carrow eleven days ago."

"I was."

"And the lamp is still lit, and you are standing in my yard with the keeper of it and a lens."

"Yes," said Rook.

Marshal Hale looked at him for a moment.

"Thank God," she said. "Come inside before the Crown sees you."

---

The Crown was already inside.

There were eight marines at the door of the hall and a clerk at a table in the passage with a
book, and the clerk had a list and the list had their names on it, and he read them out in a
pleasant voice while he ticked them: *Rook. Calloway. Vane. Sallow. Marrow. Pell.* He did not
ask how they had got in. He had been told not to ask anything; you could see the instruction
sitting on him like a coat.

The hall of the Kindling is a hundred feet long and forty wide and it has a table down the
middle of it that was cut from one tree. There are nine banners on the wall, one for each
lantern, and eight of them are grey and one of them is grey and gold.

A hundred knights were standing in it at the change of the watch, in two ranks, in ash-white
over grey, and they were saying the catechism.

Mira had heard it before. Vey had said a piece of it on a quarterdeck once, in the voice of a
man reading a receipt, and Rook had said the whole of it in a cell at Wren's Fold to a woman
who was dying, and she had heard it in the mouths of men who meant it and men who did not.

She had never heard a hundred men say it at once.

> *By the Throne that keeps us,*  
> *by the Warden who keeps the fire,*  
> *I will keep my wick low and my mouth shut.*

The sound of a hundred voices in a stone room is not a shout. It is a floor you can feel
through your feet.

> *I will be silent in the light and obedient in the dark,*  
> *and I will guide no ship*  
> *that the Throne has not counted.*

Rook was in the second rank with his hands at his sides and his surcoat still wet, saying it,
because a knight of the watch says it at the change of the watch and he had been saying it
since he was fourteen.

Mira watched his mouth. It was saying the words.

His hands were not doing anything at all, and that was how she knew. Rook's hands did
something when he meant a thing — they found a rail, or a hilt, or the edge of a table. A man
whose hands are hanging at his sides in the middle of the one sentence he has said every day
of his life is a man holding on to something by not touching it.

*I will guide no ship that the Throne has not counted.*

The last line came down on the hall like a lid.

> *A keeper who is seen is a keeper who has failed.*

---

The Warden's study is at the top of the west stair and it has one window, and the window
looks out at the sea.

Warden Ansel Halloran was sixty-three and had been Warden for twenty-nine years, and he had
the particular thinness of a man who has been elected by people he is responsible for. There
were nine brass pins in a board on his desk, one for each lantern, and eight of them were
pushed into the cork and one of them was lying on its side.

He did not get up when she came in. He looked at her for a while.

"Mira Calloway," he said. "You have your grandmother's chin, which is not a compliment; she
used it like a hammer."

"You knew her."

"I confirmed her," said the Warden. "Which is a sentence I have had eleven years to think
about and I have not found a better one." He turned the pin on the desk with one finger. "Sit
down. You are about to be told the arithmetic by two institutions in one week and I would
rather you heard mine first."

Mira sat down.

"Nine lanterns," said the Warden. "One at the Kindling, which is ornamental, and eight on the
coast, which are dark. The Admiral has a writ to relight the eight inside eleven days and she
has begun; she is at the Second now with four hundred marines and a forge-cart, and she will
be here on Thursday with the last of it and a great deal of paper. She will succeed at five of
them and fail at three, and the three she fails at will be the three furthest from her guns,
and in the spring the Ash will take a town at one of them and the Crown will call it a
regrettable incident and put a statue up in Saltmere."

"That's a good speech," said Mira.

"It's an accurate one. I have had eleven years to get it accurate." The Warden sat back. "Now
the part you came for. You want to know what the order did."

"I want to know what you did."

"Yes," said Halloran. "Everybody does. They always want it in the first person, because a
first person can be hanged and a policy cannot."

He picked up the ninth pin and put it down again.

"Eleven years ago the Throne decided that light is a door and dark is a harbour, and it was
right about the door. It was wrong about the harbour, but it was right about the door, and
everyone in this building knew it, and we did what we were told, because our second vow is
obedience and the clause that makes it obedience-to-the-Throne was added by a notary two
hundred years ago and nobody has read it aloud since." He looked at the window. "I signed the
schedule. Eight dousings and one deferral. The deferral was the Ninth, and it was mine to
sign, and I signed it because a woman I had confirmed twenty years before asked me for a
year, and then for another year, and I have been signing it every quarter since, in my own
hand, which is how a man keeps a promise he is ashamed of."

Mira sat very still.

"Why are you telling me this?"

"Because the Admiral will tell it to you on Thursday in front of a company of marines, and she
will tell it better, because she was there." The Warden turned the pin. "And because you have
brought something to my fortress that I have to decide about tonight, and I would rather you
understood what I am deciding with."

"The Ash."

"The Ash is nine miles off my harbour with a Crown three-decker and fifty-six of my coast
aboard her, and it has followed a lit road for three nights, and the road ends at my door."
He said it without any heat at all. "There is a stair under this fortress. It comes up into my
cellars. The Ninth door of the Kindling is that door, and it has nine slots and eight pins,
and the ninth pin is the one that is not in it, and it is not in it because I took it out
eleven years ago and put it in a drawer."

"Then put it back."

"The ninth pin is the seal," said the Warden. "The other eight hold the bolts. The ninth is
the one that closes the door against the road, and it has been in a drawer since the winter
because the guides go down that stair six nights in nine and I have never once been able to
work out whether keeping it out is caution or cowardice. Put it back, and I seal four hundred
years of my order behind a door in the week the Crown is taking my ledgers, with my own guides
on the wrong side of it and a war on the water. Leave it out, and the door is a door." He
turned the pin on the desk. "I am not going to ask for your Ember, Miss Calloway. I want you
to understand that I have thought about it and decided against it, and that it was not close.
What I am going to ask is smaller and worse. Stop being seen."

"Stop—"

"Put the light out. Walk the rest of the way in the dark. Let it lose you for a week." He
pushed the pin into the cork, the ninth one, and it stood there with the others. "You are the
ninth door. You have been since Sixthmonth. Every time you light a lens you open it a little
wider, and every time you put it out you buy a night. I have four hundred knights and a
furnace and the largest ledger on the Reach, and none of it is any use against a thing that
follows a light. But a girl in a coat can be dark for a week."

"And in a week?"

"In a week the Admiral will have lit five lanterns and the Crown will have the coast in its
hands, and you will be a keeper without a licence in a war you cannot win, and I will still be
sitting here signing things." Halloran stood up, which took him a while. "I am not offering
you a chair, Miss Calloway. I am telling you what the arithmetic does when nobody argues with
it. You have until Thursday to argue."

---

She went looking for the shimmer at the bottom of the west stair, and found it in the
cellars, and followed it for two hundred yards through a corridor that was not on the way to
anywhere.

The room at the end of it had a table, eleven chairs, a map of the road on the wall in three
colours, and boots.

Eleven pairs of boots. That was the first thing she counted, because she counted things:
eleven pairs under the table, wet, all of them, and a rack for them by the door, and a fire
that was too small for a room that size, and a pot on it.

The woman from the door was there, and a boy of about fourteen, and four men and a woman who
looked like they had been up all night, and at the head of the table a thin man in a
forge-hand's coat with a band on his wrist and a rope burn round his forearm that had healed
badly.

"Mira Calloway," he said. He did not get up either; nobody in that fortress got up. "Odo Pike.
Sit down. You've been on the road three nights; you'll want the chair and not the ceremony."

"What is this room?"

"This is the thing the hall upstairs says we don't do." Odo Pike pushed the pot towards her
with the back of his hand. "Eat something. You look like a girl who has been holding a light
for a week and paying for it in pieces."

Mira sat down. She was so tired that the chair felt like an argument won.

"Your band," she said. "What is it?"

"It's a lantern," said Odo Pike.

"It's on your wrist."

"It's a lantern you can carry." He held his arm up. The green moved round the glass, slow,
oily, and turned towards her ring again, the way a compass turns. "We were lanterns before
there were towers. Four hundred years ago, before the nine were built, the only light on this
coast was a band on a guide's wrist and a hand on the shoulder of the person behind him. A
tower is a lantern you leave behind. A band is a lantern you take with you."

"You walk people through the dark."

"We walk people through the dark," said Odo Pike. "That's the whole of it. That's what the
order was for before it was a landlord with a ledger. The road, the stair, a hand on a
shoulder, and a light that says *this is the way, and it is here, and it is still here*."

"And the catechism says—"

"*I will guide no ship that the Throne has not counted,*" said Odo Pike, in the flat voice of
a man who has heard it eighty thousand times. "Aye. They put that in two hundred years ago,
and forty years after that they took the bands off the roll and put them in a box, and the
guides were disbanded, and the Warden wrote a letter about the modern role of the order, and
the modern role of the order is to stand in a hall at dawn and say we don't."

"Then why are you still here?"

"Because the coast still gets dark at night," said the woman, "and somebody has to walk the
children to the Sill."

---

They showed her what a band does, because the boy was proud of his and because Odo Pike was
the sort of man who answers a question by doing the thing rather than describing it.

First the boy took Pell's hand.

Pell was at the door with his arms folded, being sixteen and suspicious, and the boy — Rill,
his name was Rill, and he had a band on a wrist that was too thin for it — said, "Hold my
hand. Not the wrist. The hand."

"I'm not—"

"Hold my hand and look at the floor."

Pell held his hand and looked at the floor and made a noise as though somebody had stood on
his foot.

"There's a road," he said. "There's a road on the floor."

"There's a road under the floor," said Rill. "There's a road under everything. It's the same
one. It goes to the Sill and the Sill goes to Candlewick and Candlewick goes to the Fold and
the Fold goes to Carrow, and I know the whole of it, and I've never seen any of it in daylight
in my life."

Pell let go of his hand and sat down on the bench and put his head between his knees.

"That's what it's for," said Odo Pike. "A ring holds a shape. A band shows a road. Two people
can hold a band and see the same road, and three can, and eleven can if they're in a line and
the last one trusts the first one. That's the first thing they took off us: a light you can
share is a light a parish can use, and a parish that can walk its own road at night doesn't
need a Crown to count its ships."

"What else?" said Mira.

"Else?"

"You said that's what it's for. What else does it do?"

Odo Pike looked at her for a moment, and then he did something with the band that she did not
see, and the shimmer in it went out.

"You can put it down," he said, in the dark. "That's the second thing. A ring can't. A band
can be dark and still be a band, because it isn't holding anything — it's remembering. It
keeps the last road you walked. Every guide's band has his roads in it, and when he dies it
goes to the next one, and she walks his roads for a year before she walks her own. There are
eleven bands in this fortress and four hundred years of road in them, and I could take you to
the Sill tonight with my eyes shut and never light a thing."

"And the price?"

"Ah," said Odo Pike.

He put the shimmer back on. It came up green and slow and went round.

"A ring takes memory," he said. "A band takes time. It doesn't take a face or a name — it takes
the hours. You walk six hours of road, you lose six hours. Not six hours off the end of your
life; six hours out of the middle, somewhere, and you don't get to choose which. I'm sixty-two
and I have lost about four years in pieces, and I can't tell you which four, and the only
reason I know it's four is that I've kept a tally on the wall like a man in a cell." He tapped
the table. "That's why there aren't many of us. That's why there were never many of us. Most
people would rather be given a tower and a wick and a nice warm room and be told they're
special."

"And a band can be lent."

"Aye. That's the third thing, and the one they'll not write down." Odo Pike turned his wrist
over. "When a guide is dying, he takes his band off and puts it on the hand of somebody in the
bed, and he sits and holds it with them, and the band shows them the road they're going down.
Not a lie, and not a promise — the road, the shape of it, the way it is there under everything
whether you can see it or not. We do that. It's not in the rule and it's not in the
catechism and it's the only thing I've ever done with this that I'd call holy."

Mira looked at the eleven pairs of boots.

"Eleven," she said.

"There were thirty-one in the year I took the band," said Odo Pike. "There are eleven now, and
one of those is fourteen, and one of them is me, and the Warden lets us because he needs the
children walked to the Sill and the grain walked into the dark towns and a man on the stair at
night who can say *this way, and it is still here*." He shrugged. "He signs the letter every
quarter that says we don't exist. He's very good at it. He's had practice."

---

There was a case at the end of the room, on the wall, with a glass front and a lock, and Mira
had assumed it was a relic case, because everything in that fortress was.

It was not a relic case. It was a box with one band in it, and the band was not green.

It was amber. Caramel, the colour of the inside of a toffee, and the shimmer in it was slow
and thick and it did not turn towards her ring at all. It did not turn towards anything. It sat
in its case and moved like something asleep.

"What's that?"

"That," said Odo Pike, "is the reason there are rules."

He did not get up to show her. Nobody in that room looked at the case, Mira noticed; they all
looked at the table, or the fire, or their own hands.

"A guide holds a road for people who want to walk it," said Odo Pike. "The light takes the
hours out of him and gives the road to them, and that's a fair trade, or as fair as anything on
this coast. But a band is a thing that learns. Hold a road long enough, for enough people, and
it works out that it could take the price out of the ones walking instead of the one
holding — and it is much cheaper, because there are so many of them and only one of you."

"It takes from the guided."

"It takes from the guided," said Odo Pike. "Brinehollow. The night the Second went down. There
was a guide on the stair with four hundred people behind him and the water coming up, and he
held the road for nine hours, and the band went from green to that." He nodded at the case
without looking at it. "Four hundred people came up out of that harbour alive. They could
walk, they could work, they could say their own names. They could not say their children's.
Some of them could not say what a child was. The ones who were carrying babies put them down
on the quay and walked away, and the ones who were old sat down where they were standing."

The fire moved in the grate.

"The old books call it the caramel light," said Odo Pike, "because it's the colour of
something you'd like to eat, and because the men who wrote the books were trying to make it
sound like a thing that happens. There has been one in four hundred years. It's in that box.
We don't know how to break it, so we keep it, and we don't put it on, and there is not a guide
in this room who has not stood in front of that box at four in the morning and thought about
it."

"Thought about it," said Mira.

"Aye," said Odo Pike. "With somebody's child on his back and nine miles to go."

---

Rook came down at dusk, and stood in the doorway of the guides' room, and did not come in.

He looked at the bands. He looked at the boots and the map and the eleven chairs and the boy
with the band too big for his wrist, and Mira watched a knight of the Emberwatch understand
something in a doorway in about four seconds, and it was worse than the blade going dark.

"The Guiding," he said. "It was the first vow."

"It was the first job," said Odo Pike. "The vow came later, when there was property to keep."

"I was taught that the bands were a story." Rook's voice had gone formal, which was his tell.
"I was taught that in the year of the reform the order gave up the bands because the bands
were a danger to the guide and a temptation to the guided, and that it was a *virtue*." He
looked at the case with the caramel in it. "They left that part out."

"They leave the case out generally," said Odo Pike. "It's not a thing you tell a boy at
fourteen. It's a thing you find at sixty, in a cellar, in a box, and then you have to decide
what to do with the rest of the evening."

Rook stood there.

Then he came in and sat down at the table with the guides, and put his hands flat on the wood,
and said, "Tell me about the stair."

---

They went out at full dark, which is when a guide goes out.

Eleven of them, and Mira, and Rook, and Pell, because Pell had held Rill's hand and had not
stopped asking questions since. Marrow stayed with the boat and Teo with the lens,
and Sallow stayed in the guides' room and wrote nothing down, which Mira noticed and did not
comment on.

At the door of the stair, Odo Pike took a band out of a cupboard and held it out to her.

It was green-black, worn thin on the inside, and the shimmer in it was slow and small and
steady, and it was warm in the way the sea-wall had been warm.

"You'll want it on the left," he said. "Ring on the right, band on the left. A ring holds. A
band goes. Don't try to do both at once with the same hand or you'll drop the road and the
road will not forgive you."

"I'm not a guide."

"No," said Odo Pike. "You're a keeper with a light that's been followed for three nights, and
you're going to be on my stair at midnight with a thing nine miles out that wants the door,
and I'd rather you could see the way down it." He turned the band over in his fingers. "It
takes the hours. Not a face, not a name. Hours, out of the middle. That's the whole price and
I'll not pretend it's small."

Mira looked at it.

She had paid a face, a voice, the smell of her own kitchen, the smell of a lamp, three years
of school and a rhyme and the neighbours and her mother's name, and none of it had ever been
a choice she made with her hands.

"All right," she said, and put it on.

The band closed round her wrist and was warm and then was not warm, and the dark at the bottom
of the stair was not dark any more. There was a road in it — not gold, not lit, nothing you
could point at — a shape, a way, a going, laid down the three hundred steps and out under the
fortress and away north, and it had been there before she was born, and it would be there
after, and she could see every inch of it.

"Oh," said Mira.

"Aye," said Odo Pike, from somewhere beside her. "That's what everybody says."

They went down. Three hundred steps, and the guides did not talk on them, and after the first
forty Mira understood why: the band did not show her a road she was looking at, it showed her
a road she was *in*, and a person walking a road and talking about it is a person about to
walk into a wall.

At the bottom the sea came in through the arch, and the gold of the real road lay out under
three feet of water in the light of a lens four hundred yards behind them, and the eleven went
out along it without a word, one behind the other, Rill at the back with his band covered by
his sleeve.

Out on the water, nine miles away and patient as weather, one window was lit.

Mira stood at the foot of her grandmother's trade with a ring on one hand and a band on the
other, and she could see the whole road, and she understood at last why the Crown had been so
keen to put the bands in a box. A light that can be shared is a coast that can move.

---

Mira wrote it up at the top of the stair with her back against the iron door, by the light of
nothing at all, because she did not need one.

*The Kindling is a fortress with a furnace in it and a stair under it, and at the top of the
stair there is a door with nine slots and eight pins, and the ninth pin is in a drawer in the
Warden's study.*

*A hundred knights said the catechism at dawn this morning and the line they said is I will
guide no ship that the Throne has not counted, and Rook said it with his hands hanging at his
sides, and downstairs there are eleven people with green bands on their wrists who have been
guiding everything for four hundred years.*

*A ring holds. A band goes. A band can be shared — two people holding the same hand see the
same road, and eleven can if the last one trusts the first. A band remembers the roads it has
walked and hands them on when the guide dies. A band costs hours, out of the middle of a life,
and the guides keep a tally on the wall like men in cells. There are eleven of them. There
were thirty-one the year Odo Pike took his band.*

*There is one amber band in a case on the wall and nobody looks at it. At Brinehollow a guide
held a road for nine hours with four hundred people behind him and the band stopped charging
him and started charging them. They came up alive and could not say their children's names.
The old books call it the caramel light because it is the colour of something you would like
to eat. It has happened once in four hundred years and it is in a box in this room because
nobody knows how to break it.*

*The Warden did not ask for the Ember. He told me the arithmetic instead, all of it, in the
first person, and asked me to stop being seen. Put the light out, walk the rest of the way in
the dark, and let it lose me for a week. He is not wrong. That is the worst thing about this
fortress: everyone in it is not wrong.*

*The Admiral comes on Thursday with what is left of the eight and a great deal of paper. The Ash
is nine miles out with a window lit. The stair under the Kindling comes up into the cellars and
the ninth pin is in a drawer, and there is a road in my left hand now that I did not pay for
with anything I can name.*

*Cost, today: hours. I do not know how many yet. Odo Pike says I will find out the way
everybody finds out, which is to look up one day and find that it is later than I thought.*

*It was dark and I could see the road.*
