# Chapter Fifteen

Candlewick had been a fishing town and was now a fishing town with better wine.

They came in on the jury rig at dusk with the *Mercy Bell* wallowing like a drunk being
helped home, and the harbour was full of small boats, and the light was not coming from
the keep.

The keep was still there, of course. It stood at the end of the mole with its tower and
its gallery and its great brass lens housing, and there was a sign over the door in
painted letters with a picture of a lamp on it: **THE FOURTH LAMP — ROOMS — HOT FOOD**.

Inside, the lens was hanging over the bar.

Mira stopped in the doorway with her mouth open.

It was the size and shape of a mill wheel, cut into a hundred rings of glass, hung on
three chains from the old lamp-room beam with candles set round the rim in little brass
cups. It threw a soft, stupid, yellow light over forty drinkers and a bar and a fire,
and it made the whole room look like a painting of a room.

"Eleven years," said Isolde, at her shoulder. "Candles in it. Candles *in* it."

There was a framed deed on the wall by the door, and Rook stopped in front of it the way
a man stops in front of his own name in a list of the dead. Mira read it upside down.

*By writ of the Salt Throne, the commandery of the Fourth Light is dissolved; its lands,
chattels and instruments are transferred to the Crown; the keeper is released from his
vows with the Throne's thanks.*

And underneath, in a smaller hand, four signatures. The third one was a Warden of the
Kindling, dead twelve years.

A man at the far end of the bar said, "What's he here for?"

"He's here because I need a knight," said Isolde, "and this coast has one."

"That's not what I asked."

"No," said Isolde, "it isn't." She picked up her hat from the bar and put it back on,
which was the end of that conversation and the beginning of every other one in the room.

Rook put his hand inside his coat and found, as he had found every day since he was fourteen,
that there was nothing in it. He took the hand out again and let it hang at his side.

"He's not drinking," said Isolde, to the room. "He's working."

"Ser Rook," said Isolde. "Table."

He came and sat down and did not say anything for a while, and then he said, "We signed
the keeps away one at a time, and we called it obedience, and I have never read one of
those deeds until tonight."

---

Orren Beck was a big slow man of fifty-five with flour on his sleeves and a way of
looking at people that made Mira feel she had been counted.

"Captain Sable," he said. "You've come about the lens."

"I've come about the lens."

"You offered me eighty salt for it in the spring."

"I'll offer you a hundred and twenty now."

"It's not for sale at a hundred and twenty." Beck wiped his hands and put them on the
bar. "It's not for sale at all. I've had a man from Saltmere offer me four hundred and a
letter of thanks from the Admiral. I told him I'd think about it." He picked up a glass
and started drying it, which Mira would learn was what he did instead of shouting. "I
think about it every night. It's the only thing in this town worth more than the boats."

"Then why not sell?"

"Because it's not mine to sell," said Beck. "It's the Fourth's. It was here before me
and it'll be here after me, and the only reason it's hanging over a bar with candles in
it is that the Crown couldn't think of a better insult." He put the glass down. "Now.
The captain I know. The knight I can guess at. Who's the girl?"

"The keeper of the Ninth," said Mira.

Beck looked at her for a long time.

"Say that again," he said, "and don't say it like a girl who's been told to."

"I'm the keeper of the Ninth," said Mira. "I lit Carrow in Sixthmonth. I've got the ring
and I've got an Ember in a bucket and a road to walk and no lens to walk it by."

"Show me your hand."

She put her hand on the bar. He looked at the ring for a long moment, and then he
straightened up and shouted, "Jory! Bring the good bottle and three glasses and then
leave the bar to the girl, she can manage it," and a young man of about twenty came out
of the back with a bottle and set it down and said, "Right you are, Da," and went to
serve.

Mira looked at Beck.

Beck looked at the bar.

"Don't," he said, quietly. "Whatever it is you're about to say. Don't say it in the
room."

---

They went out the back, into a yard with a wall and a water butt and the tower going up
into the dark above them.

"I was the keeper of the Fourth," said Beck. "Eleven years. My father was before me. I
had a wife and a boy and a ring on my hand, and the Crown came down on the eleventh of
Sixthmonth with a captain and a writ and a company of marines, and they put the light
out in front of the whole town. There was a speech. The speech was about safety." He
said the word as though it had a taste. "And while the speech was going on, my boy — he
was six — he was out on the mole with the other children, because everyone was out, and
he went in the water."

Mira's mouth was dry. "And you—"

"I held him up. I had the ring on and the lantern still had a glow in it and I put a
hand out over the mole and I held my son up out of the harbour for a count of forty." He
stopped. "And when they got him out, he was fine. Wet. Frightened. Fine."

"And the price."

"The light took him out of me," said Beck. "Not the boy. The boy's alive and he's in
there serving beer. It took the *boy* out of my head. His face, his name, the day he was
born, the way he said my name. He's been standing behind that bar since he was sixteen
and I call him 'lad', and I know — I *know*, in here —" he put a fist against his chest,
"— that he's mine, the way you know a word is a word. But when I look at him, there's a
man I employ."

Mira's hands were cold.

"My wife died the year after," said Beck. "She didn't stop being angry and it wore her
out. And I've been keeping the Fourth's lens over a bar with candles in it ever since,
because it's the only thing in this town that remembers what we were, and if I sell it to
the Admiral I'll have a purse and a letter and nothing at all." He looked up at the tower.
"You want it. Everyone wants it. So I'll tell you what I told the man from Saltmere:
show me it matters. Not that it's pretty, not that it's old. Show me the thing is worth
more than the price of it."

"How?"

"That's your problem," said Beck. "You're the keeper."

---

She thought about it for a long time, sitting on the water butt in the yard with the
tower above her, and then she went and found the others.

"Bring the bucket," she said.

"Mira—" said Isolde.

"Bring the bucket, and get everybody out of the taproom, and open the front doors."

---

Here is what she did, and Isolde told it afterwards in every port from Candlewick to
the Fall, getting it wrong every time.

The centre of the lens was a hole the size of a fist at the height of a tall man's chest,
and the Ember was the size of a fist, and those two facts had been waiting for each other
for four hundred years in a way that was no practical use to anybody.

She got up on the bar. Beck put a hand out and she took it without thinking, and he took
her weight the way a man takes a sack of flour, and then she was standing on the wood with
her head inside the lens and her shoulders in the light.

The brass cup had a cradle in it: three claws and a bed of old ash, and the ash had been
there since the last keeper of the Fourth put a wick in it and went home. She set the
Ember down in the cradle. It did not fit the way a thing fits when it was made for the
hole. It fitted the way a hand fits a glove that has been somebody else's.

Then she got down, and her hands were shaking, and she asked Beck to take the candles out
of the rim.

"All of them?"

"All of them."

He did it, one at a time, forty little brass cups, and the room got darker and darker
and the drinkers got quieter and quieter, and then Mira stood in the middle of the
taproom in the dark with her hand on a housing and the whole town's night outside the
open doors, and she lit it.

The lens took the light and threw it.

Not over the water — the tower had no height on it any more, the gallery had a roof over
it, and the light went out through the open doors and along the mole and up the street
and into the windows of every house in Candlewick, flat and gold and enormous, and out
across the harbour where the old stone quay still was under four feet of water.

The drowned road came up out of the sea.

It ran from the mole in a straight line along the old harbour wall, gold under the
water, all the way to the point where it turned inland, and the whole town came out into
the street to look at it.

They came out the way people come out for a fire — coats over nightclothes, a candle in
one hand and a child's wrist in the other — and then they stopped, all along the street,
and looked at the road.

An old woman in a shawl put her hand flat on the wall of her own house, the way Mira had
once put her hand flat on the glass of a chimney.

A man in a boat-builder's apron said, "That's the wall. That's where the crane was," and
said it again to nobody, and then stood with his hands in his pockets and cried without
making any noise about it.

Two children got down the quay steps and into the shallows and stood in the road with
the water at their knees, and their mother did not call them back.

And a man near the back of the crowd turned round and saw the knight standing in the open
doorway with the lamp's empty cup in his hands, and said, in a voice that carried further
than he meant it to: "That's a knight of the watch."

The street went quiet in a way that had nothing to do with the light.

Rook did not move. He stood in the door of a tavern with his order's small flame at his
collar and let four hundred people look at him.

It was the bravest thing she had ever seen anybody do with no weapon in their hands.

Then the boat-builder said, "Ser. Is it coming back?"

"It is coming back," said Rook.

"When?"

"When it has been paid for."

Mira held it.

She held it past the first minute, when her shoulder went. She held it past the second,
when the room started going thin at the edges and she could feel the weight of it in her
teeth. She held it for four minutes by the tavern clock, with the whole of Candlewick
standing in the light and a man behind her saying *oh, oh, oh* very quietly, over and
over, like a person being sick.

And when she let go, she sat down on the floor.

"Girl," said Beck.

"Give me a minute."

"Girl, look at me."

She looked up. He was kneeling down in front of her, this big slow man with flour on his
sleeves, and he was crying in the way that men of fifty-five cry, which is without any
noise at all.

"There's a road," he said. "There's still a road out there. I've been telling people
that road was there for eleven years and they've been saying it's a story, and there's a
boy in the back of my tavern who's never seen it, and —" He stopped. "What did it cost
you?"

Mira tried to think of a lie and found she did not have one in her.

"I don't know yet," she said.

She found out the next morning, when she came down for breakfast and the kitchen smell
came out of the back — bread and onions and hot fat — and she stood in the doorway of
the taproom and discovered that she could describe it and could not smell it.

Not gone like a thing gone faint. Gone like a word on the tip of her tongue, forever.

She sat down at the bar and got out the notebook and wrote it down, because that was what
she did now.

---

Beck gave them the lens.

He would not take money. He would not take a promise about money. What he took instead
was a page out of the notebook, and what was on it was a name.

"Jory Beck," Mira wrote. "Born the third of Ninthmonth. Son of Orren Beck, keeper of the
Fourth, and of Maret Beck, who had a temper. He went into the water the year the light
went out, at six years old, and his father held him up for a count of forty."

Beck read it three times.

Then he went to the bar and got a knife and cut a piece out of the varnish above the
bottle shelf, a space the size of a playing card, and he pasted the page in it, and he
stood back and looked at it.

"Every morning," said Mira, "before you open, I want you to read it out loud."

"That's a fool's errand."

"Everything I do is a fool's errand," said Mira, "and it's working so far."

Beck read it out loud. His voice did something on the second line and got it back.

Behind him, in the doorway to the back, Jory Beck stood with a tray in his hands and did
not say anything, and did not come in, and did not go away.

---

They carried the lens down to the ship in the dark, wrapped in sailcloth, with six men
and a handcart and the whole crew swearing in a whisper.

It took the frame and the chains and the brass and forty minutes, and it weighed as much
as a horse, and when it went into the *Mercy Bell*'s hold the ship went down three inches
and did not come back up.

"That's the difference between a hope and a cargo," said Isolde, satisfied.

"Do you know what you did tonight?" she said, when the crew were busy and there was nobody
at the rail but the two of them.

"I lit a lamp."

"You lit a lamp in a room with four hundred people in it, for four minutes, and every one
of them will be telling it to somebody by Sunday." The captain took her hat off, looked at
it, put it on. "That isn't a lamp in my hold. That's the only thing on this coast that
can't be bought and can't be broken, and I have been in this trade thirty years and I have
never carried anything I was afraid of."

Mira looked at the dark where Candlewick was.

"You'll be asked to do that again," said Isolde. "In front of more people. It will cost
more, and you'll do it, because you have the arithmetic of a keeper, which is: somebody
has to."

Rook stayed on the mole with the empty glass cup of the lantern in his hands — he had
taken it down himself, the way a knight takes down a thing that belongs to a broken
order — and he was quiet for a long time.

"Eleven years," he said. "I have walked past this town twice and never come in."

"You'll come in again," said Mira.

"With a lens to give back?"

"With a light in it."

Rook put the empty cup down on the mole very carefully, the way you put down something
that is heavier than it looks.

"Then I had better learn to be the sort of man who does that," he said, "and I had better
learn it quickly, because the woman with the white gloves is going to be at the Kindling
in nine days and I have read the deed on that wall."
