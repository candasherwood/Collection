# Chapter Four

Mira had spent her whole life being polite to people who had known her grandmother,
which is why it took her a moment to recognise the feeling that arrived with the
second stranger on the stairs. Being outnumbered is a room getting smaller while
you stay the same size.

The woman came up the last steps as though she had climbed them before, and perhaps
she had. She was in her forties, in a coat that had cost more than the tower's roof,
with a hat she did not remove and a knife in her belt that she was using, just now,
to spear a piece of apple.

"Carrow," she said, looking around the lamp room. "I hate this island. Nothing on it
is where you left it."

"Captain," said Teo.

"Navigator. You were meant to be back with the boat."

"I was meant to be back with a dark tower."

The woman took the chair. She sat the way people sit who have decided, a long time
ago, that chairs are for them. Then she looked at Mira, and smiled, and the smile
was not unkind, which was somehow the most alarming thing that had happened all
evening.

"Isolde Sable," she said. "That is Captain Isolde Sable, of the *Mercy Bell*, and
you are Ede Calloway's girl, which is a coincidence I fully intend to profit from."

Behind her, filling the doorway like a held-up coat, was a man built out of rope and
old weather. "Marrow," Isolde said, without turning. "Bosun. Don't mind him, he's
shy."

"I'm not shy," said Marrow. "I'm quiet."

He came in, looked at the lamp, looked at the log book open on the desk, and then
stood by the window with his hands behind his back and his boots planted, and did
not say another word for a quarter of an hour. Mira would learn that this was not
silence. It was a man counting something.

"How did you get up a hundred and twelve steps in the dark?" Mira said.

"Badly," said Isolde. "I climbed a hundred and nine. The last three I was carried,
and I would have been carried up all of them if the bosun's back were in better
order. It isn't, and he's sensitive."

"You're not even out of breath."

"I am extremely out of breath," said Isolde. "I'm simply not doing it anywhere you
can see."

From somewhere inside her coat Isolde produced half a loaf, a heel of cheese and a
second apple, and set them on the desk, one item at a time, in a line, like a person
dealing cards.

"When did you last eat?"

Mira did not answer, because the answer was *this morning* and it had been a very
long climb since.

"Eat," said Isolde. "I'm not going to negotiate with someone who's about to fall
over. It reflects badly on me."

Mira ate. She was ashamed of how fast, and she did not stop. The bread was warm. She
did not ask about that. On this coast you did not ask where a warm loaf had come
from in the middle of the night; you ate it and you said thank you and you did not
look at the crust too closely.

"Thank you, Isolde," she said.

"Captain."

"...Thank you, Captain."

"There," said Isolde, to the room at large. "Manners. In a tower. In the dark. I
shall write it down."

"Nobody's coming," Mira said.

She had not meant to say it. It came out of the place where she had been keeping it
since the moment the light went out over the water — the flat, patient, entirely
certain knowledge that she had lit the best light on the Reach and that Carrow would
watch it from below and go to bed.

Isolde stopped chewing. "Say that again."

"Somebody's lit the tower. In Carrow, that's not good news. It's a thing that
happened." Mira put the apple down. "They'll come up tomorrow, when it's light and
there's enough of them to make a party of it. They'll come to look. They won't come
to help. If the Crown's boat gets here first they'll stand at the bottom of the
cliff and be glad they weren't the ones who climbed."

"And your grandmother's neighbours. The ones who baked the bread you're eating
without asking where it came from."

"They'd bring me bread," said Mira. "They'd bring me bread and they'd stand at the
door of the tower with it, and they would not come in."

Isolde looked at her for a long moment, and then she did a thing Mira would come to
recognise: she took the useless answer, folded it, and put it away, and went on with
the business in front of her.

"Give me the log," she said.

Mira gave her the log.

Isolde turned it round, propped her elbows, and read it the way a woman reads a
thing she has read a thousand of. Date, wind, road, oil. Date, wind, road, oil. She
went back a page, forward two, found the place where the hand changed, and then she
found the place where the log stopped being a log.

She read the one sentence. Then she turned a page and read it again, and turned
another, and went on turning — twenty, thirty, fifty pages of the same four words in
the same hand — and her face did not move at all.

"Eleven years," she said.

"Five hundred and seventy weeks."

"Of *trimmed and filled*." Isolde shut the book and put one hand flat on the cover,
the way people do when they want a book to stay shut. "Girl. Your grandmother kept
this light until the night she didn't, and on that night she wrote *trimmed and
filled* and went down the stairs, and she never came up again, and nobody in this
parish ever came up to ask why."

Mira said nothing, because it was true, and because there was a part of it that was
worse than Isolde knew. The wick was trimmed. The oil was full. The sentence had
gone on being true after the woman who wrote it stopped coming.

"That's not a log," said Isolde. "That's a penance. Somebody's been keeping a
promise at you."

She got up and walked the room with her hands behind her back, the way a woman
prices a house she has no intention of buying.

"Who made this?"

"I don't know."

"You should know. Somebody's name is on it somewhere." Isolde put her face close to
the brass and turned it until the light caught a mark she could read. "There. A
maker's stamp out of Saltmere, struck twice, which means the man was proud of it." She straightened up. "There's a glazier in
Saltmere would give you six hundred salt for that lens and cut it into windows. He'd
be a fool. It's the best glass on the coast; it was the best glass on this coast
before your grandmother was born, and it will be the best glass on it when the tower
falls down."

"When the tower falls down?"

"Twenty years. The salt's in the mortar, not the stone. You can hear it when the
wind's from the east." Isolde came back to the desk and sat on the corner of it,
which was not a thing anyone had done in the lamp room before. "Do you know what a
light like that is worth?"

"It's a light."

"It's a toll," said Isolde. "Every hull that comes past this island in the dark
pays the tower for the road, one way or another — in salt, in water, in a barrel of
pitch for the wick, in a man on the mole with a boat to row you off if the wind
turns. Nine miles of road and a gate at the end of it. That's why the Crown took the
towers and left the towers standing. Not the heresy. Nobody hangs a keeper over a
prayer."

Mira looked at the lens, at the chains of glass in their brass frame, at the little
hole in the middle with the flame behind it.

"It's both," said Isolde, before she could say anything. "It's a light and it's a
toll, and that's the trouble with lights. The people who love them and the people
who bill for them are never the same people, and the second lot always turn up with
a writ."

She ate the last of the cheese. "So. Tomorrow. What's your plan for tomorrow?"

Mira opened her mouth and found that she did not have one. She had had a plan for
tonight, and it had taken her about a year to make, and it had worked, and she had
not thought one single step past the light coming on.

"I thought not," said Isolde. "Tonight's easy. Tonight's a match."

"Here is the shape of it," she said, "and then you can tell me what you're going to
do with it." "That ship you can see out
there with the bell — my ship — spent forty years on the bottom off this island. I
was on it. My crew was on it. And then one night the Brine Mother put her hand under
the keel and lifted us up and said: *you may keep sailing, and one day you will pay
me the light of a lantern, freely given.*"

The apple in Mira's mouth turned to a dry, sweet nothing.

"Eleven years," Isolde said. "I have been looking for a lit lantern for eleven
years. Do you know how many I found? None. And then tonight the sea went flat and
the bell rang by itself and my navigator — who is insufferable and occasionally
right — said: *somebody's lit the Ninth.*"

She leaned forward. "So. Freely given. You can hand it to me, girl. It doesn't have
to hurt, and you'd be choosing your own life over a lamp, which any reasonable
person on this coast would call sensible."

"No."

"You haven't thought about it."

"I don't have to. If I give it to you the road goes out. The ships go back to being
nothing. Carrow has no other light." Mira put the apple down. "It was lit for eleven
years before me. It was lit when the rest of them went dark. My grandmother climbed
those stairs every night of her life so that a road under the water stayed visible,
and the only reason it's dark now is that she stopped, and I don't know why, and I'm
going to find out."

"Where is she now?"

Mira looked at the desk.

Isolde did not ask twice. That was the first thing Mira liked about her, and she
noted it, and put it away with everything else she was going to need later.

"You'll find out," Isolde said, "from the bottom of the sea, when the Crown's knight
breaks that ring on your finger and the tower comes down on top of you."

Teo, by the window, said, "Captain."

"Don't."

"She doesn't know. Look at her. She doesn't know what the ring is."

Mira looked at her own hand. The green-black hoop was warm now.

Isolde sighed, and used the knife to point at her. "The Mother doesn't collect
lamps," she said. "She collects *light* — a keeper's light, given. There's a
difference. It's not the sort of difference you enjoy explaining to someone who's
eating your food."

"What difference?"

"One of them leaves you able to walk afterwards."

Mira looked at Teo. He was not looking at her. He was looking out of the window with
his jaw set, and he had one hand on the rail around the lens, which she would later
learn was what he did before saying something he meant.

"Captain," he said again, quietly. "There's a ship."

Isolde was up before he finished the word. All the laziness went out of her; she
crossed the lamp room in four steps and put her eye to the glass, and the light went
through her — through her coat, through her hand — and kept going out over the
water, and she did not flinch.

Out past the fleet, coming in from the east, was a ship that was not in the beam so
much as missing from it: a three-decker with a masthead of brass and no lantern
anywhere on her, a hole in the light the shape of a ship.

"*Vigil*," Isolde said. "The Admiral's own. That's a dousing party." She stepped back
from the glass. "That's a dousing party with a knight on it, and they've launched
already."

Through the window, at the foot of the cliff, a small boat was coming in on the
swell, eight oars going like a mill wheel, and one figure standing in the bow, not
holding on to anything.

"They'll take the light," Mira said.

"They'll try." Isolde picked up her hat from where she had not put it down. "They
don't hang keepers, girl, if that's what's worrying you. They break the ring. It's
tidier, and it doesn't leave a body, and afterwards the tower is just a tower and
everyone agrees it was very sad."

Mira heard herself say, "Help me keep it lit."

Isolde looked at her for a long moment, and something moved behind her face — not
softness; arithmetic.

"Passage," she said at last. "Passage, and my protection, and when this is over you
do me one favour, and I choose it, and you do it without asking what it costs."

"That's a terrible offer."

"It is," Isolde agreed. "You're learning."

Teo shifted his weight. "She'll do it," he said, to nobody in particular. "She's that
sort."

Marrow, at the window, said one word: "Eight."

"Eight what?" said Isolde.

"Eight in the boat. And a ninth standing up in it that isn't rowing." He turned
round. "And there's a light on the beach, Captain. Small. Somebody's lit a lantern at
the foot of the path."

Mira's stomach went cold. "That's the parish."

"That's the parish," Marrow agreed. "They've come down to watch."

From the foot of the tower, muffled by one hundred and twelve steps and a door that
had not been shut properly in eleven years, came a knock. Three knocks, evenly
spaced, patient as a landlord.

Then a voice, young, and formal, and carrying much better than it had any right to:
"By order of the Salt Throne, and of the Warden of the Kindling: douse this light."

Mira put her hand flat on the warm glass of the chimney, and did not move it.
