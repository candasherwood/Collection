# Chapter Ten

The middle watch was two hours long, and Mira had started to look forward to it,
which frightened her more than the Quiet had.

It was not the ship that frightened her. She had learned the ship: which plank spoke
under which foot, where the rope went when the wind came round, how the *Mercy Bell*
complained in the night like an old woman turning over. Two weeks of that and a body
starts to trust a floor.

It was that the watch was hers. Two hours, the deck, the bucket, and whoever had the
wheel. Nobody asked her to be useful. Nobody assessed her, or doused her, or wrote
her name in a book. She sat with her back against the mast and her hands round a
warm brass hoop and the whole black sea went past her, and for two hours she was the
person who was awake, and she liked it, and liking it was a thing that could be taken
away.

Tonight the wheel had Teo on it and the deck had Corbin Sallow on it, which was a
different kind of two hours.

"I don't sleep," Sallow said, from the hatch cover, in the tone of a man offering a
service. "Before you ask. It's not a choice and it's not a condition. It's more of a
policy. Sleep is where the drowning happens."

"You don't drown," said Mira.

"No," he agreed. "But I don't know that until I wake up, do I." He tipped his head
back and looked at the sky, which was doing its trick of having too many stars in the
wrong arrangement. "Two hundred and eleven years of theatre on this coast and nobody
ever wrote a good part for the drowned man. It's always 'rise, walk, groan, exit'. I
could do a *comedy*."

"You could be quiet," said Teo, from the wheel.

"I could be *quiet*," Sallow agreed, delighted. "That's good. That's a line. Write
that down, girl, that's a line."

Mira did not write it down. She had a notebook in her coat with four names on a page
and she was not going to spend ink on him.

Below, muffled by two decks and a hatch, Rook began to sing.

He did it every night at the turn of the watch, and he did it badly, and he had
explained on the first night that a knight of the watch was required to say the hours,
and that if he said them in silence on a ship full of sailors they would think he was
praying at them.

*"By the wick that is not mine,"* sang Ser Cadan Rook, off every note available,
*"by the fire I did not light —"*

"He's doing the long one," said Sallow, with enormous satisfaction. "Six verses. The
last one's about a door."

*"— I will keep the dark at distance, and I will not sleep tonight."*

*"— and the door that is not mine,"* sang Rook, taking the tune somewhere it had not
been before, *"— and the hour that is not mine, and the hand that is not mine —"*

A hatch cover lifted an inch in the fo'c'sle and went down again. Somebody laughed,
one syllable, and thought better of it.

"He's been doing it nine nights," said Pell, from the galley hatch, in the voice of a
boy who had been keeping score. "Two of the verses aren't in the catechism. I've
looked."

"They're in the long one," said Mira.

"They're not in *his* long one. He puts a bit on the end about being seen."

"Leave him his verses," said Marrow, from the rail, and that ended the discussion,
because on that ship Marrow ending a discussion was the whole of the law.

"That's not a hymn," said Mira.

"No," said Sallow. "It's a complaint with harmony."

---

The sea got up at the turn of the hour, the way it does when nobody has been
watching it, and by half past there was water coming over the bow in flat grey
handfuls and the ship was working.

Mira had been in worse. That was the thing she kept noticing: she had been in worse.
A tower in a gale at fifteen, with a lamp to trim. A cliff path in February. The fact
that she had a scale now, and that this weather was somewhere near the middle of it,
was a thing she could not say out loud without sounding like a boast.

"Pell's below," Teo said, nodding at the foredeck. "Getting the dry wick."

The foredeck store hatch was on the far side of the deckhouse. It had a cover of
oiled canvas over oak, and the pin that held it had been replaced twice since Carrow,
and Mira could hear it now — a flat wooden *bang* every time the bow came down, and
then a scrape, and then nothing.

Then, on the next sea, the cover lifted.

She saw it go up out of the corner of her eye: a black rectangle standing up in the
air at the far side of the deckhouse, and the dark under it, and the ladder going
down into it, and Pell's lantern somewhere at the bottom of the ladder.

"Pell!" Teo shouted.

The cover came down and the noise it made was not a bang. It was the sound a door
makes when it is trying to close on somebody's hand.

Mira's ring went cold.

She did not stand up. That was the strangest part, afterwards. She stayed sitting
with her back to the mast and her hands in her lap and she looked at the far side of
the deckhouse, at the corner of it, where she could not see the hatch at all.

She pictured the cover. Not the idea of the cover: the *thing*. Two inches of oak and
a skin of oiled canvas, the grain going the long way, a nick out of the leading edge
where a block had hit it in some other decade. The iron handle. The pin.

The light came off the bucket.

Not a rope this time. No line, nothing she could see. The light in the bucket simply
*gave*, the way a full bucket gives when you put your hand in it, and out of it came
a shape she could feel with her back teeth: a lid. A heavy, flat, patient weight that
closed over something out of her sight, three fathoms of deck away, and stayed.

She heard it hit. She felt it resist.

"Hold it," said Marrow, from somewhere. He had come up the ladder from the hold with
a hammer in his fist and he was already going. "Hold it, girl."

Mira held it.

The ship came down and the sea came over and she held it, and her shoulder went, then
her back, then her teeth, and the count started somewhere behind her eyes because
counting was what she did with the things she could not carry.

*One. Two. Three.*

The weight was not the weight of a hatch. It was the weight of every hatch she had
ever lifted, all at once, on the end of an arm that was not there. She heard herself
make a noise.

*Twelve. Thirteen.*

"There's a pin coming," Marrow said, from very far away. "Pell, get up the ladder.
Girl. Hold it."

*Forty. Forty-one.*

Something in her chest was going thin, like ice over deep water. She thought about
stopping, and the thought was so reasonable and so warm that she nearly did, and then
she thought about the sound the cover had made, and she got to fifty.

At seventy the deck went quiet. At ninety she felt the pin go in — felt it, through
the lid, the way you feel a key turn in a lock you are holding — and let go.

Then she was on her side on the deck with her cheek against wet wood and the world
doing a slow, dignified circle.

"Pell's up," said Marrow. "Wet. Angry."

Marrow stayed where he was for a moment with his hand flat on the cover, and then took
his knife out and cut the old pin free of its lashing and put the new one in himself,
and tested it twice, and then a third time, and looked at it the way a man looks at
something that has already failed him once.

"Twice now," he said. "That cover."

"What was the first time?"

"Nobody. It came up in a calm." Marrow folded his knife and put it away. "There's no
such thing as a calm on this water. There's only a Quiet with the light off."

"Put him to bed," said Isolde, from the quarterdeck, in a voice like a door closing.
Then, after a moment: "Girl. You all right?"

Mira said a word that her grandmother would not have liked. She heard herself say it
from a long way off.

Then Teo's hands were under her arms and he had her sitting up against the mast, and
the light in the bucket was burning exactly as it had been, as though nothing at all
had happened to it, which she found offensive.

"Ninety seconds," Teo said.

"I counted."

"You counted." He was not smiling. "That's thirty feet, through a deckhouse, blind.
You couldn't do that in the tower."

"I know."

He sat down next to her, hard, as if his legs had made a decision without him. "Say
the thing you're going to say," he said. "The one that makes it a joke, so I have to
stop being frightened."

Mira looked at the bucket. The light in it was leaning. It had never leaned before.
It leaned towards her, very slightly, the way a dog leans towards the hand that feeds
it, and she would have given a year of her life for it not to be doing that in front
of him.

"I'm getting stronger," she said.

"Yes."

"And it costs."

"Yes." Teo rubbed his face with both hands. "What did it cost?"

And that was when she noticed.

She was trying to find the voice. Not the things her grandmother had said — she had
those, she had written them down, page nineteen and page twenty and the note on the
matchbox. The *voice*. The particular cracked, warm, impatient sound of the woman
saying her name in a kitchen.

It was not gone the way a thing is gone from a memory, faded at the edges, coming
back if you stopped trying. It was gone the way a room is gone when the furniture has
been carried out: the shape of it still there, the door still opening onto it, and
nothing in it at all.

She sat on a wet deck in the middle watch and opened the door onto her grandmother's
voice and found a bare room.

"Her voice," Mira said. "I had her voice."

Teo did not say anything. That was the kindest thing he had ever done.

Mira put her hand flat on the deck. Then she said, out loud, to the sea and the mast
and the boy sitting next to her, because she needed to hear whether it was true:

"I am Mira Calloway, keeper of the Ninth, and I am not afraid."

It was the worst lie she had ever told. She said it anyway, and her voice did not
shake, which was somehow worse than shaking, and Teo took her hand and held it hard
enough to hurt.

"Don't do that again," he said.

"I can't promise that."

"I know." He looked at the bucket, at the light leaning towards her like a dog. "Then
promise me something smaller. Promise you'll tell me what it costs, every time, even
the ones that don't sound like anything."

"That's a strange thing to ask."

"It's the only thing I can check," said Teo. "You can lie about being all right. You
can't lie about a list."

---

Behind them, Sallow had not moved from the hatch cover.

He had watched the whole thing with his hands folded and no jokes in his face, and
when the light leaned he had gone still, the way a man goes still when he sees a
thing he recognises from somewhere worse.

"Girl," he said, when she was up. "How many times can you do that?"

"I don't know."

"Find out slowly," said the Understudy. "I'm serious. I have been dead four times and
I can't remember any of them, and I would give a great deal to have a list." He lay
back down on the hatch cover with his hands behind his head. "The worst part of not
dying isn't the dying. It's that you keep having to watch."

---

At the end of the watch, when the sky was going the colour of a knife and Nettle came
up to take the deck, Mira opened the notebook and found the page with the four names
on it, and turned it over, and wrote on the back:

*She had a voice. It went off like a kitchen and came back cross and it said my name
like it was a chore. I have spent it and I did not get to choose.*

Then she sat with the pencil in her hand for a while.

"Teo."

"Mm."

"Say my name."

He did not laugh at her, or ask why. "Mira Calloway."

"Again."

"Mira Calloway." He was quiet. Then, because he was Teo and because he had learned to
read her the way she read a page: "Do you want me to write it down?"

"Yes."

He took the pencil out of her hand, which she had not offered, and wrote underneath,
in his own bad hand:

*Mira Calloway. Keeper of the Ninth. She held a door for ninety seconds and it cost
her a voice she loved. She is not afraid.*

"Read it back," she said.

He read it back. She listened to every word of it, and to the sound of him saying her
name, and filed it where the other one used to be.
