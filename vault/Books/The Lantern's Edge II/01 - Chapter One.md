# Chapter One

The bill did not come.

She had stood at the glass on the first night waiting for it, and on the second, and then she
had stopped waiting and started watching, which is a slower trade and lasts longer. A year and
a half of six o'clock. The light went out over Carrow every evening and cost her nothing at
all, and she had written that in the notebook so often that the pencil had worn a grey groove
in the page.

Nine keeps, and she could say them the way Ede said names. The Kindling, where the fire was
kept small on purpose and a hundred knights swore at dawn that they guided nothing. The Second
at Brinehollow, under water to the second floor, with a clean room in it and no light and a
woman in it who was not allowed to be there. The Third at the Sill, lit. The Fourth at
Candlewick, forty candles over a bar and a page pasted above the bottle shelf. The Fifth at
Saltmere, lit, by a man who could not read what he had written and dictated it instead. The
Sixth at the Gull's Teeth, lit, by a keeper who had told them no and then written a letter that
Mira kept in the notebook and read when the year got long. The Seventh at Barrowfoot, lost,
with its bell under three feet of water and ringing with no wind when the Ash was close, and it
had not rung since the ninth night. The Eighth at Wren's Fold, empty, and swept twice a year
because she could not think of anything better to do with the broom. The Ninth at Carrow, lit,
and hers.

Four lit. It was not nine and she was not going to pretend it was nine, and the Throne had a
commission sitting in Saltmere for two years that was going to produce a report about the
difference.

Her grandmother was seventy-two and had given up the wall in the spring.

That was not a thing anybody had argued about. Ede Calloway had come down the sea-wall one
morning with her stick and her hip and said, "That's me done," and had gone in and had her
breakfast, and she had not gone back up, and the parish had gone quiet about it in the way a
parish does when it has decided to be tactful at somebody.

She kept the book instead. She sat in the kitchen with the door open and the book of names on
her knees and a pencil behind her ear, and she said them every morning at first light, and Mira
said them with her on the mornings she was not asleep at the top of a tower.

"Names are not a memory," Ede had told her once, when Mira had asked why she still said them
when the name was all that was left of her daughter and the face had gone with the seasons.
"Names are a fence. You put them round a thing so you know when it has got out."

---

Teo wrote the weather on the gallery.

He had a board and a pencil and a rag, and he did it at four, and at eight, and at the turn of
the light, and the board was ruled into columns in his own hand, which was better than it had
been and still bad. Wind, sea, cloud, the hour the gulls came in. He could not see the road any
more. He could see the board because he held it six inches from his face, and he could see the
size of the sea because he had spent his life on it and could hear the difference between a
swell with a mile of water behind it and a swell with a hundred feet.

"North-west four," he said, the night the bill did not come. "Sea's got up. There's a boat out
past the point with its oars wrong."

"There's no boat out past the point."

"There's a boat," said Teo, "with its oars wrong."

She went and looked. There was no boat. She came back and sat down on the gallery boards beside
him with her back against the rail, and he did not say anything about being wrong, because he
had been wrong about a boat about once a month for a year and a half and they had an
arrangement about it.

"Say the thing," he said.

"You say the thing."

"You said it last time and you got the second line wrong, and I am not letting you get it wrong
twice." He put the pencil behind his ear. "You are my left, I am your right."

"You hold the dark, I hold the light," she said.

"If one of us goes out tonight —"

" — the other one keeps walking," said Mira, and got it right, and did not feel anything about
getting it right, which was the point of it.

"Again tomorrow," said Teo.

"Again tomorrow."

Below them, in the yard, Rook came round the corner of the boathouse with a bucket in each
hand.

He had carried the water every night for a year and a half, which was a hundred and four steps
each way and six of them wide, and he had counted them out loud on the first night and had
never counted them again. His surcoat was in a chest. The Warden had confirmed him twice and
told him he would not wear it until he could say the three questions and mean them, and he had
tried on the anniversary and got as far as the second and stopped, and had gone back to the
well.

He had Ede's ring on his right thumb, worn thin on the inside and dead as a stone, and the iron
thumb-ring in his left hand, and he carried both the way a man carries an argument he has not
finished.

"Ser," said Mira.

"Keeper."

"Do you want to hear the count?"

"No," said Rook, and put the buckets down. "I want to hear it when it is a count. Pell's got a
book with four hundred and eleven names in it and a hundred and three people in this square and
the two have never once agreed, and I have stopped asking him which is wrong."

---

The square at Carrow had a well in it and a wall round it and a hundred and three people in the
morning.

They were the ones with no house left. Barrowfoot was a hole in the ground and the Hollow Bell
was under water and the villages under the Sill had been eaten in the first year of the dark,
and when the coast had been walked home there had been a hundred and eleven of them left over
with nowhere to be put, and Carrow had the empty houses, and Carrow had taken them. They
worked. They were courteous. A man would stand aside for you on the steps and take his hat off,
and a woman would hang her washing in a straight line, and if you said good morning to any of
them they would say good morning back in a pleasant voice that had nothing behind it at all.

Nell Cusk was hanging washing.

She had come up out of the Hollow Bell with the rest of them, and she had been given the house
at the top of the green with the bad step, and she had been in it for a year and a half, and
she did the same washing every Thursday and hung it the same way: shirts, then the small
things, then the sheets, and the sheets always last and always by the corners.

Mira heard the humming from the slip.

She had been carrying the day's oil up from the store and she stopped in the road with the can
in her hand, and the humming went on, four lines, over and over, in a voice with no breath in
it.

*Sleep, and the light will keep the rest.*

She had heard it once, in a column of eight hundred people on the road in the dark, on the
second night of the walk home, and Pell had gone along the row with a lamp and looked at every
face and come back with his hands shaking, and Mira had written it in the notebook and
underlined the row and the hour. *Somebody in there is still somebody. Do not go and get them
out. Sit up and listen.*

Pell had sat up every night since. A year and a half of nights, and a book of rows, and in a
year and a half it had sung four times: at the Sill in the spring, twice at Candlewick in the
summer in two different rows, and once at Carrow in the first winter, in the third row from the
wall.

Never twice in the same place. That had been the rule they had made, because it was the only
rule that gave them anything to measure.

Nell Cusk was standing at her line with a wet shirt in her hands, in the fourth row from the
wall, and she was humming.

Mira put the oil down.

She crossed the road slowly and stood at the bottom of the green and did not go up, because the
last three times anybody had spoken to one of them in the middle of it, the humming had stopped
and had not come back for a year, and Pell had a page about that too.

*Sleep, and the dark will do its best.*

The shirt went up. The pegs were wooden and old and one of them was a different colour from the
others, which was the sort of thing Mira noticed now and could not stop noticing.

*I have your hand. I have your name.*

Mira stood in the road with her hands at her sides and counted, because that was the other
thing she could not stop doing.

*The water takes us both the same.*

Then Nell Cusk put her hand in the basket and took out a sheet, and shook it out, and said, in
her own voice, in the flat pleasant voice that a hundred and three people in that square used
for everything:

"*Count the ones that came to land.*"

Mira did not move.

The sheet went up by the corners. The pegs went in. Nell Cusk smoothed it down with the flat of
her hand, the way you do, and stood back and looked at the line, and then she said, to the
sheet, or to the wall, or to nobody:

"Twelve."

---

Pell's book was on the table in the boathouse with a stone on it, and it had two hundred and
eleven lines and four hundred and eleven names and an inventory of the dead that would have
done credit to a treasury.

He was seventeen and he had grown four inches in a year and he still could not be told anything
without going white about the mouth first, and he had the book open before Mira had finished
the sentence.

"Fourth row," he said. "From the wall. Nell Cusk, house at the top of the green, came up out of
the Hollow Bell in the ninth month, one of the hundred and eleven."

"Was she one of the eleven at the turn?"

"She was one of the nine at four." He turned the page back. "Sallow counted the eleven, and the
Receiver said he could, and I wrote them down from him: eleven names, and one of them was
Konrad Voss, and the Admiral watched him go."

"Eleven," said Mira. "Not twelve."

"Eleven," said Pell. "I've counted that page four hundred times." He looked up. "What did she
say?"

"She said *twelve*."

The boathouse was quiet. Out on the water somebody was rowing badly, and a gull was doing what
gulls do.

"Miss," said Pell. "She might have meant something else. They say things. They say the hour and
then they say it wrong. There's a man in the second row who tells the time all day and half the
time it's the wrong time."

"She said *twelve came to land*," said Mira. "She said it in the song. The song's got five
lines now and it had four, and I have the four written down in this notebook in the hand I had
a year and a half ago, and the fifth one is not mine and it is not hers."

Pell looked at the page for a while.

"Then somebody's been writing," he said.

---

They took it to the kitchen at dusk, because that was where Ede was, and Ede listened with her
hands flat on the table and her eyes shut, and when Mira had finished she opened them and said,
"Don't."

"Grandmother —"

"I know what it is. I held a ring round this island for eleven years and I know what that song
is, and I know what the fifth line is, and the answer is don't." Ede turned her cup round by
the handle. "There's a road to the dead. It was never made. Every generation of guides has the
same rule about it and the rule is one word long and it is not a joke and it is not
superstition, it's a price list. The white band has no prices on it. Everything else on this
coast costs a season or an hour or a name and you know what you're paying because somebody
wrote it down. That one doesn't say. That's what's wrong with it."

"Nobody's talking about a white band," said Mira.

"You are," said Ede. "You've been talking about it since you were fourteen and you didn't know
the word for it. It's in your face. It's the face you make when a door's shut."

Rook stood in the doorway with his hands at his sides.

"Ma'am," he said. "There is a count that does not close. Eleven went over at the turn and a
woman in this square has said twelve, and she said it in a song with a line in it that nobody
wrote." He said it without any heat, the way he said everything now. "I am not asking about a
road. I am asking what we do with a number."

Ede looked at him for a long time.

"You write it down," she said at last. "You write it down and you put the date on it and you go
on saying the names in the morning, and if it is twelve then one of them is walking about this
island with a body on, and you will find out which one the way you find out everything, which
is by counting."

---

They counted at dusk, in the square, with the light going round above them.

Mira did it from the top of the steps with Pell on one side and Rook on the other, and the
parish came out for it because the parish came out for everything now, and the hundred and
three stood in their rows with their faces to the water and did not mind being counted, and the
four hundred did not stand in rows because they had been told they did not have to.

It took eleven minutes. It always took eleven minutes.

"Ninety-eight," said Pell. "Ninety-nine. There's a child on the wall. Miss — there's a child up
on the wall."

"Count her."

"Ninety-nine, one hundred, one hundred and one —" He stopped. "One hundred and four."

Mira looked down at the book in her hands. One hundred and three names, and a line through two
of them from the winter, and the total at the bottom in Pell's hand with the date beside it.

"Again," she said.

They did it again. It took eleven minutes again and it came out the same, and the parish
waited, and a woman near the back said, "Is it short?" and somebody else said, "It's over," and
the two of them argued about it in the way of people who have been frightened for a year and a
half and have got used to being frightened in company.

The extra one was standing at the back by the sea-wall gate.

She was about thirty, in a good grey coat, and her hair was pinned and her boots were dry, and
she had a satchel over her shoulder and ink on the fingers of her right hand. She was not in a
row. She was standing where somebody stands who has come to a thing and does not know yet
whether they are in it.

Mira went down the steps with the book under her arm.

"You're not on my list," she said.

"No," said the woman. She had a coast accent and it was not quite right, the way a thing is not
quite right when it has been learned from a book. "I'd not be. I came in this afternoon."

"There's no boat came in this afternoon."

"There's no boat," the woman agreed, and looked at the light going round, and then at the
tower, and then at Mira, with an attention that was not unfriendly and was not ordinary either.
"You're the keeper."

"I'm the keeper."

"Then you'll have the date." She took the pencil from behind her ear — it was Mira's own trick,
and seeing it done by a stranger on that slip was worse than the humming had been — and opened
the satchel on a clean page. "I've been three days on the road and I've lost the count of them.
What's the date?"
